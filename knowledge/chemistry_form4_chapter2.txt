CHAPTER OVERVIEW

This chapter introduces the microscopic structure of matter. Although matter can be observed in everyday life, its particles are too small to be seen directly using the naked eye.

All matter consists of tiny particles. These particles may be atoms, molecules or ions.

Students will learn:
1. The meaning and characteristics of matter.
2. The kinetic theory of matter.
3. The properties of solids, liquids and gases.
4. Changes of state of matter.
5. The development of atomic models.
6. The three main subatomic particles.
7. Proton number and nucleon number.
8. Electron arrangements.
9. Isotopes and their applications.
10. Calculations involving relative atomic mass based on isotopic abundance.

The knowledge in this chapter is important for understanding chemical formulae, chemical equations, the Periodic Table and chemical bonding.

2.1 BASIC CONCEPTS OF MATTER
------------------------------------------------------------

2.1.1 DEFINITION OF MATTER

Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space.

Examples of matter:
- Water
- Air
- Wood
- Iron
- Oxygen
- Carbon dioxide
- Sugar
- Salt

Matter exists in different physical states.

The three main states of matter are:
1. Solid
2. Liquid
3. Gas

Important concepts:

Mass:
The amount of matter contained in an object.

Volume:
The space occupied by matter.

Matter must have both mass and volume.

Example:
Air is matter even though it cannot normally be seen. Air has mass and occupies space.

Light and sound are not classified as matter because they do not have mass and occupy space in the same way as material substances.

2.1.2 PARTICLES IN MATTER

Matter consists of extremely small particles.

Depending on the substance, its particles may be:
- Atoms
- Molecules
- Ions

Atom:
The smallest particle of an element that retains the chemical properties of that element.

Examples:
- Helium atom, He
- Sodium atom, Na
- Carbon atom, C

Molecule:
A group of two or more atoms chemically bonded together.

Examples:
- Oxygen molecule, O2
- Hydrogen molecule, H2
- Water molecule, H2O
- Carbon dioxide molecule, CO2

Ion:
An electrically charged particle formed when an atom or group of atoms loses or gains electrons.

Examples:
- Sodium ion, Na+
- Chloride ion, Cl-
- Magnesium ion, Mg2+
- Sulphate ion, SO4^2-

Important distinction:

Atom:
Usually electrically neutral.

Molecule:
A group of chemically bonded atoms. A molecule can be neutral overall.

Ion:
Has an electrical charge because the number of electrons is different from the number of protons.

2.1.3 KINETIC THEORY OF MATTER

The kinetic theory of matter explains the behaviour of particles in matter.

Main ideas:

1. Matter consists of tiny particles.
2. The particles are in constant motion.
3. The particles possess kinetic energy.
4. The particles have spaces between them.
5. There are attractive forces between particles.
6. The movement and arrangement of particles differ between solids, liquids and gases.

Kinetic energy:
Energy possessed by a particle due to its motion.

The higher the temperature of a substance, the higher the average kinetic energy of its particles.

When temperature increases:
- Particles gain kinetic energy.
- Particles move faster on average.
- The average separation between particles may increase during expansion.
- The substance may eventually change state if enough energy is supplied.

Temperature is related to the average kinetic energy of particles, not the total kinetic energy of all particles in a sample.

2.1.4 THE THREE STATES OF MATTER

A. SOLID

Characteristics:
- Has a fixed shape.
- Has a fixed volume.
- Particles are packed closely together.
- Particles are arranged in an orderly arrangement in crystalline solids.
- Strong attractive forces hold particles in position.
- Particles vibrate about fixed positions.
- Solids are generally difficult to compress.
- Solids do not flow.

Explanation:

The particles in a solid are closely packed. They cannot move freely from one position to another. They only vibrate around their fixed positions.

This explains why solids maintain their shape and volume.

Examples:
- Ice
- Copper
- Iron
- Salt crystals
- Wood

B. LIQUID

Characteristics:
- Has a fixed volume.
- Does not have a fixed shape.
- Takes the shape of its container.
- Particles are close together.
- Particles are arranged randomly.
- Attractive forces are weaker than those in a typical solid.
- Particles can slide past one another.
- Liquids can flow.
- Liquids are difficult to compress.

Explanation:

Liquid particles have enough kinetic energy to move past one another while remaining relatively close together.

This allows liquids to flow and take the shape of their containers.

Examples:
- Water
- Ethanol
- Cooking oil
- Mercury

C. GAS

Characteristics:
- Has no fixed shape.
- Has no fixed volume.
- Expands to fill its container.
- Particles are far apart.
- Particles move rapidly and randomly.
- Attractive forces between particles are very weak under ordinary conditions.
- Gases can be compressed easily.
- Gases have low density compared with most solids and liquids.

Explanation:

Gas particles are separated by large spaces. They move freely in random directions and collide with one another and the walls of their container.

This explains why gases expand to fill the available space.

Examples:
- Oxygen
- Nitrogen
- Carbon dioxide
- Helium
- Water vapour

COMPARISON OF THE THREE STATES

Solid:
- Shape: Fixed
- Volume: Fixed
- Particle arrangement: Closely packed and generally orderly
- Particle movement: Vibrate at fixed positions
- Attractive forces: Strong
- Compressibility: Very low

Liquid:
- Shape: Not fixed
- Volume: Fixed
- Particle arrangement: Close together but randomly arranged
- Particle movement: Slide past one another
- Attractive forces: Moderate
- Compressibility: Very low

Gas:
- Shape: Not fixed
- Volume: Not fixed
- Particle arrangement: Far apart and random
- Particle movement: Move freely and rapidly
- Attractive forces: Very weak
- Compressibility: High

2.1.5 CHANGES OF STATE OF MATTER

Matter can change from one physical state to another when heat energy is absorbed or released.

The main changes of state are:

1. Melting
2. Freezing
3. Boiling
4. Condensation
5. Sublimation
6. Deposition

A. MELTING

Melting is the process in which a solid changes into a liquid when heat energy is absorbed.

Example:
Ice changes into liquid water.

Explanation:
- Heat energy is absorbed.
- Particles gain energy.
- Particles vibrate more vigorously.
- At the melting point, particles overcome some of the attractive forces holding them in fixed positions.
- Particles can move past one another.
- The solid becomes a liquid.

B. FREEZING

Freezing is the process in which a liquid changes into a solid when heat energy is released.

Example:
Liquid water changes into ice.

Explanation:
- Heat energy is released to the surroundings.
- Particles lose kinetic energy.
- Their movement becomes slower.
- Attractive forces hold particles in fixed positions.
- The liquid becomes a solid.

C. BOILING

Boiling is the process in which a liquid changes into a gas throughout the liquid at its boiling point.

Example:
Water changes into steam when it boils.

Explanation:
- Heat energy is absorbed.
- Particles gain kinetic energy.
- At the boiling point, particles have enough energy to overcome the attractive forces holding them in the liquid.
- Gas bubbles form throughout the liquid.
- The liquid changes into gas.

D. EVAPORATION

Evaporation is the process in which particles escape from the surface of a liquid to form gas.

Evaporation:
- Occurs at the surface of a liquid.
- Can occur at temperatures below the boiling point.
- Does not require the entire liquid to reach its boiling point.

Factors affecting the rate of evaporation:
1. Temperature
2. Surface area
3. Air movement
4. Humidity

Higher temperature:
Particles have higher average kinetic energy, so more particles can escape from the surface.

Larger surface area:
More particles are exposed at the surface, increasing the rate of evaporation.

Greater air movement:
Removes water vapour near the surface and can increase evaporation.

Higher humidity:
Reduces the rate of evaporation because the surrounding air already contains more water vapour.

E. CONDENSATION

Condensation is the process in which a gas changes into a liquid when heat energy is released.

Example:
Water vapour forms liquid water droplets on a cold surface.

Explanation:
- Gas particles lose kinetic energy.
- Their movement becomes slower.
- Particles move closer together.
- Attractive forces become more significant.
- A liquid is formed.

F. SUBLIMATION

Sublimation is the direct change of a solid into a gas without passing through the liquid state.

Examples:
- Dry ice, solid carbon dioxide, changes directly into carbon dioxide gas.
- Iodine can sublime when heated under suitable conditions.

G. DEPOSITION

Deposition is the direct change of a gas into a solid without passing through the liquid state.

Example:
Formation of frost from water vapour under suitable conditions.

IMPORTANT COMPARISON

Melting:
Solid -> Liquid
Heat absorbed.

Freezing:
Liquid -> Solid
Heat released.

Boiling:
Liquid -> Gas
Heat absorbed.

Condensation:
Gas -> Liquid
Heat released.

Sublimation:
Solid -> Gas
Heat absorbed.

Deposition:
Gas -> Solid
Heat released.

2.1.6 EFFECT OF HEAT ON PARTICLE MOVEMENT

When a substance is heated:
- Its particles absorb energy.
- Their average kinetic energy increases.
- Their movement becomes faster.
- The substance may expand.
- If sufficient energy is supplied, a change of state may occur.

When a substance is cooled:
- Its particles release energy.
- Their average kinetic energy decreases.
- Their movement becomes slower.
- The substance may contract.
- It may change into another state.

Important:
During a change of state at a fixed pressure, the temperature of a pure substance remains constant while the change occurs.

This is because the energy supplied or released is involved in overcoming or forming attractive forces between particles rather than increasing or decreasing their average kinetic energy.

2.1.7 HEATING CURVE AND COOLING CURVE

A heating curve shows how the temperature of a substance changes as heat is supplied.

For a pure substance initially in the solid state, the typical heating curve contains five stages:

Stage A:
Solid is heated.

- Particles gain kinetic energy.
- Particles vibrate more vigorously.
- Temperature increases.

Stage B:
Melting occurs.

- Solid and liquid coexist.
- Temperature remains constant at the melting point.
- Energy is absorbed to overcome attractive forces between particles.

Stage C:
Liquid is heated.

- Liquid particles gain kinetic energy.
- Particles move faster.
- Temperature increases.

Stage D:
Boiling occurs.

- Liquid and gas coexist.
- Temperature remains constant at the boiling point.
- Energy is absorbed to overcome attractive forces between particles.

Stage E:
Gas is heated.

- Gas particles gain kinetic energy.
- Particles move faster.
- Temperature increases.

COOLING CURVE

A cooling curve shows how temperature changes when a substance loses heat.

During freezing:
- Liquid changes into solid.
- Temperature remains constant at the freezing point while freezing occurs.
- Energy is released as attractive forces form or become more effective.

During condensation:
- Gas changes into liquid.
- Temperature remains constant at the condensation point while condensation occurs.
- Energy is released.

IMPORTANT EXAM CONCEPT:

A horizontal section of a heating or cooling curve indicates a change of state at constant temperature for a pure substance under constant pressure.

A sloping section indicates a change in temperature within one physical state.

2.1.8 MELTING POINT AND BOILING POINT

Melting point:
The temperature at which a solid changes into a liquid at a specified pressure.

Boiling point:
The temperature at which the saturated vapour pressure of a liquid equals the external pressure, allowing boiling to occur throughout the liquid.

For a pure substance at a fixed pressure:
- Melting occurs at a constant temperature.
- Boiling occurs at a constant temperature.

Impurities can affect melting and boiling behaviour.

Generally:
- Impurities lower the melting point of a pure solid.
- Impurities can cause the boiling point of a liquid to increase, depending on the substance and conditions.

A pure substance normally has a sharp melting point, while an impure substance often melts over a temperature range.

2.1.9 DETERMINING THE MELTING POINT AND BOILING POINT OF NAPHTHALENE

An activity involving naphthalene can be used to study changes of state and the effect of heating on temperature.

Naphthalene is a molecular substance that is solid at room temperature.

General experimental idea:

1. A sample of naphthalene is heated using an appropriate laboratory setup.
2. Its temperature is measured at regular time intervals.
3. The temperature readings are recorded.
4. A temperature-time graph is plotted.
5. The graph is examined to identify the melting region and boiling region, where relevant to the experimental procedure.

Expected observations:
- Before melting, temperature increases.
- During melting, the temperature remains approximately constant for a pure sample under controlled conditions.
- After melting, the liquid temperature increases when heating continues.
- During boiling, temperature remains approximately constant under fixed pressure.

Scientific explanation:

At the melting point, energy supplied is used to overcome attractive forces between particles rather than increasing average kinetic energy.

Therefore, the temperature remains constant during the change of state.

EXPERIMENTAL VARIABLES

Manipulated variable:
Time of heating or another variable specified in the experimental design.

Responding variable:
Temperature of the naphthalene.

Fixed variables:
- Mass of naphthalene.
- Heating conditions.
- Apparatus arrangement.
- Pressure.
- Temperature measurement method.

Safety:
Heating naphthalene and handling heated apparatus must only be done under teacher supervision using an approved laboratory procedure. Avoid inhaling vapours and follow laboratory safety instructions.

Do not assume that an experimental graph or measured value must match an ideal textbook graph exactly. Experimental readings can be affected by heat loss, thermometer response and other practical limitations.

2.2 THE DEVELOPMENT OF THE ATOMIC MODEL
------------------------------------------------------------

2.2.1 INTRODUCTION TO ATOMS

An atom is the smallest particle of an element that retains the chemical properties of that element.

Atoms are extremely small and consist of subatomic particles.

The three main subatomic particles are:
1. Proton
2. Neutron
3. Electron

Protons and neutrons are found in the nucleus.

Electrons occupy regions around the nucleus called electron shells or energy levels in the simplified atomic model used at this level.

2.2.2 DALTON'S ATOMIC MODEL

Scientist:
John Dalton.

Historical contribution:
Dalton proposed an early scientific atomic theory.

Main ideas:
- Matter consists of tiny particles called atoms.
- Atoms of the same element were proposed to be identical in mass and properties.
- Atoms of different elements were proposed to have different masses and properties.
- Atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios to form compounds.
- Chemical reactions involve rearrangement of atoms.
- Atoms are not created or destroyed in ordinary chemical reactions.

Model:
An atom was represented as a tiny, solid, indivisible sphere.

Limitation:
Dalton's model could not explain the existence of subatomic particles because atoms are actually made up of protons, neutrons and electrons.

Modern correction:
Atoms of the same element can have different numbers of neutrons. These atoms are called isotopes.

2.2.3 THOMSON'S ATOMIC MODEL

Scientist:
J. J. Thomson.

Discovery:
The electron.

Thomson's experiments involving cathode rays provided evidence for negatively charged particles within atoms.

Main ideas of Thomson's model:
- An atom is a positively charged sphere.
- Negatively charged electrons are embedded throughout the positive sphere.
- The total positive charge balances the total negative charge.
- The atom is electrically neutral.

Model name:
Plum pudding model.

Explanation:
The positive charge was imagined to be spread throughout the atom, with electrons embedded inside it.

Limitation:
Thomson's model could not explain the results of Rutherford's alpha-particle scattering experiment.

2.2.4 RUTHERFORD'S ATOMIC MODEL

Scientist:
Ernest Rutherford.

Experiment:
Alpha-particle scattering experiment using a thin gold foil.

Main observations:
1. Most alpha particles passed straight through the gold foil.
2. Some alpha particles were deflected.
3. A very small number were deflected through large angles or bounced back.

Conclusions:

Observation 1:
Most alpha particles passed straight through.

Conclusion:
Most of the atom is empty space.

Observation 2:
Some alpha particles were deflected.

Conclusion:
The atom contains a positively charged region that repels positively charged alpha particles.

Observation 3:
A very small number experienced large deflections.

Conclusion:
The positive charge and most of the mass of an atom are concentrated in a very small, dense nucleus.

Main features of Rutherford's atomic model:
- An atom contains a small, dense, positively charged nucleus.
- Most of the atom is empty space.
- Electrons move around the nucleus.
- Most of the mass of an atom is concentrated in the nucleus.

Limitation:
Rutherford's model did not adequately explain the arrangement of electrons in specific energy levels or the stability of atoms.

2.2.5 BOHR'S ATOMIC MODEL

Scientist:
Niels Bohr.

Main ideas:
- Electrons occupy specific energy levels or shells around the nucleus.
- Electrons in an allowed energy level have a specific energy.
- Electrons can absorb energy and move to a higher energy level.
- Electrons can release energy and move to a lower energy level.

Important:
Electrons do not simply occupy any arbitrary energy level in the Bohr model.

Electron shells are commonly represented by:
- First shell: K
- Second shell: L
- Third shell: M
- Fourth shell: N

For the simplified electron arrangement of the first 20 elements, the maximum numbers used in the basic shell-filling model are:
- First shell: 2 electrons
- Second shell: 8 electrons
- Third shell: 8 electrons for the first 20 elements in the school-level arrangement
- Fourth shell: begins to fill after the third shell has 8 electrons in this simplified arrangement.

Limitation:
The Bohr model is a simplified representation. Modern atomic theory describes electrons using orbitals and probability distributions rather than fixed circular paths.

2.2.6 CHADWICK'S ATOMIC MODEL

Scientist:
James Chadwick.

Discovery:
The neutron in 1932.

Main contribution:
Chadwick provided evidence for a neutral particle in the nucleus, called the neutron.

Importance:
- Explained the existence of particles in the nucleus with no electrical charge.
- Helped explain why the mass of an atom is greater than the mass accounted for by its protons alone.
- Helped establish the modern picture of the nucleus containing protons and neutrons.

2.2.7 SEQUENCE OF THE DEVELOPMENT OF ATOMIC MODELS

The historical sequence:

1. Dalton
   - Atom represented as a solid sphere.
   - Atoms were considered indivisible.

2. Thomson
   - Positive sphere with embedded electrons.
   - Plum pudding model.

3. Rutherford
   - Small, dense, positive nucleus.
   - Most of the atom is empty space.

4. Bohr
   - Electrons occupy specific energy levels or shells.

5. Chadwick
   - Discovery of the neutron.
   - Nucleus contains protons and neutrons.

IMPORTANT EXAM POINT:

Students must know the correct sequence and the main contribution of each scientist.

Do not mix up:
- Thomson: discovery of electron.
- Rutherford: nuclear model of atom.
- Bohr: electron energy levels.
- Chadwick: discovery of neutron.

2.3 ATOMIC STRUCTURE
------------------------------------------------------------

2.3.1 SUBATOMIC PARTICLES

Atoms contain three main subatomic particles.

PROTON

Symbol: p or p+

Relative charge: +1

Relative mass: 1

Location: Nucleus.

Characteristics:
- Positively charged.
- Contributes to the mass of the atom.
- Determines the identity of an element through the proton number.

NEUTRON

Symbol: n

Relative charge: 0

Relative mass: 1

Location: Nucleus.

Characteristics:
- Electrically neutral.
- Contributes to the mass of the atom.
- Different numbers of neutrons give rise to isotopes of an element.

ELECTRON

Symbol: e or e-

Relative charge: -1

Relative mass: 1/1840 (approximately).

Location: Electron shells or regions around the nucleus.

Characteristics:
- Negatively charged.
- Has a very small mass compared with a proton or neutron.
- Involved in chemical bonding and the formation of ions.

COMPARISON TABLE

Proton:
- Charge: +1
- Relative mass: 1
- Location: Nucleus

Neutron:
- Charge: 0
- Relative mass: 1
- Location: Nucleus

Electron:
- Charge: -1
- Relative mass: 1/1840
- Location: Outside nucleus

2.3.2 WHY IS AN ATOM ELECTRICALLY NEUTRAL?

A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons.

Protons have positive charges.

Electrons have negative charges.

The charges cancel each other when their numbers are equal.

Example:

A sodium atom has:
- 11 protons.
- 11 electrons.

Total positive charge = +11.

Total negative charge = -11.

Net charge = 0.

Therefore, the sodium atom is electrically neutral.

2.3.3 PROTON NUMBER

Proton number is the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom.

Symbol: Z.

The proton number identifies the element.

Examples:

Hydrogen:
Proton number = 1.

Carbon:
Proton number = 6.

Oxygen:
Proton number = 8.

Sodium:
Proton number = 11.

Chlorine:
Proton number = 17.

IMPORTANT:
Every atom of the same element has the same proton number.

For example:
Every carbon atom has 6 protons.

Changing the number of protons changes the identity of the element.

2.3.4 NUCLEON NUMBER

Nucleon number is the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus of an atom.

Symbol: A.

Formula:

Nucleon number = Number of protons + Number of neutrons

Therefore:

A = Z + Number of neutrons

Number of neutrons:

Number of neutrons = A - Z

Example:

An atom has:
Proton number = 11.
Nucleon number = 23.

Number of neutrons:

= 23 - 11
= 12

Answer: 12 neutrons.

2.3.5 STANDARD REPRESENTATION OF AN ATOM

The standard representation of an atom is written as:

      A
      X
      Z

In text notation:

^A_Z X

Where:
- X = Chemical symbol.
- A = Nucleon number.
- Z = Proton number.

Example:

^23_11 Na

This represents a sodium atom.

Information:
- Element: Sodium.
- Proton number: 11.
- Nucleon number: 23.
- Number of protons: 11.
- Number of neutrons: 23 - 11 = 12.
- Number of electrons in a neutral atom: 11.

Another example:

^35_17 Cl

Information:
- Element: Chlorine.
- Proton number: 17.
- Nucleon number: 35.
- Number of protons: 17.
- Number of neutrons: 18.
- Number of electrons in a neutral atom: 17.

2.3.6 DETERMINING THE NUMBER OF SUBATOMIC PARTICLES

For a neutral atom:

Number of protons = Proton number.

Number of electrons = Proton number.

Number of neutrons = Nucleon number - Proton number.

Example 1:

^27_13 Al

Protons = 13.

Electrons = 13.

Neutrons = 27 - 13 = 14.

Example 2:

^40_20 Ca

Protons = 20.

Electrons = 20.

Neutrons = 40 - 20 = 20.

Example 3:

^16_8 O

Protons = 8.

Electrons = 8.

Neutrons = 16 - 8 = 8.

2.3.7 ELECTRON ARRANGEMENT

Electron arrangement describes how electrons are distributed among the shells of an atom.

For the first 20 elements, the simplified shell arrangement is commonly used.

Maximum shell capacities in the simplified model:
- First shell: 2.
- Second shell: 8.
- Third shell: 8 for the first 20 elements.
- Fourth shell: begins after the third shell has 8 electrons in this arrangement.

Examples:

Nitrogen:

Oxygen:
Proton number = 8.
Electron arrangement = 2.6.

Fluorine:
Proton number = 9.
Electron arrangement = 2.7.

Neon:
Proton number = 10.
Electron arrangement = 2.8.

Sodium:
Proton number = 11.
Electron arrangement = 2.8.1.

Magnesium:
Proton number = 12.

IMPORTANT:

2.3.8 VALENCE ELECTRONS

Valence electrons are the electrons in the outermost occupied electron shell of an atom.

Importance:
- They determine many chemical properties.
- They are involved in chemical bonding.
- They influence how atoms form ions.

Examples:

Sodium:
Electron arrangement = 2.8.1.
Valence electrons = 1.

Magnesium:
Electron arrangement = 2.8.2.
Valence electrons = 2.

Chlorine:
Electron arrangement = 2.8.7.
Valence electrons = 7.

Argon:
Electron arrangement = 2.8.8.
Valence electrons = 8.

2.3.9 FORMATION OF IONS

An ion is a charged particle formed when an atom or group of atoms loses or gains electrons.

IMPORTANT:
Protons are not transferred during ordinary ion formation.

A. FORMATION OF POSITIVE IONS

A positive ion is formed when an atom loses electrons.

The number of electrons becomes smaller than the number of protons.

Example: Sodium.

Neutral sodium:
- 11 protons.
- 11 electrons.
- Electron arrangement: 2.8.1.

Sodium loses one electron.

Sodium ion:
Na+

- 11 protons.
- 10 electrons.
- Electron arrangement: 2.8.

The sodium ion has a +1 charge because it has one more proton than electron.

B. FORMATION OF NEGATIVE IONS

A negative ion is formed when an atom gains electrons.

The number of electrons becomes greater than the number of protons.

Example: Chlorine.

Neutral chlorine:
- 17 protons.
- 17 electrons.
- Electron arrangement: 2.8.7.

Chlorine gains one electron.

Chloride ion:
Cl-

- 17 protons.
- 18 electrons.
- Electron arrangement: 2.8.8.

The chloride ion has a -1 charge because it has one more electron than proton.

IMPORTANT COMPARISON:

Cation:
- Positive ion.
- Formed by losing electrons.
- Has fewer electrons than protons.

Anion:
- Negative ion.
- Formed by gaining electrons.
- Has more electrons than protons.

2.3.10 CALCULATING ELECTRONS IN IONS

For a positive ion:

Number of electrons = Proton number - Positive charge magnitude.

For a negative ion:

Number of electrons = Proton number + Negative charge magnitude.

Example 1:

Mg2+

Proton number = 12.

Number of electrons:
= 12 - 2
= 10.

Example 2:

Al3+

Proton number = 13.

Number of electrons:
= 13 - 3
= 10.

Example 3:

Cl-

Proton number = 17.

Number of electrons:
= 17 + 1
= 18.

Example 4:

O2-

Proton number = 8.

Number of electrons:
= 8 + 2
= 10.

Remember:
The number of protons and neutrons does not change when an ordinary atom forms an ion.

2.4 ISOTOPES AND ITS USES
============================================================

2.4.1 DEFINITION OF ISOTOPES

Isotopes are atoms of the same element that have the same proton number but different nucleon numbers.

This means isotopes have:
- The same number of protons.
- Different numbers of neutrons.
- Different nucleon numbers.

For neutral atoms of the same element, isotopes also have the same number of electrons.

Example:

Carbon-12 and Carbon-14.

Carbon-12:
- Proton number = 6.
- Nucleon number = 12.
- Number of neutrons = 12 - 6 = 6.

Carbon-14:
- Proton number = 6.
- Nucleon number = 14.
- Number of neutrons = 14 - 6 = 8.

Both are carbon atoms because both contain 6 protons.

They are isotopes because they have different numbers of neutrons.

2.4.2 WHY DO ISOTOPES HAVE THE SAME CHEMICAL PROPERTIES?

Isotopes of the same element have the same proton number.

Neutral isotopes therefore have the same number of electrons and the same electron arrangement.

Chemical properties are mainly determined by electron arrangement, especially the valence electrons.

Therefore, isotopes of the same element generally have similar chemical properties.

However, their physical properties can differ because their masses are different.

2.4.3 NATURAL ABUNDANCE OF ISOTOPES

Natural abundance refers to the percentage of each isotope of an element found naturally.

Some elements exist naturally as mixtures of isotopes.

Different isotopes may have different natural abundances.

Example:

Chlorine has two common naturally occurring isotopes:
- Chlorine-35.
- Chlorine-37.

Their approximate natural abundances are:
- Chlorine-35: 75%.
- Chlorine-37: 25%.

The natural abundance is important when calculating the relative atomic mass of an element.

2.4.4 RELATIVE ATOMIC MASS

Relative atomic mass is the weighted average mass of the atoms of an element compared with one-twelfth of the mass of one carbon-12 atom.

Symbol: Ar.

Important characteristics:
- It is a relative value.
- It has no unit.
- It takes isotopic abundance into account.
- It is not necessarily a whole number.

The relative atomic mass of an element can be calculated using the relative masses and natural abundances of its isotopes.

FORMULA:

Relative atomic mass =
[Sum of (isotopic mass x percentage abundance)]
/ 100

In general:

Ar = [(m1 x a1) + (m2 x a2) + ...] / 100

Where:
m = isotopic mass.
a = percentage abundance.

2.4.5 WORKED CALCULATION: RELATIVE ATOMIC MASS

Example 1:

An element has two isotopes:

Isotope A:
Mass = 10.
Natural abundance = 20%.

Isotope B:
Mass = 11.
Natural abundance = 80%.

Calculate its relative atomic mass.

Step 1: Write the formula.

Ar = [(10 x 20) + (11 x 80)] / 100

Step 2: Multiply each isotope mass by its percentage abundance.

= (200 + 880) / 100

Step 3: Add the values.

= 1080 / 100

Step 4: Calculate.

Ar = 10.8

Answer: 10.8.

Example 2: Chlorine

Chlorine consists of:
- Chlorine-35: 75%.
- Chlorine-37: 25%.

Calculate its relative atomic mass.

Ar = [(35 x 75) + (37 x 25)] / 100

= (2625 + 925) / 100

= 3550 / 100

Ar = 35.5

Answer: 35.5.

Example 3: Finding an unknown abundance

An element has two isotopes:
- Isotope X has mass 20.
- Isotope Y has mass 22.

The relative atomic mass is 20.4.

Calculate the percentage abundance of each isotope.

Let the abundance of isotope X be x%.

The abundance of isotope Y is (100 - x)%.

Use the weighted average formula:

20.4 = [20x + 22(100 - x)] / 100

Multiply both sides by 100:

2040 = 20x + 2200 - 22x

2040 = 2200 - 2x

2x = 160

x = 80

Therefore:
- Isotope X = 80%.
- Isotope Y = 20%.

Answer:
80% and 20%.

CHECKING CALCULATIONS:

The sum of all isotope percentage abundances must equal 100%.

The calculated relative atomic mass must lie between the masses of the lightest and heaviest isotopes.

2.4.6 USES OF ISOTOPES

Some isotopes are radioactive.

Radioactive isotopes contain unstable nuclei that release radiation as they change into more stable forms.

The radiation emitted can be useful in medicine, agriculture, industry and scientific research.

Important distinction:

Stable isotopes:
Do not undergo spontaneous radioactive decay.

Radioactive isotopes:
Have unstable nuclei and emit radiation as they decay.

A. MEDICINE

Cobalt-60:
- Used as a source of gamma radiation in radiotherapy.
- Gamma radiation can be used to destroy cancer cells under carefully controlled medical conditions.
- Treatment must be planned and controlled by trained medical professionals.

Technetium-99m:
- Used in medical imaging.
- Emits gamma radiation that can be detected by specialised equipment.
- Helps doctors examine organs and investigate certain medical conditions.

Iodine-131:
- Used in certain medical treatments involving the thyroid gland.
- Its use is based on the thyroid's ability to take up iodine.

B. AGRICULTURE

Phosphorus-32:
- Can be used as a tracer to study the uptake and movement of phosphorus in plants.
- Helps researchers investigate how plants absorb nutrients.

Other suitable radioactive tracers:
- Used to study nutrient movement and biological processes.
- Can help improve agricultural research.

C. INDUSTRY

Cobalt-60:
- Gamma radiation can be used in industrial radiography.
- Helps inspect internal structures and identify defects in materials.

Radioisotopes can also be used in thickness monitoring:
- Radiation passing through a material can be measured.
- Changes in the detected radiation can indicate changes in material thickness.

D. ARCHAEOLOGY AND GEOLOGY

Carbon-14:
- Used in radiocarbon dating.
- Helps estimate the age of once-living materials.
- The method is useful for suitable archaeological samples.

Important:
Carbon-14 dating is not suitable for determining the age of every type of rock or every ancient object.

E. SCIENTIFIC RESEARCH

Radioisotopes can be used as tracers.

A tracer is a substance that can be followed or detected to study the movement of materials through a system.

Examples:
- Studying nutrient movement in plants.
- Investigating biological processes.
- Studying the movement of substances in research systems.

IMPORTANT EXAM POINT:

When asked to justify the use of an isotope, state:
1. The name of the isotope.
2. The field in which it is used.
3. Its specific application.
4. Why its properties make it suitable.

Example:

2.1 SOLID, LIQUID AND GAS

Solid:
- Fixed shape.
- Fixed volume.
- Particles vibrate at fixed positions.

Liquid:
- No fixed shape.
- Fixed volume.
- Particles slide past one another.

Gas:
- No fixed shape.
- No fixed volume.
- Particles move freely and randomly.

2.2 EVAPORATION AND BOILING

Evaporation:
- Occurs at the surface.
- Can occur below the boiling point.
- Does not require the entire liquid to boil.

Boiling:
- Occurs throughout the liquid.
- Takes place at the boiling point for a given pressure.
- Bubbles form throughout the liquid.

2.3 MELTING AND FREEZING

Melting:
- Solid becomes liquid.
- Heat absorbed.
- Particles gain energy.

Freezing:
- Liquid becomes solid.
- Heat released.
- Particles lose energy.

2.4 THE DEVELOPMENT OF ATOMIC MODELS

Dalton:
Solid sphere.

Thomson:
Positive sphere containing electrons.

Rutherford:
Small, dense, positive nucleus and mostly empty space.

Bohr:
Electrons occupy specific energy levels.

Chadwick:
Discovery of neutron.

Proton:
- Positive charge.
- Relative mass 1.
- In nucleus.

Neutron:
- No charge.
- Relative mass 1.
- In nucleus.

Electron:
- Negative charge.
- Relative mass 1/1840.
- Outside nucleus.

2.6 PROTON NUMBER AND NUCLEON NUMBER

Proton number:
Number of protons.

Nucleon number:
Number of protons plus neutrons.

Neutrons:
Nucleon number - Proton number.

2.7 ATOM AND ION

Neutral atom:
Number of protons = Number of electrons.

Positive ion:
Has lost electrons.

Negative ion:
Has gained electrons.

2.8 ISOTOPES

Same:

- Element identity.
- Electron arrangement for neutral atoms.

Different:
- Number of neutrons.
- Nucleon number.
- Mass.

- Weighted average of isotope masses.
- Takes natural abundance into account.
- Has no unit.
- May be a decimal.

3.5 IMPORTANT SCIENTIFIC RULES

- Proton number determines the identity of an element.
- Nucleon number is the total number of protons and neutrons.
- Electrons have a much smaller relative mass than protons and neutrons.
- Ordinary ion formation involves gaining or losing electrons.
- Isotopes have the same proton number but different neutron numbers.
- Relative atomic mass is a weighted average.
- Percentage abundances must add up to 100%.
- Relative atomic mass has no unit.

4.1 IMPORTANT DEFINITIONS TO MEMORISE

Matter:
Anything that has mass and occupies space.

Atom:
The smallest particle of an element that retains the chemical properties of that element.

Molecule:
A group of two or more atoms chemically bonded together.

Ion:
An electrically charged particle formed when an atom or group of atoms loses or gains electrons.

Proton number:
The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom.

Nucleon number:
The total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus of an atom.

Isotopes:
Atoms of the same element that have the same proton number but different nucleon numbers.

Relative atomic mass:
The weighted average mass of the atoms of an element compared with one-twelfth of the mass of one carbon-12 atom.

4.2 COMMON EXAM MISTAKES

Mistake 1:
Confusing proton number with nucleon number.

Correction:
Proton number counts only protons.
Nucleon number counts protons and neutrons.

Mistake 2:
Subtracting proton number from electron number to calculate neutrons.

Correction:
Number of neutrons = Nucleon number - Proton number.

Mistake 3:
Thinking that electrons have the same mass as protons.

Correction:
The relative mass of an electron is approximately 1/1840.

Mistake 4:
Thinking that positive ions gain electrons.

Correction:
Positive ions form when atoms lose electrons.

Mistake 5:
Thinking that negative ions lose electrons.

Correction:
Negative ions form when atoms gain electrons.

Mistake 6:
Thinking that isotopes have different proton numbers.

Correction:
Isotopes have the same proton number but different numbers of neutrons.

Mistake 7:
Thinking that relative atomic mass must always be a whole number.

Correction:
Relative atomic mass is a weighted average and can be a decimal.

Mistake 8:
Forgetting to divide by 100 when using percentage abundance.

Correction:
Use the correct weighted-average formula.

Mistake 9:
Confusing evaporation with boiling.

Correction:
Evaporation occurs at the surface and can happen below the boiling point. Boiling occurs throughout the liquid at its boiling point.

Mistake 10:
Saying that temperature increases during melting.

Correction:
For a pure substance at constant pressure, temperature remains constant during melting.

4.3 CALCULATION TECHNIQUE

For subatomic particle questions:

Step 1:
Identify the proton number.

Step 2:
Identify the nucleon number.

Step 3:
Calculate neutrons using A - Z.

Step 4:
Determine electrons based on whether the species is neutral or an ion.

For isotope calculations:

Step 1:
Write the isotope masses.

Step 2:
Write the percentage abundance of each isotope.

Step 3:
Multiply each mass by its percentage abundance.

Step 4:
Add all the products.

Step 5:
Divide by 100.

Step 6:
Check that the result lies between the isotope masses.

Suggested answer:
Condensation.

(c) Explain why temperature remains constant during the melting of a pure substance. [2 marks]

The heat energy absorbed is used to overcome attractive forces between particles rather than increasing their average kinetic energy. Therefore, the temperature remains constant during melting.

Evaporation occurs at the surface of a liquid and can occur below its boiling point. Boiling occurs throughout the liquid at its boiling point.
