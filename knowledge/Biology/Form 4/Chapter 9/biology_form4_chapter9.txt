# CHAPTER 9: NUTRITION AND THE HUMAN DIGESTIVE SYSTEM

## 9.1 DIGESTIVE SYSTEM

### 9.1.1 Importance of Nutrition

Nutrition is the process of obtaining and using nutrients from food.

Humans need nutrients for:

- Growth and development
- Repair of damaged tissues
- Production of energy
- Maintaining body temperature
- Maintaining normal body functions
- Replacing worn-out cells
- Maintaining good health

The main classes of nutrients are:

1. Carbohydrates
2. Proteins
3. Lipids
4. Vitamins
5. Mineral salts
6. Fibre
7. Water

---

## 9.1.2 Human Digestive System

The human digestive system consists of the alimentary canal and several accessory organs.

### Alimentary canal

The alimentary canal is a continuous muscular tube extending from the mouth to the anus.

The main organs are:

**Mouth → Oesophagus → Stomach → Small intestine → Large intestine → Rectum → Anus**

### Accessory organs

Important accessory organs include:

- Salivary glands
- Liver
- Gall bladder
- Pancreas

These organs produce or store substances needed for digestion.

---

## 9.1.3 Functions of Digestive Organs

### Mouth

Digestion begins in the mouth.

The mouth contains:

- Teeth
- Tongue
- Salivary glands

#### Teeth

Teeth carry out mechanical digestion by cutting, tearing and grinding food.

Different teeth have different functions.

**Incisors**
- Cut food.

**Canines**
- Tear food.

**Premolars and molars**
- Crush and grind food.

#### Tongue

The tongue:

- Mixes food with saliva.
- Helps form a bolus.
- Pushes the bolus towards the pharynx during swallowing.
- Contains taste receptors.

#### Salivary glands

Salivary glands produce saliva.

Saliva:

- Moistens food.
- Helps form the bolus.
- Contains salivary amylase.

Salivary amylase begins the digestion of starch.

---

### Pharynx

The pharynx is a common passage for food and air.

During swallowing, the epiglottis helps prevent food from entering the trachea.

---

### Oesophagus

The oesophagus connects the pharynx to the stomach.

Food is moved through the oesophagus by **peristalsis**.

Peristalsis is the rhythmic contraction and relaxation of muscles in the wall of the alimentary canal that pushes food along the canal.

---

### Stomach

The stomach is a muscular organ.

Its functions include:

- Temporary storage of food.
- Mechanical digestion through muscular contractions.
- Mixing food with gastric juice.
- Beginning protein digestion.

The stomach produces hydrochloric acid.

Hydrochloric acid:

- Provides an acidic medium.
- Provides the suitable pH for pepsin to function.
- Helps kill some microorganisms entering with food.

The stomach also produces mucus that helps protect its lining from the acidic contents and digestive enzymes.

---

### Liver

The liver is the largest internal organ.

One important digestive function of the liver is producing **bile**.

Bile:

- Is alkaline.
- Neutralises acidic chyme entering the small intestine.
- Emulsifies fats into smaller droplets.

Bile does **not** contain digestive enzymes.

The liver also performs many other functions, including processing absorbed nutrients and detoxification.

---

### Gall bladder

The gall bladder stores and concentrates bile produced by the liver.

When required, bile is released into the small intestine.

---

### Pancreas

The pancreas produces pancreatic juice.

Pancreatic juice contains digestive enzymes, including enzymes that digest:

- Carbohydrates
- Proteins
- Lipids

It also contributes to neutralising the acidic contents entering the small intestine.

---

### Small intestine

The small intestine is the main site of:

- Completion of digestion
- Absorption of digested nutrients

The inner surface contains numerous finger-like projections called **villi**.

Each villus contains:

- A network of blood capillaries
- A lacteal

The villi greatly increase the surface area for absorption.

---

### Large intestine

The large intestine absorbs:

- Water
- Some mineral salts

The remaining undigested material becomes more solid as water is absorbed.

---

### Rectum

The rectum temporarily stores faeces.

---

### Anus

The anus is the opening through which faeces are expelled from the body.

---

# 9.2 DIGESTION

## 9.2.1 Meaning of Digestion

Digestion is the process of breaking down large, complex and insoluble food molecules into smaller, simpler and soluble molecules that can be absorbed by the body.

Digestion is necessary because large food molecules generally cannot pass through the intestinal wall directly.

---

## 9.2.2 Types of Digestion

There are two main types.

### Mechanical digestion

Mechanical digestion breaks food into smaller pieces without changing its chemical composition.

Examples:

- Chewing by teeth
- Churning in the stomach

Mechanical digestion increases the surface area of food exposed to digestive enzymes.

---

### Chemical digestion

Chemical digestion uses digestive enzymes to break large food molecules into smaller molecules.

Examples:

- Starch → maltose
- Proteins → peptides
- Lipids → fatty acids and glycerol

---

# 9.2.3 Digestion of Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates include:

- Starch
- Glycogen
- Disaccharides
- Monosaccharides

Starch is a large carbohydrate molecule that must be broken down before absorption.

### In the mouth

Salivary amylase breaks starch into maltose.

**Starch → Maltose**

Salivary amylase works best in a suitable near-neutral/slightly alkaline environment.

---

### In the stomach

The acidic condition in the stomach stops the action of salivary amylase.

Therefore, carbohydrate digestion by salivary amylase does not continue effectively in the stomach.

---

### In the small intestine

Pancreatic amylase continues the digestion of starch.

**Starch → Maltose**

Maltose is then broken down by maltase.

**Maltose → Glucose + Glucose**

Glucose is a monosaccharide and can be absorbed into the blood.

---

# 9.2.4 Digestion of Proteins

Proteins are large molecules made from amino acids.

### In the stomach

The stomach secretes pepsin.

Pepsin breaks proteins into smaller peptides.

**Protein → Peptides**

Hydrochloric acid provides a suitable acidic medium for pepsin.

---

### In the small intestine

Protease enzymes from the pancreas and intestinal wall continue protein digestion.

Peptides are broken down into amino acids.

**Peptides → Amino acids**

Amino acids can then be absorbed.

---

# 9.2.5 Digestion of Lipids

Lipids include fats and oils.

Lipids are digested into:

**Fatty acids + glycerol**

### Role of bile

Bile does not digest lipids chemically.

Instead, bile emulsifies fats.

Emulsification breaks large fat droplets into many smaller droplets.

This:

- Increases the surface area of fat.
- Allows lipase to act more efficiently.

---

### Lipase

Lipase catalyses the digestion of lipids.

**Lipids → Fatty acids + glycerol**

---

# 9.2.6 Digestive Enzymes Summary

| Enzyme | Substrate | Product | Main site |
|---|---|---|---|
| Amylase | Starch | Maltose | Mouth, small intestine |
| Maltase | Maltose | Glucose | Small intestine |
| Pepsin | Protein | Peptides | Stomach |
| Proteases | Proteins/peptides | Smaller peptides/amino acids | Small intestine |
| Lipase | Lipids | Fatty acids + glycerol | Small intestine |

### Important distinction

**Enzymes catalyse digestion.**

**Bile does not contain digestive enzymes.**

Bile helps fat digestion by emulsifying fats and helping create a suitable alkaline environment in the small intestine.

---

# 9.2.7 Conditions Required for Enzyme Action

Digestive enzymes require suitable conditions.

Important factors include:

- Temperature
- pH
- Substrate concentration
- Enzyme concentration

Human digestive enzymes generally work best around body temperature.

Very high temperatures can denature enzymes.

Different enzymes have different optimum pH values.

For example:

- Pepsin works in an acidic environment.
- Many intestinal enzymes work better in an alkaline environment.

---

# 9.2.8 Peristalsis

Peristalsis is the rhythmic contraction and relaxation of the muscles in the wall of the alimentary canal.

The circular muscles contract behind the food bolus while muscles in front relax.

This creates a wave that pushes food forward.

Peristalsis allows food to move through the alimentary canal even when a person is not upright.

---

# 9.3 ABSORPTION

## 9.3.1 Meaning of Absorption

Absorption is the process by which digested, soluble nutrients pass through the wall of the small intestine into the blood capillaries or lacteals.

Most absorption occurs in the small intestine.

---

# 9.3.2 Adaptations of the Small Intestine for Absorption

The small intestine is highly adapted for efficient absorption.

### 1. Very long

Its considerable length provides a large area for absorption.

### 2. Numerous villi

The inner surface contains numerous villi.

### 3. Microvilli

The epithelial cells of villi have microvilli, further increasing surface area.

### 4. Thin epithelium

The wall of each villus has a thin epithelial layer.

This provides a short diffusion distance.

### 5. Dense blood capillary network

Each villus contains many blood capillaries.

These rapidly transport absorbed nutrients away from the villus.

### 6. Lacteal

Each villus contains a lacteal.

The lacteal is involved in the absorption and transport of products of lipid digestion.

---

# 9.3.3 Absorption of Glucose and Amino Acids

Glucose and amino acids enter the blood capillaries in the villi.

They are transported through the hepatic portal vein to the liver.

The liver processes and regulates absorbed nutrients before they enter the general circulation.

---

# 9.3.4 Absorption of Fatty Acids and Glycerol

Products of lipid digestion are absorbed through the intestinal epithelium.

They are associated with lipid transport particles and enter the lacteals.

They are transported through the lymphatic system before eventually entering the bloodstream.

---

# 9.3.5 Diffusion and Active Transport

Some nutrients can move into intestinal cells by diffusion when a concentration gradient exists.

Other nutrients can be absorbed by active transport.

### Diffusion

Particles move from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration.

### Active transport

Substances are moved against a concentration gradient using energy.

Active transport allows nutrients to be absorbed even when their concentration in the intestine is lower than their concentration in the intestinal cells.

---

# 9.4 ASSIMILATION

## 9.4.1 Meaning of Assimilation

Assimilation is the process by which absorbed nutrients are incorporated into body cells and used for various functions.

Absorption and assimilation are not the same.

**Absorption:** Nutrients enter the blood or lymph.

**Assimilation:** Nutrients are used by cells.

---

## 9.4.2 Assimilation of Glucose

Glucose can be:

- Used in cellular respiration to release energy.
- Converted to glycogen for storage in the liver and muscles.
- Converted into other substances required by the body.

Excess glucose is not simply stored indefinitely as glucose.

It can be converted into other storage substances, including fat, when energy intake exceeds requirements.

---

## 9.4.3 Assimilation of Amino Acids

Amino acids are used to:

- Build new proteins.
- Repair damaged tissues.
- Produce enzymes.
- Produce some hormones and other important molecules.

Excess amino acids cannot be stored as amino acids.

They undergo deamination mainly in the liver.

The nitrogen-containing part is removed and eventually converted into urea for excretion.

---

## 9.4.4 Assimilation of Fatty Acids and Glycerol

Fatty acids and glycerol can be used:

- To form lipids.
- As an energy source.
- For building cell membranes.
- For energy storage in adipose tissue.

Lipids are also important components of cell membranes.

---

# 9.5 DEFAECATION

## 9.5.1 Meaning of Defaecation

Defaecation is the process of expelling faeces from the body through the anus.

Faeces consist mainly of:

- Undigested food material
- Fibre
- Water
- Bacteria
- Dead cells
- Other waste materials

---

## 9.5.2 Formation of Faeces

After digestion and absorption occur in the small intestine, undigested material enters the large intestine.

The large intestine absorbs water and some mineral salts.

As water is removed, the remaining material becomes faeces.

Faeces are stored temporarily in the rectum.

When the rectum is sufficiently stretched, the defaecation reflex is triggered.

The faeces are expelled through the anus.

---

## 9.5.3 Importance of Dietary Fibre

Dietary fibre is largely indigestible material from plant foods.

Fibre:

- Adds bulk to faeces.
- Helps stimulate movement of the alimentary canal.
- Helps prevent constipation.

Adequate water intake is also important for maintaining softer faeces.

---

# 9.6 BALANCED DIET

## 9.6.1 Meaning of a Balanced Diet

A balanced diet contains all the essential nutrients in appropriate amounts according to the body's requirements.

The nutrients include:

- Carbohydrates
- Proteins
- Lipids
- Vitamins
- Mineral salts
- Fibre
- Water

The amount required varies according to factors such as:

- Age
- Sex
- Body size
- Physical activity
- Health condition
- Pregnancy or growth requirements

---

# 9.6.2 Functions of Nutrients

## Carbohydrates

Main function:

- Provide energy.

Sources include:

- Rice
- Bread
- Potatoes
- Cereals
- Fruits

---

## Proteins

Functions:

- Growth
- Repair of tissues
- Formation of enzymes
- Formation of some hormones
- Formation of antibodies

Sources include:

- Fish
- Meat
- Eggs
- Milk
- Beans
- Soy products

---

## Lipids

Functions:

- Concentrated source of energy
- Formation of cell membranes
- Insulation
- Protection of organs
- Absorption of fat-soluble vitamins

Sources include:

- Oils
- Butter
- Nuts
- Seeds
- Fatty fish

---

## Vitamins

Vitamins are required in small amounts for normal body functions.

Examples:

**Vitamin A**
- Important for normal vision and healthy epithelial tissues.

**Vitamin C**
- Important for collagen formation and healthy tissues.

**Vitamin D**
- Important for calcium absorption and healthy bones.

---

## Mineral Salts

Mineral salts are inorganic substances required by the body.

Examples:

**Calcium**
- Important for bones and teeth.
- Important in muscle contraction and blood clotting.

**Iron**
- Required to form haemoglobin.

**Iodine**
- Required for production of thyroid hormones.

---

## Fibre

Functions:

- Adds bulk to faeces.
- Helps maintain normal movement of the alimentary canal.
- Helps prevent constipation.

Sources:

- Fruits
- Vegetables
- Whole grains
- Legumes

---

## Water

Water is important for:

- Chemical reactions
- Transport of substances
- Removal of metabolic waste
- Temperature regulation
- Maintaining normal cell function

---

# 9.6.3 Energy Value of Food

Energy is required for:

- Cellular respiration
- Movement
- Growth
- Repair
- Maintaining body temperature
- Other metabolic activities

The energy value of food can be expressed in **kilojoules (kJ)**.

### Energy values

**Carbohydrates: approximately 17 kJ per gram**

**Proteins: approximately 17 kJ per gram**

**Lipids: approximately 39 kJ per gram**

Lipids provide more than twice as much energy per gram as carbohydrates or proteins.

---

# 9.6.4 Formula for Calculating Energy Value

### Basic formula

**Energy value = mass of nutrient × energy value per gram**

Therefore:

### Carbohydrate

**Energy = mass × 17 kJ/g**

### Protein

**Energy = mass × 17 kJ/g**

### Lipid

**Energy = mass × 39 kJ/g**

---

# 9.6.5 Worked Example 1

A food contains:

- 20 g carbohydrate
- 10 g protein
- 5 g lipid

Calculate the total energy provided.

### Step 1: Energy from carbohydrate

20 × 17 = **340 kJ**

### Step 2: Energy from protein

10 × 17 = **170 kJ**

### Step 3: Energy from lipid

5 × 39 = **195 kJ**

### Step 4: Total

340 + 170 + 195 = **705 kJ**

Therefore, the total energy provided is:

**705 kJ**

---

# 9.6.6 Worked Example 2

A meal contains 30 g of carbohydrate and 8 g of lipid.

Calculate the energy provided by the meal.

Carbohydrate:

30 × 17 = 510 kJ

Lipid:

8 × 39 = 312 kJ

Total:

510 + 312 = **822 kJ**

---

# 9.6.7 Calculating the Mass of a Nutrient

The formula can be rearranged.

### Mass of nutrient

**Mass = energy value ÷ energy value per gram**

Example:

A food provides 780 kJ from lipid.

Mass of lipid:

780 ÷ 39 = **20 g**

---

# 9.6.8 Comparing Energy Contributions

Example:

Food A contains:

- 10 g carbohydrate
- 10 g protein
- 10 g lipid

Energy:

Carbohydrate = 10 × 17 = 170 kJ

Protein = 10 × 17 = 170 kJ

Lipid = 10 × 39 = 390 kJ

Total = **730 kJ**

Although the mass of each nutrient is the same, lipid provides much more energy.

---

# 9.6.9 Important Calculation Rule

Always check the units.

If the mass is given in grams:

**grams × kJ/g = kJ**

Do not accidentally multiply the total food mass by 17 or 39.

Only the mass of the specific nutrient is multiplied by its corresponding energy value.

---

# 9.6.10 Energy Balance

Energy balance occurs when energy intake is approximately equal to energy expenditure over time.

### Energy intake > energy expenditure

Excess energy may be stored, mainly as body fat.

Long-term excessive energy intake can contribute to overweight and obesity.

### Energy intake < energy expenditure

The body may use stored energy reserves.

Long-term insufficient energy intake can lead to undernutrition and other health problems.

---

# 9.7 HEALTH ISSUES RELATED TO THE DIGESTIVE SYSTEM AND EATING HABITS

## 9.7.1 Constipation

Constipation occurs when defaecation is difficult or faeces are hard and difficult to pass.

Factors that can contribute include:

- Low fibre intake
- Insufficient water intake
- Lack of physical activity
- Ignoring the urge to defaecate

### Prevention

- Eat sufficient fibre.
- Drink adequate water.
- Maintain regular physical activity.
- Maintain regular bowel habits.

---

# 9.7.2 Diarrhoea

Diarrhoea involves frequent passage of loose or watery faeces.

It can result in excessive loss of:

- Water
- Mineral salts

Severe diarrhoea can lead to dehydration.

Maintaining adequate fluid and electrolyte replacement is important, especially when fluid loss is significant.

---

# 9.7.3 Obesity

Obesity occurs when excessive body fat accumulates to a level that can negatively affect health.

A major contributing factor can be long-term energy intake exceeding energy expenditure.

Risk can be influenced by:

- Excessive energy intake
- High-fat or high-sugar dietary patterns
- Physical inactivity
- Genetic and environmental factors

Obesity can increase the risk of several health problems.

---

# 9.7.4 Malnutrition

Malnutrition occurs when the body receives an inadequate, excessive or imbalanced supply of nutrients.

Malnutrition can involve:

- Undernutrition
- Specific nutrient deficiencies
- Excessive nutrient or energy intake

A person can therefore be malnourished even if they are not visibly underweight.

---

# 9.7.5 Deficiency Diseases

An inadequate intake of certain nutrients can cause deficiency diseases.

### Iron deficiency

Can lead to anaemia.

Iron is required for haemoglobin production.

Possible effects include:

- Tiredness
- Weakness
- Reduced ability of blood to transport oxygen

---

### Vitamin C deficiency

Can lead to scurvy.

Possible effects include:

- Poor wound healing
- Bleeding gums
- Weak connective tissues

---

### Vitamin D deficiency

Can interfere with normal bone development and mineralisation.

---

### Iodine deficiency

Can interfere with normal thyroid function and may cause enlargement of the thyroid gland.

---

# 9.7.6 Food Poisoning

Food poisoning occurs when contaminated food or drink causes illness.

Contamination can involve:

- Pathogenic microorganisms
- Toxins
- Harmful chemicals

Good food hygiene reduces the risk.

Examples of good practices:

- Wash hands before handling food.
- Keep raw and cooked foods separate.
- Cook food thoroughly.
- Store food at appropriate temperatures.
- Use clean utensils.
- Avoid consuming spoiled food.

---

# 9.7.7 Eating Disorders

Eating disorders involve unhealthy patterns of eating and attitudes towards food.

Examples include:

### Anorexia nervosa

A serious eating disorder involving severe restriction of food intake and an unhealthy disturbance in body image.

It can result in severe nutrient and energy deficiency.

### Bulimia nervosa

A serious eating disorder involving repeated episodes of excessive food intake followed by compensatory behaviours.

Both conditions can cause serious health problems and require professional support.

---

# IMPORTANT DIFFERENCES

## Digestion vs Absorption vs Assimilation vs Defaecation

### Digestion

Large food molecules are broken down into smaller soluble molecules.

### Absorption

Digested nutrients pass through the intestinal wall into the blood or lymph.

### Assimilation

Absorbed nutrients are used or incorporated by body cells.

### Defaecation

Faeces are expelled from the body through the anus.

# COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS

### Misconception 1
"Bile is a digestive enzyme."

**Correction:** Bile contains no digestive enzymes. It emulsifies fats and helps neutralise acidic chyme.

### Misconception 2
"Absorption and assimilation are the same."

**Correction:** Absorption is the movement of digested nutrients into blood or lymph. Assimilation is the use of those nutrients by body cells.

### Misconception 3
"Proteins are absorbed as proteins."

**Correction:** Proteins are digested into smaller molecules, mainly amino acids, before absorption.

### Misconception 4
"Fats are digested by bile."

**Correction:** Bile emulsifies fats. Lipase chemically digests lipids.

### Misconception 5
"The stomach is the main site of nutrient absorption."

**Correction:** Most absorption of digested nutrients occurs in the small intestine.

### Misconception 6
"All energy from food comes from carbohydrates."

**Correction:** Carbohydrates, proteins and lipids can all provide energy.

### Misconception 7
"One gram of fat provides the same energy as one gram of carbohydrate."

**Correction:** Fat provides approximately 39 kJ/g, while carbohydrate provides approximately 17 kJ/g.

# SPM-STYLE PRACTICE QUESTIONS

## Question 1 — 3 marks

Explain why digestion is necessary in humans.

### Answer

Food contains large, complex and insoluble molecules. These molecules cannot be absorbed efficiently through the intestinal wall. Digestion breaks them down into smaller, simpler and soluble molecules that can be absorbed.

---

## Question 2 — 4 marks

Explain how bile helps in the digestion of lipids.

### Answer

Bile emulsifies large fat droplets into many smaller droplets. This increases the surface area of the fat exposed to lipase. Therefore, lipase can digest lipids more efficiently.

---

## Question 3 — 4 marks

Explain four adaptations of a villus for efficient absorption.

### Answer

The villus has a large surface area due to numerous villi and microvilli. Its epithelium is thin, providing a short diffusion distance. It contains many blood capillaries that rapidly transport absorbed nutrients. It also contains a lacteal for absorption and transport of lipid products.

---

## Question 4 — 4 marks

Differentiate between absorption and assimilation.

### Answer

Absorption is the process by which digested nutrients pass through the intestinal wall into the blood or lymph. Assimilation is the process by which absorbed nutrients are incorporated into body cells and used for functions such as energy production, growth and tissue repair.

---

## Question 5 — 3 marks

Explain how fibre helps prevent constipation.

### Answer

Fibre is largely indigestible and adds bulk to faeces. This stimulates movement of the alimentary canal and helps faeces move through the large intestine. Therefore, fibre helps facilitate defaecation and reduce constipation.

---

# ENERGY-VALUE QUESTIONS

## Question 6 — 4 marks

A food contains:

- 25 g carbohydrate
- 10 g protein
- 5 g lipid

Calculate the total energy provided.

### Answer

Carbohydrate:

25 × 17 = 425 kJ

Protein:

10 × 17 = 170 kJ

Lipid:

5 × 39 = 195 kJ

Total:

425 + 170 + 195

= **790 kJ**

---

## Question 7 — 3 marks

A food provides 1,170 kJ of energy entirely from lipid. Calculate the mass of lipid.

### Answer

Mass = Energy ÷ energy value per gram

Mass = 1,170 ÷ 39

= **30 g**

---

## Question 8 — 5 marks

A meal contains 40 g carbohydrate, 20 g protein and 10 g lipid.

Calculate:

a) Energy from carbohydrate  
b) Energy from protein  
c) Energy from lipid  
d) Total energy

### Answer

a) 40 × 17 = **680 kJ**

b) 20 × 17 = **340 kJ**

c) 10 × 39 = **390 kJ**

d) 680 + 340 + 390 = **1,410 kJ**

# HIGH-VALUE EXAM CONNECTIONS

### Why does the small intestine have villi?

Villi increase the surface area available for absorption.

### Why are villi supplied with many blood capillaries?

Blood capillaries rapidly transport absorbed nutrients away, helping maintain concentration gradients.

### Why is the villus epithelium thin?

A thin epithelium provides a short diffusion distance, allowing rapid absorption.

### Why is a lacteal present in each villus?

The lacteal absorbs and transports products of lipid digestion.

### Why is bile important even though it contains no enzyme?

It emulsifies fats and helps provide a suitable alkaline environment for enzymes in the small intestine.

### Why does excessive energy intake lead to obesity?

If energy intake repeatedly exceeds energy expenditure, excess energy can be stored in the body, mainly as fat.
