3.1 STRUCTURE OF PLASMA MEMBRANE
============================================================

1. INTRODUCTION

The plasma membrane is a thin boundary that surrounds the cytoplasm of a cell.

It separates the internal contents of the cell from its external environment.

Its main function is to control the movement of substances into and out of the cell.

The plasma membrane is selectively permeable. This means that it allows certain substances to pass through more easily than others.

This property helps the cell maintain a stable internal environment.

2. FLUID MOSAIC MODEL

The structure of the plasma membrane is explained using the fluid mosaic model.

The model describes the plasma membrane as a flexible phospholipid bilayer containing different proteins and other molecules.

The word FLUID means that certain components of the membrane can move sideways within the bilayer.

The word MOSAIC describes the arrangement of different components, such as proteins, within the phospholipid bilayer.

3. PHOSPHOLIPID BILAYER

The basic structure of the plasma membrane consists of two layers of phospholipid molecules.

Each phospholipid molecule consists of:

A. Hydrophilic phosphate head
- Attracted to water.
- Faces the watery environment outside the cell.
- Also faces the cytoplasm inside the cell.

B. Hydrophobic fatty acid tails
- Repelled by or avoid water.
- Face towards one another in the middle of the membrane.
- Form a hydrophobic region inside the membrane.

The arrangement of the phospholipids forms a barrier that controls the movement of substances across the membrane.

Many charged particles and large water-soluble substances cannot easily pass directly through the hydrophobic interior.

4. MEMBRANE PROTEINS

Proteins are found on the surface of or embedded within the phospholipid bilayer.

They perform several important functions.

A. Channel proteins
- Form passages through the membrane.
- Allow certain substances, such as specific ions, to cross.

B. Carrier proteins
- Bind to particular substances.
- Change shape to help move substances across the membrane.

C. Receptor proteins
- Receive signals from outside the cell.
- Help cells respond to their environment.

D. Recognition proteins
- Help cells recognise one another.
- Important in interactions between cells.

5. CHOLESTEROL

Cholesterol is found between phospholipid molecules in many animal cell membranes.

Functions:
- Helps regulate membrane fluidity.
- Contributes to membrane stability.
- Helps maintain suitable membrane properties.

6. GLYCOPROTEINS AND GLYCOLIPIDS

Glycoproteins are proteins with carbohydrate chains attached.

Glycolipids are lipids with carbohydrate chains attached.

Functions include:
- Cell recognition.
- Cell communication.
- Interactions between cells.

7. IMPORTANCE OF THE PLASMA MEMBRANE

The plasma membrane:
- Separates the cell from its surroundings.
- Controls the entry of nutrients.
- Regulates the removal of waste products.
- Helps maintain suitable internal conditions.
- Allows cells to receive signals.
- Helps cells recognise other cells.

KEY TERMS

Plasma membrane:
A selectively permeable boundary surrounding a cell.

Selectively permeable:
Allows certain substances to pass through more readily than others.

Hydrophilic:
Attracted to water.

Hydrophobic:
Repelled by or avoids water.

Phospholipid bilayer:
Two layers of phospholipid molecules forming the basic structure of the plasma membrane.

Fluid mosaic model:
A model describing the plasma membrane as a flexible phospholipid bilayer containing different components.

3.2 CONCEPT OF MOVEMENT OF SUBSTANCES ACROSS A PLASMA MEMBRANE
============================================================

1. WHY DO SUBSTANCES MOVE ACROSS THE MEMBRANE?

Cells need different substances to survive and carry out their functions.

Examples:
- Oxygen is required for aerobic respiration.
- Nutrients are required for growth and energy.
- Water is required for cellular processes.
- Waste products must be removed from cells.

The plasma membrane regulates the movement of these substances.

There are two main categories of transport:

A. Passive transport
B. Active transport

2. CONCENTRATION GRADIENT

A concentration gradient is a difference in the concentration of a substance between two regions.

Movement down a concentration gradient means movement from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration.

Movement against a concentration gradient means movement from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration.

3. PASSIVE TRANSPORT

Passive transport is the movement of substances across a membrane without the cell using energy from ATP to power the transport.

Types:
- Simple diffusion.
- Facilitated diffusion.
- Osmosis.

A. SIMPLE DIFFUSION

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, down the concentration gradient, due to random particle movement.

Examples:

Oxygen:
Oxygen can diffuse into cells when its concentration is higher outside the cells than inside.

Carbon dioxide:
Carbon dioxide can diffuse out of cells when its concentration is higher inside than outside.

Characteristics:
- Does not require ATP.
- Movement occurs down the concentration gradient.
- Does not require transport proteins.
- Particles continue moving randomly in both directions.

At equilibrium, particles still move, but there is no net movement in one direction.

B. FACILITATED DIFFUSION

Facilitated diffusion is the passive movement of substances across a membrane down their concentration gradient with the assistance of membrane proteins.

Some substances cannot easily pass through the hydrophobic interior of the phospholipid bilayer.

Channel proteins:
- Form passages across the membrane.
- Allow particular substances to pass.

Carrier proteins:
- Bind to particular substances.
- Change shape to transport them across the membrane.

Characteristics:
- Does not require ATP.
- Movement occurs down the concentration gradient.
- Requires membrane proteins.
- Transport proteins are selective.

C. OSMOSIS

Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules across a selectively permeable membrane from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential.

In simple terms, water moves from a more dilute solution towards a more concentrated solution when the dissolved solute cannot freely cross the membrane.

Characteristics:
- Involves water molecules.
- Requires a selectively permeable membrane.
- Does not require ATP.
- Water molecules move in both directions, but there is a net movement towards lower water potential.

4. ACTIVE TRANSPORT

Active transport is the movement of substances across a membrane against their concentration gradient, from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration.

It requires energy from ATP and transport proteins.

Characteristics:
- Requires ATP.
- Requires specific transport proteins.
- Moves substances against their concentration gradient.
- Allows cells to accumulate substances even when their concentration is already higher inside the cell.

EXAMPLE:

Root hair cells may absorb mineral ions from the soil even when the concentration of those ions is lower in the soil than inside the root hair cells.

This process requires active transport.

5. COMPARISON OF TRANSPORT PROCESSES

SIMPLE DIFFUSION:
- Moves particles down a concentration gradient.
- Does not require transport proteins.
- Does not require ATP.

FACILITATED DIFFUSION:
- Moves substances down a concentration gradient.
- Requires channel or carrier proteins.
- Does not require ATP.

OSMOSIS:
- Involves water molecules.
- Moves from higher water potential to lower water potential.
- Requires a selectively permeable membrane.
- Does not require ATP.

ACTIVE TRANSPORT:
- Moves substances against a concentration gradient.
- Requires transport proteins.
- Requires energy from ATP.

6. FACTORS AFFECTING THE RATE OF DIFFUSION

A. Concentration difference
A larger concentration difference generally increases the rate of net diffusion.

B. Temperature
Higher temperature increases the kinetic energy of particles and can increase the rate of diffusion, provided the temperature does not damage the cells.

C. Surface area
A larger surface area allows more substances to cross the membrane at the same time.

D. Diffusion distance
A shorter diffusion distance generally increases the rate of diffusion.

E. Particle properties
The size and charge of particles affect their ability to cross the membrane.

F. Transport proteins
The availability of suitable channel and carrier proteins affects facilitated diffusion and active transport.

7. COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS

- Diffusion and osmosis are not the same.
- Osmosis specifically involves water molecules.
- Facilitated diffusion uses membrane proteins but does not require ATP.
- Active transport requires ATP.
- At equilibrium, particles do not stop moving.

3.3 MOVEMENT OF SUBSTANCES ACROSS A PLASMA MEMBRANE IN LIVING ORGANISMS
============================================================

1. MOVEMENT OF OXYGEN AND CARBON DIOXIDE

Oxygen and carbon dioxide can move across cell membranes through diffusion.

A. Oxygen movement

Cells require oxygen for aerobic respiration.

During respiration, oxygen is used by the cells.

As a result, the oxygen concentration inside a respiring cell may become lower than the concentration outside.

Oxygen then diffuses into the cell down its concentration gradient.

B. Carbon dioxide movement

Carbon dioxide is produced during cellular respiration.

Its concentration may become higher inside the cell than outside.

Carbon dioxide then diffuses out of the cell down its concentration gradient.

The direction of diffusion always depends on the concentration gradient.

2. OSMOSIS IN PLANT CELLS

Plant cells have a cell wall outside the plasma membrane.

The cell wall provides support and resists excessive expansion when water enters the cell.

A. PLANT CELL IN A DILUTE SOLUTION

When a plant cell is placed in a dilute solution:

1. The solution outside has a higher water potential than the cell sap.
2. Water enters the cell by osmosis.
3. The vacuole becomes larger.
4. The cytoplasm and plasma membrane are pushed towards the cell wall.
5. The cell becomes turgid.

A turgid cell is firm because it contains water and develops turgor pressure.

The cell wall prevents excessive expansion and helps prevent the cell from bursting.

B. PLANT CELL IN A CONCENTRATED SOLUTION

When a plant cell is placed in a concentrated solution:

1. The solution outside has a lower water potential than the cell sap.
2. Water leaves the cell by osmosis.
3. The vacuole becomes smaller.
4. The cytoplasm shrinks.
5. The cell becomes flaccid.

If sufficient water leaves the cell, the plasma membrane and cytoplasm may pull away from the cell wall.

This condition is called plasmolysis.

A cell that has undergone this process is described as plasmolysed.

C. PLANT CELL IN A SOLUTION WITH SIMILAR WATER POTENTIAL

- Water moves in both directions.
- There is no net movement of water.
- The cell does not gain or lose water overall.

3. OSMOSIS IN ANIMAL CELLS

Animal cells do not have a cell wall.

Therefore, they respond differently from plant cells when placed in solutions with different water potentials.

A. ANIMAL CELL IN A DILUTE SOLUTION

1. The solution outside has a higher water potential than the cytoplasm.
2. Water enters the cell by osmosis.
3. The cell swells.
4. The cell may burst if too much water enters.

B. ANIMAL CELL IN A CONCENTRATED SOLUTION

1. The solution outside has a lower water potential than the cytoplasm.
2. Water leaves the cell by osmosis.
3. The cell shrinks.

C. ANIMAL CELL IN AN ISOTONIC SOLUTION

- The solution has a similar effective solute concentration to the cell.
- There is no net movement of water.
- The cell generally maintains its normal size.

4. COMPARISON OF PLANT AND ANIMAL CELLS

DILUTE SOLUTION

Plant cell:
- Water enters by osmosis.
- Cell becomes turgid.
- Cell wall resists excessive expansion.

Animal cell:
- Water enters by osmosis.
- Cell swells.
- Cell may burst.

CONCENTRATED SOLUTION

Plant cell:
- Water leaves by osmosis.
- Cell becomes flaccid.
- May undergo plasmolysis.

Animal cell:
- Water leaves by osmosis.
- Cell shrinks.

SIMILAR WATER POTENTIAL

Plant cell:
- No net movement of water.
- May become flaccid rather than fully turgid.

Animal cell:
- No net movement of water.
- Generally maintains normal size.

5. ABSORPTION OF WATER AND MINERAL IONS BY ROOT HAIR CELLS

Root hair cells absorb water and mineral ions from the soil.

A. Water absorption

Water enters root hair cells by osmosis when the water potential in the soil solution is higher than that inside the cell.

Root hairs provide a large surface area for water absorption.

B. Mineral ion absorption

Mineral ions may enter root hair cells by active transport when their concentration is lower in the soil than inside the cells.

Active transport requires:
- ATP.
- Transport proteins.
- Movement against the concentration gradient.

IMPORTANT DIFFERENCE:

Water enters by osmosis.

Mineral ions may enter by active transport.

3.4 MOVEMENT OF SUBSTANCES ACROSS A PLASMA MEMBRANE AND ITS APPLICATION IN DAILY LIFE
============================================================

1. FOOD PRESERVATION USING SALT AND SUGAR

Concentrated salt and sugar solutions are used to preserve certain foods.

How it works:

1. A concentrated salt or sugar solution surrounds microorganisms.
2. The external solution has a lower water potential than the microorganisms' cells.
3. Water moves out of the microbial cells by osmosis.
4. The loss of water makes it difficult for many microorganisms to grow and carry out normal life processes.
5. Food spoilage is slowed.

Examples:
- Salted foods.
- Sugared fruits.
- Certain preserved foods.

This process does not necessarily kill every microorganism immediately.

Food preservation also depends on concentration, cleanliness, temperature and storage conditions.

2. INTRAVENOUS FLUIDS

Intravenous fluids are used in medical care.

Their concentration must be selected carefully by qualified healthcare professionals.

A. Fluid that is much more dilute than blood plasma

- Water may enter red blood cells by osmosis.
- Red blood cells swell.
- They may burst if excessive water enters.

B. Fluid that is much more concentrated than blood plasma

- Water leaves red blood cells by osmosis.
- Red blood cells shrink.

C. Isotonic fluid

- Has a similar effective solute concentration to blood plasma.
- Reduces net water movement into or out of red blood cells.
- Helps maintain normal cell volume.

This section explains the biological principle only. Medical fluids must be selected and administered by qualified healthcare professionals.

3. WATER ABSORPTION BY PLANT ROOTS

Plant roots absorb water from the soil.

- Water enters root hair cells by osmosis.
- Root hairs provide a large surface area.
- Water moves according to the water potential gradient.
- Mineral ions may be absorbed through active transport.

4. PICKLING AND FOOD PRESERVATION

Some pickling methods use concentrated salt solutions.

Salt can draw water out of microorganisms through osmosis.

This can slow the growth of many microorganisms and help preserve food.

Other factors, such as acidity and storage conditions, also contribute to preservation.

5. DIALYSIS: GENERAL APPLICATION

Dialysis uses a selectively permeable membrane to separate certain small dissolved substances from larger components in a fluid.

The membrane allows some substances to pass while restricting others.

The exact process depends on the dialysis system.

EXAM ANSWER GUIDANCE
============================================================

1. DEFINE DIFFUSION

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, down the concentration gradient.

2. DEFINE OSMOSIS

Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules across a selectively permeable membrane from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential.

3. DEFINE ACTIVE TRANSPORT

Active transport is the movement of substances across a membrane against their concentration gradient, using energy from ATP and transport proteins.

4. WHY DOES FACILITATED DIFFUSION NOT REQUIRE ATP?

Substances move down their concentration gradient. Channel or carrier proteins help the substances cross the membrane, but ATP is not required.

5. WHY DO ROOT HAIR CELLS USE ACTIVE TRANSPORT?

The concentration of some mineral ions in the soil may be lower than inside the root hair cell.

The ions must move against the concentration gradient.

Therefore, the cell uses active transport, which requires ATP and transport proteins.

6. WHAT HAPPENS TO A PLANT CELL IN A CONCENTRATED SOLUTION?

- The external solution has a lower water potential.
- Water leaves the cell by osmosis.
- The vacuole and cytoplasm shrink.
- The cell becomes flaccid.
- If sufficient water is lost, the plasma membrane pulls away from the cell wall.
- The cell becomes plasmolysed.

7. WHY DOES A PLANT CELL USUALLY NOT BURST IN A DILUTE SOLUTION?

Water enters the cell by osmosis.

The cell becomes turgid.

The rigid cell wall resists further expansion and helps prevent the cell from bursting.

8. COMPARE PLANT AND ANIMAL CELLS IN A DILUTE SOLUTION

Both cells gain water by osmosis.

The plant cell becomes turgid because its cell wall resists expansion.

The animal cell swells and may burst because it does not have a cell wall.

QUESTION 7:
Explain how concentrated salt can help preserve food.

ANSWER:
The concentrated salt solution has a lower water potential than the microbial cells.
