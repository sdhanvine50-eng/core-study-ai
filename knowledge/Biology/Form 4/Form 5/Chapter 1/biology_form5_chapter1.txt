# CHAPTER 1: ORGANISATION OF PLANT TISSUES AND GROWTH

## 1.1 ORGANISATION OF PLANT TISSUES

### 1.1.1 Organisation in Plants

Plants are multicellular organisms made up of different types of cells. These cells are organised into tissues and organs that work together to carry out life processes.

The levels of organisation in plants are:

**Cell → Tissue → Organ → System → Organism**

- **Cell:** The basic structural and functional unit of a living organism.
- **Tissue:** A group of similar cells that work together to perform a specific function.
- **Organ:** A structure made up of different tissues working together.
- **System:** A group of organs that work together to perform particular functions.
- **Organism:** A complete living individual.

Examples:

- Cells form tissues such as xylem and phloem.
- Different tissues form organs such as roots, stems and leaves.
- Roots, stems and leaves work together as part of the plant shoot and root systems.

### 1.1.2 Types of Plant Tissues

Plant tissues can be classified into two main groups:

1. Meristematic tissue
2. Permanent tissue

#### A. Meristematic Tissue

Meristematic tissue consists of actively dividing cells. These cells are responsible for producing new cells that allow plants to grow.

Characteristics:

- Cells are small and generally have thin cell walls.
- Cells have dense cytoplasm.
- Cells contain a prominent nucleus.
- Vacuoles are absent or very small.
- Cells are closely packed with little or no intercellular space.
- Cells undergo mitosis actively.

Functions:

- Produces new cells.
- Increases the length of roots and shoots.
- Increases the thickness of stems and roots in plants with lateral meristems.
- Replaces damaged or old cells in certain growing regions.

#### B. Permanent Tissue

Permanent tissues are formed when meristematic cells differentiate and become specialised to perform particular functions.

Differentiation is the process by which an unspecialised cell develops into a specialised cell with a specific structure and function.

Permanent tissues include simple permanent tissues and complex permanent tissues.

### 1.1.3 Simple Permanent Tissues

Simple permanent tissues consist mainly of one type of cell.

**1. Parenchyma**

Characteristics:

- Living cells.
- Thin cell walls.
- Usually have large vacuoles.
- Often have intercellular spaces.
- Generally have a flexible structure.

Functions:

- Stores food such as starch.
- Stores water.
- Performs photosynthesis when chloroplasts are present.
- Helps with gas exchange.
- Can contribute to tissue repair and regeneration.

Examples:

- Cortex and pith of stems and roots.
- Mesophyll tissue in leaves.
- Storage tissues in fruits and other plant organs.

**2. Collenchyma**

Characteristics:

- Living cells.
- Cell walls are unevenly thickened, especially at the corners.
- Cell walls contain cellulose and pectin.
- Cells provide flexible support.

Functions:

- Supports young stems and leaves.
- Provides flexibility.
- Allows growing plant parts to bend without breaking easily.

**3. Sclerenchyma**

Characteristics:

- Cells usually become dead at maturity.
- Cell walls are thick and strengthened with lignin.
- Have a strong and rigid structure.
- Include fibres and sclereids.

Functions:

- Provides mechanical strength.
- Supports mature plant parts.
- Protects certain plant structures.

### Comparison of Simple Permanent Tissues

| Feature              | Parenchyma                 | Collenchyma        | Sclerenchyma             |
| -------------------- | -------------------------- | ------------------ | ------------------------ |
| Cell status          | Living                     | Living             | Usually dead at maturity |
| Cell wall            | Thin                       | Unevenly thickened | Thick and lignified      |
| Main function        | Storage and photosynthesis | Flexible support   | Rigid support            |
| Intercellular spaces | Often present              | Usually little     | Usually absent           |

**Exam tip:** Do not confuse collenchyma and sclerenchyma. Collenchyma provides flexible support in growing regions, whereas sclerenchyma provides strong, rigid support.

### 1.1.4 Complex Permanent Tissues

Complex permanent tissues consist of different types of cells working together to perform a common function.

The two main complex permanent tissues are xylem and phloem.

#### A. Xylem

Xylem transports water and mineral salts from the roots to other parts of the plant.

Main components:

**1. Vessel elements**

- Dead cells at maturity.
- Arranged end to end.
- End walls are perforated or absent, forming continuous vessels.
- Walls are strengthened with lignin.
- Transport water and mineral salts.

**2. Tracheids**

- Dead cells at maturity.
- Long, narrow cells with tapering ends.
- Have thick lignified walls.
- Water moves between cells through pits.
- Provide support and transport water.

**3. Xylem fibres**

- Dead cells.
- Thick, lignified walls.
- Provide mechanical support.

**4. Xylem parenchyma**

- Living cells.
- Store food and assist in lateral transport of substances.

Functions of xylem:

- Transports water and mineral salts.
- Provides mechanical support due to lignified walls.

#### B. Phloem

Phloem transports organic substances, especially sucrose and amino acids, from sources to sinks. This movement is known as translocation.

Main components:

**1. Sieve tube elements**

- Living cells, but have no nucleus at maturity.
- Arranged end to end.
- End walls form sieve plates.
- Transport dissolved organic substances.

**2. Companion cells**

- Living cells with a nucleus.
- Closely associated with sieve tube elements.
- Provide metabolic support.
- Help control the movement of substances into and out of sieve tubes.

**3. Phloem fibres**

- Usually dead at maturity.
- Have thick walls.
- Provide mechanical support.

**4. Phloem parenchyma**

- Living cells.
- Function in storage and transport of substances.

### Comparison Between Xylem and Phloem

| Feature                      | Xylem                         | Phloem                                                |
| ---------------------------- | ----------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Main substances              | Water and mineral salts       | Sucrose and other organic substances                  |
| Main function                | Water transport               | Translocation                                         |
| Main conducting cells        | Vessel elements and tracheids | Sieve tube elements                                   |
| Conducting cells at maturity | Dead                          | Living, but sieve tube elements lack nuclei           |
| Direction                    | Mainly upwards                | From sources to sinks; can be in different directions |

**Important:** Phloem transport is not simply always upwards or always downwards. It depends on the locations of sources and sinks.

### 1.1.5 Organisation of Plant Organs

A plant has organs that contain different tissues.

**Roots**

- Anchor the plant.
- Absorb water and mineral salts.
- Store food in some plants.
- Contain vascular tissues for transport.

**Stems**

- Support leaves, flowers and fruits.
- Transport water, mineral salts and organic substances.
- May store food or water.

**Leaves**

- Main site of photosynthesis.
- Allow gaseous exchange through stomata.
- Control water loss through transpiration.

### 1.1.6 Vascular Bundles

Vascular bundles contain xylem and phloem.

Their arrangement differs between plant organs and plant groups.

- In many dicot stems, vascular bundles are arranged in a ring.
- In many monocot stems, vascular bundles are scattered throughout the ground tissue.
- In roots, xylem and phloem have a different arrangement from stems.

The vascular tissues form a transport network connecting roots, stems and leaves.

## 1.2 MERISTEM TISSUE AND GROWTH

### 1.2.1 Plant Growth

Growth is a permanent and irreversible increase in the size, volume, dry mass or number of cells of an organism.

In plants, growth occurs mainly in regions containing meristematic tissue.

Plant growth involves three major processes:

1. Cell division
2. Cell elongation
3. Cell differentiation

### 1.2.2 Cell Division

Cell division occurs through mitosis in meristematic regions.

During mitosis:

- One parent cell divides.
- Two daughter cells are produced.
- The daughter cells normally have the same chromosome number as the parent cell.
- The new cells increase the number of cells in the growing region.

Cell division supplies new cells for plant growth.

### 1.2.3 Cell Elongation

After cell division, some new cells increase in length or size.

Cell elongation occurs through:

- Enlargement of the vacuole.
- Absorption of water.
- Expansion of the cell.
- Loosening and extension of the cell wall.

Cell elongation contributes to an increase in the length of roots and shoots.

### 1.2.4 Cell Differentiation

New cells become specialised to perform particular functions.

Examples:

- Some cells differentiate into xylem vessels for water transport.
- Some cells become phloem sieve tube elements for translocation.
- Some cells develop into epidermal cells for protection.

Differentiation allows different tissues to perform specialised functions.

### 1.2.5 Types of Meristematic Tissue

Meristematic tissues are classified according to their location.

#### A. Apical Meristem

\<figure>\</figure>

Location:

- At the tips of roots.
- At the tips of shoots.

Functions:

- Responsible for primary growth.
- Increases the length of roots and shoots.
- Produces primary tissues.

Primary growth is the increase in the length of a plant due to the activity of apical meristems.

Examples:

- A young root growing deeper into the soil.
- A shoot growing upwards towards light.

#### B. Lateral Meristem

Location:

- Along the sides of stems and roots.

Examples:

- Vascular cambium.
- Cork cambium.

Functions:

- Responsible for secondary growth.
- Increases the diameter or thickness of stems and roots.
- Produces secondary tissues.

Secondary growth is the increase in the thickness of stems and roots due to the activity of lateral meristems.

**Vascular cambium**

- Produces secondary xylem towards the inside.
- Produces secondary phloem towards the outside.
- Contributes to the increase in stem and root diameter.

**Cork cambium**

- Produces cork cells that form part of the protective outer covering.
- Helps replace the epidermis as the plant organ increases in thickness.

Secondary growth is especially prominent in many dicot plants and gymnosperms. Most monocots do not show typical secondary growth.

#### C. Intercalary Meristem

Location:

- At the bases of leaves.
- At the nodes or internodes of certain plants, especially grasses.

Functions:

- Allows rapid elongation of plant parts.
- Helps grasses regrow after being cut or grazed.

Example:

- Grass can continue growing from the base even when its upper parts have been cut.

### Comparison of Meristems

| Feature     | Apical              | Lateral                  | Intercalary         |
| ----------- | ------------------- | ------------------------ | ------------------- |
| Location    | Root and shoot tips | Sides of stems and roots | Leaf bases or nodes |
| Growth      | Primary             | Secondary                | Elongation          |
| Main effect | Increases length    | Increases thickness      | Extends plant parts |
| Example     | Root tip            | Vascular cambium         | Grass               |

### 1.2.6 Primary Growth

Primary growth increases the length of roots and shoots.

In a root tip, the regions can be described in order from the tip upwards:

**1. Root cap**

- Protects the delicate root tip.
- Helps the root move through the soil.

**2. Region of cell division**

- Contains the apical meristem.
- Cells divide actively by mitosis.

**3. Region of cell elongation**

- Newly formed cells increase in length.
- Contributes significantly to root lengthening.

**4. Region of cell differentiation**

- Cells become specialised.
- Different permanent tissues develop.
- Root hair cells may be present in this region.

### 1.2.7 Secondary Growth

Secondary growth increases the diameter of stems and roots.

In a typical woody dicot stem:

1. Vascular cambium becomes active.
2. It divides and produces secondary xylem towards the inside.
3. It produces secondary phloem towards the outside.
4. Accumulation of secondary xylem contributes to the increase in thickness.
5. Cork cambium produces protective cork tissue.

Secondary xylem forms much of the wood in woody plants.

Annual rings may form in trees growing in environments with seasonal changes. Differences in the growth of xylem during different seasons can produce visible rings.

**Exam tip:** Primary growth increases length. Secondary growth increases thickness. Do not reverse these definitions.

### 1.2.8 Factors Affecting Plant Growth

Plant growth is affected by internal and external factors.

**Internal factors**

- Genetic information.
- Plant hormones.
- Age and developmental stage of the plant.

**External factors**

- Light intensity and duration.
- Water availability.
- Mineral availability.
- Temperature.
- Oxygen availability.
- Carbon dioxide availability for photosynthesis.

These factors influence processes such as photosynthesis, respiration, cell division and cell expansion.

### 1.2.9 Plant Hormones and Growth

Plant hormones are chemical substances produced in small quantities that regulate plant growth and development.

Examples include:

**Auxins**

- Promote cell elongation in many plant tissues.
- Involved in phototropism and gravitropism.
- Help regulate apical dominance.

**Gibberellins**

- Promote stem elongation.
- Can stimulate seed germination.
- May promote flowering or fruit development in certain plants.

**Cytokinins**

- Promote cell division.
- Help regulate shoot development.
- Can delay leaf ageing in some situations.

**Abscisic acid (ABA)**

- Helps plants respond to water stress.
- Promotes stomatal closure during water shortage.
- Can maintain seed dormancy.

**Ethylene**

- A gaseous plant hormone.
- Promotes fruit ripening.
- Is involved in leaf and fruit ageing and abscission.

## 1.3 GROWTH CURVES

### 1.3.1 Meaning of a Growth Curve

A growth curve is a graph that shows changes in the growth of an organism over a period of time.

Growth may be measured using:

- Height.
- Length.
- Dry mass.
- Fresh mass.
- Volume.
- Number of cells.

The choice of measurement depends on the organism and the investigation.

### 1.3.2 Sigmoid Growth Curve

A sigmoid growth curve is an S-shaped curve commonly associated with the growth of many plants and other organisms under suitable conditions.

It can be divided into several phases.

**1. Lag phase**

- Growth is slow at first.
- Cells become active and adjust to conditions.
- There may be limited measurable increase in size.

**2. Exponential phase**

- Growth occurs rapidly.
- Cells divide and expand actively.
- The growth rate increases.

**3. Stationary phase**

- Growth rate slows.
- The organism approaches its maximum size under the given conditions.
- Factors such as nutrients, space or other environmental limitations may restrict further growth.

The exact shape and duration of each phase depend on the organism and conditions.

### 1.3.3 Discontinuous Growth in Arthropods

Arthropods, such as insects and crustaceans, have an exoskeleton.

The exoskeleton is rigid and does not expand continuously with the animal's body.

Therefore, arthropods grow through a process called moulting or ecdysis.

Moulting is the process of shedding the old exoskeleton and forming a new one.

Stages:

1. The animal prepares to shed its old exoskeleton.
2. The old exoskeleton splits.
3. The animal emerges from the old exoskeleton.
4. The new exoskeleton is initially soft.
5. The animal's body expands.
6. The new exoskeleton hardens.

Growth occurs in steps rather than as a continuous increase in body size.

A graph of body size against time may show:

- A period with little change in measurable external size.
- A sudden increase after moulting.
- Another period of relatively stable size before the next moult.

### 1.3.4 Growth Curves in Plants and Arthropods

| Feature        | Typical plant growth         | Arthropod growth                      |
| -------------- | ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| Growth pattern | Often continuous             | Discontinuous in measurable body size |
| Main reason    | Meristem activity            | Exoskeleton limits expansion          |
| Growth process | Cell division and elongation | Growth followed by moulting           |
| Graph          | Often sigmoid                | Step-like pattern                     |

**Important:** Not every plant or organism follows an identical growth curve. Growth patterns depend on species, developmental stage and environmental conditions.

### 1.3.5 Measuring Plant Growth

Plant growth can be investigated by measuring:

- Height over time.
- Number of leaves.
- Root length.
- Fresh mass.
- Dry mass.

Dry mass is often useful because fresh mass can change due to differences in water content.

A fair test should:

- Use similar plants.
- Keep environmental conditions consistent.
- Measure at regular time intervals.
- Use suitable measuring equipment.
- Repeat measurements where possible.

### 1.3.6 Interpreting Growth Curves

When interpreting a growth graph:

1. Read the axes and units.
2. Identify the period being compared.
3. Determine whether growth is increasing, decreasing or remaining stable.
4. Compare the gradients to identify changes in growth rate.
5. Support conclusions using data from the graph.

The gradient of a growth curve represents the rate of growth.

A steeper positive gradient indicates a faster rate of growth.

A horizontal section indicates little or no measurable increase during that interval.

# COMMON EXAM MISTAKES

1. Saying that xylem transports food. Xylem mainly transports water and mineral salts.
2. Saying that phloem transports only sucrose. It also transports other organic substances.
3. Confusing collenchyma with sclerenchyma.
4. Saying that primary growth increases thickness. Primary growth increases length.
5. Saying that secondary growth increases length. Secondary growth increases thickness.
6. Confusing cell division with cell elongation.
7. Saying that arthropods grow continuously without moulting.
8. Forgetting that the new exoskeleton is initially soft after moulting.
9. Assuming that all plant species have the same pattern of secondary growth.
10. Describing a growth curve without referring to the axes or data.

# SPM-STYLE PRACTICE QUESTIONS

## Section A: Structured Questions

**Question 1**

(a) State the two main groups of plant tissues. [2 marks]

(b) Explain the differences between parenchyma, collenchyma and sclerenchyma. [6 marks]

(c) Explain why xylem is important to a plant. [3 marks]

**Suggested answers:**

(a)

- Meristematic tissue.
- Permanent tissue.

(b)

- Parenchyma consists of living cells with thin cell walls and functions in storage and photosynthesis.
- Collenchyma consists of living cells with unevenly thickened walls and provides flexible support.
- Sclerenchyma cells are usually dead at maturity, have thick lignified walls and provide rigid support.

(c)

- Xylem transports water from the roots to other plant parts.
- It transports dissolved mineral salts.
- Its lignified walls provide mechanical support.

## Section B: Application Questions

**Question 2**

A student observes that the stem of a young plant increases in length over several weeks.

(a) Name the meristem responsible for this growth. [1 mark]

(b) Explain how this meristem increases the length of the plant. [3 marks]

**Suggested answers:**

(a) Apical meristem.

(b)

- Cells in the apical meristem divide by mitosis.
- New cells are produced.
- The cells elongate and differentiate, increasing the length of the shoot.

**Question 3**

A grass plant is cut close to the ground. After several days, new leaves grow from its base.

(a) Name the meristem responsible. [1 mark]

(b) Explain its role. [2 marks]

**Suggested answers:**

(a) Intercalary meristem.

(b)

- It contains actively dividing cells near the base of the leaves or nodes.
- These cells divide and elongate, allowing the grass to regrow.

## Section C: Growth Curves

**Question 4**

(a) What is a growth curve? [2 marks]

(b) Describe the main phases of a sigmoid growth curve. [6 marks]

(c) Explain why arthropods show discontinuous growth. [4 marks]

**Suggested answers:**

(a)

- A graph showing changes in growth.
- The changes are measured over a period of time.

(b)

- Lag phase: growth is initially slow.
- Exponential phase: growth occurs rapidly.
- Stationary phase: growth rate slows as the organism approaches its maximum size under the given conditions.

(c)

- Arthropods have a rigid exoskeleton.
- The exoskeleton cannot expand continuously with the growing body.
- The animal sheds its old exoskeleton through moulting.
- Its body expands before the new exoskeleton hardens.
