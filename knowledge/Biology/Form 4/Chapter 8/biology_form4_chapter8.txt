# CHAPTER 8: RESPIRATORY SYSTEMS IN HUMANS AND ANIMALS

## 8.1 TYPES OF RESPIRATORY SYSTEM

### 8.1.1 The Need for Respiratory Systems

All living cells require oxygen for aerobic respiration to release energy in the form of ATP. Carbon dioxide is produced as a waste product of aerobic respiration and must be removed from cells.

Small organisms with a high surface area to volume ratio can exchange gases directly through their body surfaces. However, large multicellular organisms cannot rely only on simple diffusion across their body surface because many cells are located far from the external environment.

Therefore, larger organisms require specialised respiratory structures and systems to allow efficient exchange and transport of respiratory gases.

---

### 8.1.2 Adaptations of Respiratory Structures for Efficient Gas Exchange

Respiratory structures are adapted to increase the efficiency of gas exchange.

#### 1. Large total surface area to volume ratio (TSA/V)

A large total surface area provides more area for respiratory gases to diffuse across.

A high surface area to volume ratio is especially important in smaller organisms. As organisms become larger, their volume increases faster than their surface area. Therefore, large organisms need specialised respiratory structures with a large surface area to compensate.

Examples of respiratory structures with large surface areas include:

- Alveoli in humans
- Gills in fish
- Tracheal system in insects

---

#### 2. Thin respiratory structure

The respiratory surface is very thin.

In efficient respiratory structures, the exchange surface can be approximately one cell thick.

A thin respiratory surface provides a short diffusion distance. Therefore, oxygen and carbon dioxide can diffuse across the respiratory surface rapidly.

For example, the wall of an alveolus is very thin and consists of a single layer of cells.

---

#### 3. Moist respiratory surface

The surface of the respiratory structure is moist.

Oxygen and carbon dioxide must dissolve in a thin layer of moisture before they can diffuse across the respiratory surface.

Therefore, a moist respiratory surface facilitates the diffusion of respiratory gases.

---

#### 4. Network of blood capillaries

Most respiratory structures are surrounded by an extensive network of blood capillaries.

The capillaries continuously transport oxygen away from the respiratory surface and bring carbon dioxide towards it.

This helps maintain a concentration gradient between the respiratory surface and the blood, allowing diffusion to occur efficiently.

**Exception: insects**

Insects have a tracheal system and do not depend on blood capillaries to transport oxygen directly to their cells. Oxygen is delivered through the tracheal system.

---

### Summary of Adaptations

An efficient respiratory surface generally has:

- Large surface area
- Thin surface
- Moist surface
- Good ventilation
- Good blood supply, except in insects
- A maintained concentration gradient

These adaptations increase the rate and efficiency of diffusion of respiratory gases.

---

## 8.1.3 Types of Respiratory Structures

Different animals have different respiratory structures according to their body size, environment and lifestyle.

### A. Body Surface

Some simple organisms exchange gases directly through their body surface.

Examples:

- Amoeba
- Paramecium
- Planaria

Respiratory gases diffuse directly across the cell membrane or body surface.

These organisms can rely on direct diffusion because they are small and have a relatively large surface area compared with their volume.

---

### B. Gills

Gills are respiratory organs found mainly in aquatic animals such as fish.

Fish gills consist of:

- Gill arches
- Gill filaments
- Lamellae

The numerous gill filaments and lamellae provide a large surface area for gas exchange.

The lamellae contain many blood capillaries and have thin surfaces.

Water continuously flows over the gill surface, providing oxygen for diffusion.

#### Countercurrent exchange

In fish, water flows across the lamellae in the opposite direction to the flow of blood in the capillaries.

This is called **countercurrent exchange**.

It maintains a concentration gradient along the entire length of the lamellae.

As a result, oxygen can continue diffusing from the water into the blood efficiently.

---

### C. Tracheal System

Insects such as grasshoppers have a tracheal system.

The system consists of:

- Spiracles
- Tracheae
- Tracheoles

**Spiracles** are openings on the body surface.

**Tracheae** are larger tubes that carry air into the body.

**Tracheoles** are very fine tubes that branch from the tracheae and extend close to body cells.

Oxygen diffuses from the tracheoles directly to the cells.

Carbon dioxide diffuses from the cells into the tracheoles and eventually leaves through the spiracles.

Unlike humans, insects do not rely on blood to transport oxygen from the respiratory surface to body cells.

---

### D. Lungs

Lungs are respiratory organs found in terrestrial vertebrates such as humans.

In humans, air enters through the nose or mouth and travels through:

Nasal cavity → pharynx → larynx → trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli

The alveoli are the main sites of gaseous exchange.

# 8.2 MECHANISMS OF BREATHING

## 8.2.1 Human Respiratory System

The main structures involved in human breathing are:

- Nose and nasal cavity
- Pharynx
- Larynx
- Trachea
- Bronchi
- Bronchioles
- Lungs
- Alveoli
- Diaphragm
- Intercostal muscles
- Rib cage

### Functions of the Respiratory Structures

**Nasal cavity**

- Filters dust and microorganisms using hairs and mucus.
- Warms incoming air.
- Moistens incoming air.

**Trachea**

- Carries air towards the lungs.
- Supported by C-shaped cartilage rings to prevent collapse.
- Lined with mucus and ciliated cells.

**Bronchi**

The trachea divides into two bronchi, with one bronchus entering each lung.

**Bronchioles**

The bronchi branch into smaller bronchioles that distribute air throughout the lungs.

**Alveoli**

The alveoli are the main sites of gaseous exchange.

**Diaphragm**

A dome-shaped sheet of muscle separating the thoracic cavity from the abdominal cavity.

**Intercostal muscles**

Muscles between the ribs that help move the rib cage during breathing.

---

## 8.2.2 Inhalation

Inhalation is the process of breathing air into the lungs.

During inhalation:

1. External intercostal muscles contract.
2. The rib cage moves upwards and outwards.
3. The diaphragm contracts and flattens.
4. The volume of the thoracic cavity increases.
5. The pressure inside the lungs decreases.
6. Atmospheric pressure becomes greater than the pressure inside the lungs.
7. Air moves into the lungs.

### Key relationship

**Increase in thoracic volume → decrease in lung pressure → air enters the lungs**

---

## 8.2.3 Exhalation

Exhalation is the process of removing air from the lungs.

During normal exhalation:

1. External intercostal muscles relax.
2. The rib cage moves downwards and inwards.
3. The diaphragm relaxes and becomes dome-shaped.
4. The volume of the thoracic cavity decreases.
5. The pressure inside the lungs increases.
6. Lung pressure becomes greater than atmospheric pressure.
7. Air moves out of the lungs.

### Key relationship

**Decrease in thoracic volume → increase in lung pressure → air leaves the lungs**

---

## 8.2.4 Comparison Between Inhalation and Exhalation

| Feature | Inhalation | Exhalation |
|---|---|---|
| External intercostal muscles | Contract | Relax |
| Rib cage | Upwards and outwards | Downwards and inwards |
| Diaphragm | Contracts and flattens | Relaxes and becomes dome-shaped |
| Thoracic volume | Increases | Decreases |
| Lung pressure | Decreases | Increases |
| Air movement | Into lungs | Out of lungs |

### Common exam mistake

Do not say that air is "pulled" into the lungs by the lungs.

Air moves because of a **difference in pressure** between the atmosphere and the lungs.

---

# 8.3 GASEOUS EXCHANGE IN HUMANS

## 8.3.1 Alveoli as the Site of Gaseous Exchange

The alveoli are tiny air sacs located at the ends of bronchioles.

They are specialised for efficient gaseous exchange.

Each alveolus has several adaptations:

### 1. Large surface area

There are millions of alveoli in the lungs, providing a very large total surface area for gaseous exchange.

### 2. Thin walls

The wall of an alveolus is approximately one cell thick.

The capillary wall is also very thin.

Therefore, the diffusion distance between the air in the alveolus and the blood is very short.

### 3. Moist surface

The inner surface of the alveolus is moist.

Oxygen and carbon dioxide dissolve in the moisture before diffusing.

### 4. Dense capillary network

Each alveolus is surrounded by a network of blood capillaries.

This allows rapid transport of respiratory gases.

### 5. Continuous ventilation

Breathing continuously replaces the air in the alveoli.

This helps maintain a concentration gradient for oxygen and carbon dioxide.

---

## 8.3.2 Diffusion of Oxygen

The concentration of oxygen in the air inside the alveolus is higher than the concentration of oxygen in the deoxygenated blood arriving at the pulmonary capillaries.

Therefore:

**Oxygen diffuses from the alveolus → blood**

Oxygen then binds to haemoglobin in red blood cells to form oxyhaemoglobin.

---

## 8.3.3 Diffusion of Carbon Dioxide

The concentration of carbon dioxide in the blood arriving at the alveolar capillaries is higher than the concentration of carbon dioxide in the alveolar air.

Therefore:

**Carbon dioxide diffuses from the blood → alveolus**

Carbon dioxide is then removed from the lungs during exhalation.

---

## 8.3.4 Maintaining the Concentration Gradient

Efficient gaseous exchange requires a concentration gradient.

The concentration gradient is maintained by:

- Continuous ventilation of the lungs
- Continuous blood flow through pulmonary capillaries
- Continuous transport of oxygen by haemoglobin
- Continuous removal of carbon dioxide

These processes prevent the concentrations of gases on both sides of the respiratory surface from becoming equal.

---

## 8.3.5 Pathway of Respiratory Gases

### Oxygen

Atmosphere → nasal cavity → trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli → blood capillaries → red blood cells → body tissues

### Carbon dioxide

Body tissues → blood → pulmonary capillaries → alveoli → bronchioles → bronchi → trachea → nasal cavity → atmosphere

---

# 8.4 HEALTH ISSUES RELATED TO THE HUMAN RESPIRATORY SYSTEM

The respiratory system can be affected by diseases, pollutants and harmful substances.

## 8.4.1 Asthma

Asthma is a condition in which the airways become inflamed and narrowed.

This can make breathing difficult.

Possible symptoms include:

- Difficulty breathing
- Wheezing
- Coughing
- Tightness in the chest

Triggers can include:

- Dust
- Smoke
- Pollen
- Air pollution
- Certain allergens
- Physical activity in some individuals

When the airways become narrowed, less air can reach the alveoli.

---

## 8.4.2 Emphysema

Emphysema is a lung condition involving damage to the walls of alveoli.

When alveolar walls are damaged, some alveoli may lose their normal structure.

This reduces the total surface area available for gaseous exchange.

Therefore, the efficiency of gaseous exchange decreases.

Smoking is a major risk factor for emphysema.

---

## 8.4.3 Bronchitis

Bronchitis involves inflammation of the bronchi.

It can cause:

- Persistent coughing
- Increased mucus production
- Difficulty breathing

Long-term exposure to cigarette smoke and other irritants can increase the risk of chronic bronchitis.

---

## 8.4.4 Lung Cancer

Lung cancer occurs when abnormal cells in the lung grow uncontrollably.

Smoking is a major risk factor.

Long-term exposure to certain pollutants and harmful substances can also increase risk.

Lung cancer can interfere with normal respiratory function.

---

## 8.4.5 Effects of Smoking on the Respiratory System

Cigarette smoke contains harmful substances.

Examples include:

- Nicotine
- Carbon monoxide
- Tar

### Nicotine

Nicotine is an addictive substance.

It can cause dependence and affect the cardiovascular system.

### Carbon monoxide

Carbon monoxide binds strongly to haemoglobin.

This reduces the ability of haemoglobin to transport oxygen.

Therefore, less oxygen can be delivered to body tissues.

### Tar

Tar contains many harmful chemicals and can damage respiratory tissues.

It can damage cilia and increase the risk of respiratory diseases, including lung cancer.

---

## 8.4.6 Air Pollution

Air pollution can contain harmful substances such as:

- Smoke
- Fine particles
- Toxic gases
- Industrial pollutants

Long-term exposure to polluted air can increase the risk of respiratory problems.

Reducing exposure to pollutants and improving air quality can help protect respiratory health.

---

# IMPORTANT EXAM CONNECTIONS

## Why are respiratory structures thin?

A thin respiratory surface provides a short diffusion distance, allowing respiratory gases to diffuse rapidly.

## Why must respiratory surfaces be moist?

Respiratory gases must dissolve in moisture before they can diffuse across the respiratory surface.

## Why is a large surface area important?

A large surface area provides more area for respiratory gases to diffuse across, increasing the rate of gas exchange.

## Why are blood capillaries important?

Blood capillaries transport respiratory gases and help maintain a concentration gradient for diffusion.

## Why don't insects require blood capillaries for gas exchange?

Their tracheal system delivers oxygen through tracheae and tracheoles directly to body tissues.

## Why does inhalation occur?

Thoracic volume increases, causing lung pressure to decrease below atmospheric pressure. Air therefore moves into the lungs.

## Why does exhalation occur?

Thoracic volume decreases, causing lung pressure to increase above atmospheric pressure. Air therefore moves out of the lungs.

## Why is the alveolus efficient for gaseous exchange?

It has a large surface area, thin moist walls, a dense capillary network and continuous ventilation.

# COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS

### Misconception 1
"Large organisms have a larger surface area to volume ratio."

**Correction:** As an organism becomes larger, its volume increases faster than its surface area, so its surface area to volume ratio decreases.

### Misconception 2
"Blood carries oxygen directly from the lungs through the trachea."

**Correction:** The trachea carries air. Blood transports oxygen after oxygen diffuses from the alveoli into the blood.

### Misconception 3
"The diaphragm pushes air into the lungs."

**Correction:** Movement of the diaphragm changes thoracic volume and therefore changes lung pressure, causing air to move.

### Misconception 4
"Gas exchange happens in the bronchi."

**Correction:** In humans, gaseous exchange mainly occurs across the alveoli.

### Misconception 5
"Insects use blood to transport oxygen."

**Correction:** Oxygen is transported through the tracheal system directly to body tissues.

# SPM-STYLE PRACTICE QUESTIONS

## Question 1 — 2 marks

State two adaptations of an efficient respiratory surface.

### Answer

- Large surface area.
- Thin respiratory surface.

---

## Question 2 — 3 marks

Explain why the alveoli are suitable for gaseous exchange.

### Answer

The alveoli have a large surface area, providing a large area for diffusion. Their walls are thin, giving a short diffusion distance. The moist surface allows respiratory gases to dissolve before diffusing.

---

## Question 3 — 4 marks

Explain how inhalation occurs in humans.

### Answer

The external intercostal muscles contract and the diaphragm contracts and flattens. The rib cage moves upwards and outwards, increasing the volume of the thoracic cavity. Lung pressure decreases below atmospheric pressure, causing air to enter the lungs.

---

## Question 4 — 4 marks

Explain how carbon dioxide is removed from the blood in the lungs.

### Answer

The concentration of carbon dioxide in the blood entering the pulmonary capillaries is higher than that in the alveoli. Carbon dioxide therefore diffuses from the blood across the thin capillary and alveolar walls into the alveoli. It is then removed from the lungs during exhalation.

---

## Question 5 — 3 marks

Why are blood capillaries important around respiratory structures?

### Answer

Blood capillaries transport oxygen away from the respiratory surface and carbon dioxide towards it. This helps maintain a concentration gradient, allowing continuous diffusion of respiratory gases.

---

## Question 6 — 3 marks

Why can insects exchange gases without using blood capillaries?

### Answer

Insects have a tracheal system consisting of tracheae and tracheoles. Oxygen enters through spiracles and is transported through the tracheal system directly to body cells. Therefore, blood capillaries are not required to transport oxygen to the cells.
