# 14 Support and Movement in Humans and Animals

## 14.1 Types of Skeleton

### 1. Meaning and Functions of a Skeleton

A skeleton is a supporting framework that gives an organism its shape and helps it maintain its body structure.

The skeleton performs several important functions:

1. **Support** – provides structural support and maintains body shape.
2. **Protection** – protects delicate internal organs.
3. **Movement** – provides surfaces for muscle attachment and acts as a system of levers.
4. **Muscle attachment** – provides attachment points for muscles.
5. **Blood cell production** – red bone marrow produces blood cells.
6. **Mineral storage** – bones store minerals such as calcium and phosphate.

### 2. Three Types of Skeleton

There are three main types of skeleton:

- Hydrostatic skeleton
- Exoskeleton
- Endoskeleton

### A. Hydrostatic Skeleton

A hydrostatic skeleton is a supporting structure formed by fluid enclosed within a body cavity and surrounded by muscles.

It is found in animals such as earthworms and some other soft-bodied invertebrates.

**Structure:**
- The body contains fluid within a cavity.
- The fluid is relatively incompressible.
- Muscles surrounding the cavity contract to change the body's shape.

**How movement occurs:**

1. Circular muscles contract.
2. The body segment becomes longer and thinner.
3. Longitudinal muscles contract.
4. The body segment becomes shorter and thicker.
5. Coordinated contractions produce movement.

In an earthworm, alternating contractions of circular and longitudinal muscles allow the animal to move.

**Advantages:**
- Allows flexible movement.
- Supports soft-bodied organisms.
- Does not require a rigid external skeleton.

**Limitations:**
- Provides less protection for internal organs than a rigid skeleton.
- Does not support very large body structures effectively.

### B. Exoskeleton

An exoskeleton is a hard supporting structure found on the outside of an animal's body.

It is found in arthropods such as insects, crabs and spiders.

In many arthropods, the exoskeleton contains chitin. In crustaceans such as crabs, it may also contain calcium carbonate.

**Functions:**
1. Supports the body.
2. Protects internal organs.
3. Provides attachment points for muscles.
4. Reduces water loss in many terrestrial arthropods.

**How movement occurs:**

1. Muscles are attached to the inner surface of the exoskeleton.
2. Muscles contract and pull on the rigid structure.
3. Movement occurs at flexible joints between body segments or appendages.

**Advantages:**
- Provides strong protection.
- Supports the body.
- Reduces water loss in many terrestrial species.

**Limitations:**
- Does not grow continuously with the animal.
- Must be shed during moulting or ecdysis.
- The animal is vulnerable while the new exoskeleton hardens.

### Moulting (Ecdysis)

Moulting is the process in which an arthropod sheds its old exoskeleton to allow growth.

**General sequence:**

1. A new exoskeleton develops beneath the old one.
2. The old exoskeleton splits.
3. The animal leaves the old exoskeleton.
4. The new exoskeleton expands.
5. The new exoskeleton hardens.

During this process, the animal may be more vulnerable because its new exoskeleton is not yet fully hardened.

### C. Endoskeleton

An endoskeleton is an internal supporting structure found inside the body.

It is found in vertebrates such as fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals.

In humans, the endoskeleton consists mainly of bones and cartilage.

**Functions:**
1. Supports the body.
2. Protects internal organs.
3. Provides attachment points for skeletal muscles.
4. Allows movement through joints and muscle action.
5. Produces blood cells in bone marrow.
6. Stores minerals such as calcium and phosphate.

**Advantages:**
- Grows together with the organism.
- Provides strong internal support.
- Allows a wide range of movement.
- Protects important organs.

### 3. Comparison of the Three Types of Skeleton

| Feature | Hydrostatic skeleton | Exoskeleton | Endoskeleton |
|---|---|---|---|
| Location | Fluid-filled body cavity | Outside the body | Inside the body |
| Main material | Body fluid | Chitin or other hard materials | Bone and cartilage in vertebrates |
| Examples | Earthworm | Crab, insect | Human, fish |
| Growth | Body grows as tissues grow | Requires moulting in arthropods | Grows with the organism |
| Protection | Limited | Strong external protection | Protects internal organs |
| Movement | Muscle contraction against fluid | Muscles pull on exoskeleton | Muscles pull on bones |

### 4. Relationship Between Skeleton and Movement

The skeleton and muscles work together to produce movement.

- Bones provide support and act as levers.
- Joints allow bones to move relative to one another.
- Skeletal muscles contract and pull on bones.
- Tendons connect muscles to bones.
- Ligaments connect bones to other bones and help stabilise joints.

**Important:** Muscles can pull but cannot actively push bones. Therefore, many movements require antagonistic muscle pairs.

## 14.2 Musculoskeletal System of Humans

### 1. Meaning of the Human Musculoskeletal System

The human musculoskeletal system consists of the skeletal system and muscular system working together.

It includes:
- Bones
- Cartilage
- Joints
- Ligaments
- Tendons
- Skeletal muscles

**Main functions:**
1. Supports the body.
2. Maintains posture.
3. Protects internal organs.
4. Produces movement and locomotion.
5. Provides attachment points for muscles.
6. Helps maintain body stability.

### 2. Human Skeleton

The adult human skeleton normally consists of **206 bones**.

The skeleton is divided into two main parts:

1. Axial skeleton
2. Appendicular skeleton

### A. Axial Skeleton

The axial skeleton forms the central axis of the body.

It consists of:
- Skull
- Vertebral column
- Rib cage
- Sternum

**Skull**
- Protects the brain.
- Supports structures of the face.
- Protects some sensory organs.

**Vertebral column**
- Supports the head and trunk.
- Protects the spinal cord.
- Allows flexibility and movement of the trunk.
- Helps maintain upright posture.

**Rib cage**
- Protects the heart and lungs.
- Supports the chest.
- Helps in breathing through movement of the ribs.

**Sternum**
- Provides attachment for ribs through costal cartilage.
- Forms the front part of the rib cage.

### 3. Vertebral Column and Its Five Regions

The vertebral column, also called the backbone or spine, is part of the axial skeleton.

It consists of a series of bones called vertebrae. The vertebral column supports the head and trunk, protects the spinal cord and allows controlled movement of the body.

In a typical adult, it consists of **33 vertebrae before fusion**:

1. 7 cervical vertebrae
2. 12 thoracic vertebrae
3. 5 lumbar vertebrae
4. 5 sacral vertebrae
5. Usually 4 coccygeal vertebrae

The sacral vertebrae fuse to form the sacrum, while the coccygeal vertebrae usually fuse to form the coccyx.

#### A. Cervical Vertebrae (C1–C7)

- Located in the neck.
- Consist of 7 vertebrae.
- Support the head.
- Allow movements of the neck, including bending and rotation.
- Have relatively small bodies compared with lumbar vertebrae.

**Special cervical vertebrae:**

**Atlas (C1)**
- The first cervical vertebra.
- Supports the skull.
- Allows the head to nod, such as during the movement of saying "yes".

**Axis (C2)**
- The second cervical vertebra.
- Has a projection called the dens or odontoid process.
- The dens acts as a pivot that allows rotation of the atlas and head.

#### B. Thoracic Vertebrae (T1–T12)

- Located in the chest region.
- Consist of 12 vertebrae.
- Articulate with the ribs.
- Help form and support the rib cage.
- Protect organs in the thoracic cavity together with the ribs and sternum.
- Provide stability while allowing limited movement.

#### C. Lumbar Vertebrae (L1–L5)

- Located in the lower back.
- Consist of 5 vertebrae.
- Have large, strong vertebral bodies.
- Support much of the body's weight.
- Allow movements such as bending and extension of the lower back.
- Are adapted to withstand considerable compressive forces.

#### D. Sacral Vertebrae (S1–S5)

- Usually consist of 5 vertebrae.
- Fuse to form one triangular bone called the sacrum.
- Located at the base of the vertebral column.
- Connect with the pelvic bones.
- Transfer the weight of the upper body to the pelvic girdle and lower limbs.
- Help form the posterior part of the pelvis.

#### E. Coccygeal Vertebrae

- Usually consist of 4 small vertebrae.
- Fuse to form the coccyx, commonly called the tailbone.
- Located below the sacrum.
- Provide attachment points for some muscles and ligaments of the pelvic floor.

#### Summary of the Five Regions

| Region | Number of vertebrae before fusion | Location | Main function |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Cervical | 7 | Neck | Supports head and allows neck movement |
| Thoracic | 12 | Chest | Articulates with ribs |
| Lumbar | 5 | Lower back | Supports body weight |
| Sacral | 5 | Pelvic region | Transfers body weight to pelvis |
| Coccygeal | Usually 4 | Below sacrum | Provides muscle and ligament attachment |

**Total: Usually 33 vertebrae before fusion.**

**Memory aid:** C7 – T12 – L5 – S5 – Co4.

#### Intervertebral Discs

Intervertebral discs are pads of fibrocartilage located between most adjacent vertebral bodies.

**Functions:**
1. Absorb shock during movement.
2. Reduce friction between vertebrae.
3. Allow flexibility of the vertebral column.
4. Distribute pressure between vertebrae.

The discs are not found between every vertebra. For example, the fused sacral and coccygeal vertebrae do not have normal movable intervertebral discs between their fused segments.

#### Functions of the Vertebral Column

1. Supports the head and trunk.
2. Protects the spinal cord inside the vertebral canal.
3. Maintains body posture.
4. Allows bending, extension and rotation of the trunk.
5. Provides attachment points for muscles and ligaments.
6. Transfers body weight to the pelvic girdle.

### B. Appendicular Skeleton

The appendicular skeleton consists of the bones of the limbs and the girdles that attach them to the axial skeleton.

**Pectoral girdle**
- Connects the upper limbs to the axial skeleton.
- Consists mainly of the clavicle and scapula.

**Upper limbs**
- Humerus
- Radius
- Ulna
- Carpals
- Metacarpals
- Phalanges

**Pelvic girdle**
- Connects the lower limbs to the axial skeleton.
- Supports the weight of the upper body.
- Protects some organs in the pelvic cavity.

**Lower limbs**
- Femur
- Patella
- Tibia
- Fibula
- Tarsals
- Metatarsals
- Phalanges

### 4. Classification of Bones According to Shape

Bones can be classified according to their shape.

| Type of bone | Characteristics | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Long bone | Longer than it is wide | Femur, humerus |
| Short bone | Approximately cube-shaped | Carpals, tarsals |
| Flat bone | Thin and often curved | Skull bones, sternum |
| Irregular bone | Complex shape | Vertebra |
| Sesamoid bone | Develops within a tendon | Patella |

**Important distinction:** Cervical, thoracic and lumbar are regions of the vertebral column. Long, short, flat, irregular and sesamoid are classifications of bones according to shape. These are two different classification systems.

### 5. Structure of a Long Bone

A long bone such as the femur contains several important structures.

**A. Epiphysis**
- The enlarged end of a long bone.
- Contains mainly spongy bone.
- Helps form joints with other bones.

**B. Diaphysis**
- The shaft of the long bone.
- Mainly consists of compact bone.
- Provides strength and support.

**C. Compact bone**
- Dense and strong bone tissue.
- Provides mechanical strength.
- Forms much of the outer layer of bones.

**D. Spongy bone**
- Contains a network of bony structures called trabeculae.
- Makes bones lighter while maintaining strength.
- Often contains red bone marrow.

**E. Bone marrow**
- Red bone marrow produces blood cells.
- Yellow bone marrow mainly stores fat.

**F. Periosteum**
- A tough connective tissue membrane covering the outer surface of bone, except at joint surfaces.
- Contains blood vessels and nerves.
- Helps with bone growth, repair and nourishment.

**G. Articular cartilage**
- Covers the ends of bones at joints.
- Reduces friction.
- Absorbs shock during movement.

**H. Medullary cavity**
- A cavity within the shaft of many long bones.
- Contains yellow bone marrow in many adults.

### 6. Bone Growth and Repair

Bones are living tissues that can grow and repair themselves.

**Bone growth:**
- Lengthwise growth occurs at the epiphyseal plates during childhood and adolescence.
- These plates contain cartilage cells that divide and are gradually replaced by bone tissue.
- When growth is complete, the plates close and become epiphyseal lines.

**Bone repair:**
1. Blood vessels at the injured area may be damaged.
2. A blood clot forms around the fracture.
3. Repair cells form a soft callus.
4. The soft callus is gradually replaced by a hard bony callus.
5. Bone tissue is remodelled over time.

The rate of repair depends on factors such as age, blood supply, nutrition and the severity of the injury.

### 7. Cartilage

Cartilage is a flexible connective tissue found in various parts of the body.

**Functions:**
- Reduces friction at joints.
- Absorbs shock.
- Provides flexible support.
- Forms structures such as the nose and external ear.
- Forms the early framework of many bones during development.

Cartilage has no direct blood supply and receives nutrients by diffusion from surrounding tissues.

### 8. Types of Joints

A joint is a place where two or more bones meet.

Joints can be classified according to the amount of movement they allow.

**A. Immovable joints**
- Do not allow significant movement.
- Example: sutures between the bones of the skull.

**B. Slightly movable joints**
- Allow limited movement.
- Example: joints between vertebral bodies, with intervertebral discs.

**C. Freely movable joints**
- Allow a wide range of movement.
- Most are synovial joints.
- Examples: shoulder, elbow, knee and hip.

### 9. Structure of a Synovial Joint

A synovial joint allows free movement between bones.

**A. Articular cartilage**
- Covers the ends of bones.
- Reduces friction.
- Absorbs shock.

**B. Synovial membrane**
- Lines the inner surface of the joint capsule.
- Produces synovial fluid.

**C. Synovial fluid**
- Lubricates the joint.
- Reduces friction between articular surfaces.
- Helps nourish the cartilage.

**D. Joint capsule**
- Surrounds and encloses the joint.
- Helps hold the bones together.

**E. Ligaments**
- Connect bone to bone.
- Stabilise the joint.
- Limit excessive movement.

**F. Tendons**
- Connect muscle to bone.
- Transmit the force produced by muscle contraction.

### 10. Types of Synovial Joints

**A. Hinge joint**

Allows movement mainly in one plane, such as bending and straightening.

Examples:
- Elbow
- Knee
- Interphalangeal joints of fingers

**B. Ball-and-socket joint**

Allows movement in many directions, including rotation.

Examples:
- Shoulder
- Hip

**C. Pivot joint**

Allows rotation around an axis.

Example:
- Joint between the first and second cervical vertebrae, allowing rotation of the head.

**D. Gliding joint**

Allows bones to slide over one another through small movements.

Examples:
- Some joints between the carpal bones.
- Some joints between the tarsal bones.

### 11. Comparison of Hinge and Ball-and-Socket Joints

| Feature | Hinge joint | Ball-and-socket joint |
|---|---|---|
| Movement | Mainly one plane | Several directions |
| Movement examples | Flexion and extension | Flexion, extension, abduction, adduction and rotation |
| Example | Elbow | Shoulder |
| Structure | Rounded surface fits into a groove-like surface | Rounded head fits into a cup-shaped socket |

### 12. Types of Muscle Tissue

There are three main types of muscle tissue.

**A. Skeletal muscle**
- Attached to bones by tendons.
- Usually under voluntary control.
- Has a striated appearance.
- Produces body movement and helps maintain posture.

**B. Smooth muscle**
- Found in walls of organs such as the intestines and blood vessels.
- Involuntary.
- Does not have visible striations.
- Moves substances through internal organs.

**C. Cardiac muscle**
- Found in the wall of the heart.
- Involuntary.
- Striated and branched.
- Contracts rhythmically to pump blood.

### 13. Structure of Skeletal Muscle

A skeletal muscle is made up of bundles of muscle fibres.

**Organisation:**

Whole muscle → Fascicles → Muscle fibres → Myofibrils → Sarcomeres → Actin and myosin filaments.

**Important structures:**
- Muscle fibre: a long muscle cell.
- Myofibril: a structure inside a muscle fibre that contains repeating sarcomeres.
- Sarcomere: the basic functional contractile unit of striated muscle.
- Actin: thin protein filament.
- Myosin: thick protein filament.

### 14. Sliding Filament Theory

The sliding filament theory explains how skeletal muscles contract.

Muscle contraction occurs when actin filaments slide past myosin filaments, causing sarcomeres to shorten.

**Main sequence:**

1. A nerve impulse reaches the neuromuscular junction.
2. Acetylcholine is released from the motor neurone.
3. The muscle fibre membrane is stimulated.
4. An electrical signal spreads along the muscle fibre.
5. Calcium ions are released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum.
6. Calcium ions bind to troponin.
7. Tropomyosin moves away from the myosin-binding sites on actin.
8. Myosin heads attach to actin and form cross-bridges.
9. Myosin heads perform a power stroke, pulling actin filaments towards the centre of the sarcomere.
10. ATP allows myosin to detach and reset for another cycle.
11. Repeated cycles produce muscle contraction.

When stimulation stops, calcium ions are transported back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum. The binding sites become covered again and the muscle relaxes.

**Important facts:**
- Actin and myosin filaments slide past one another.
- The filaments themselves do not become shorter.
- Sarcomeres shorten during contraction.
- ATP is required for the cross-bridge cycle and calcium transport.

### 15. Role of ATP in Muscle Contraction

ATP supplies energy for muscle activity.

ATP is needed to:
1. Detach myosin heads from actin.
2. Reset myosin heads for another contraction cycle.
3. Transport calcium ions back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum during relaxation.

Without sufficient ATP, normal muscle contraction and relaxation cannot occur.

### 16. Antagonistic Muscles

Skeletal muscles can only pull on bones. They cannot push bones.

Therefore, muscles often work in pairs called antagonistic pairs.

When one muscle contracts, the opposing muscle relaxes.

**Example: Biceps and triceps at the elbow**

**When the forearm bends (flexion):**
- Biceps contracts.
- Triceps relaxes.
- The forearm moves towards the upper arm.

**When the forearm straightens (extension):**
- Triceps contracts.
- Biceps relaxes.
- The forearm moves away from the upper arm.

| Movement | Biceps | Triceps |
|---|---|---|
| Flexion | Contracts | Relaxes |
| Extension | Relaxes | Contracts |

**Exam tip:** Always state which muscle contracts and which muscle relaxes.

## 14.3 Movement and Locomotion

### 1. Meaning of Movement and Locomotion

**Movement** is a change in the position of the whole body or part of the body.

Examples:
- Bending an arm.
- Moving the head.
- Blinking.

**Locomotion** is movement that results in a change in the position of the entire organism from one place to another.

Examples:
- Walking
- Running
- Swimming
- Flying

All locomotion involves movement, but not all movement is locomotion.

### 2. Movement in Humans

Human movement is produced by the coordinated action of:
- Bones
- Joints
- Skeletal muscles
- Tendons
- Ligaments
- Nervous system

**General sequence:**

1. The nervous system sends a signal to a skeletal muscle.
2. The muscle receives stimulation.
3. The muscle contracts.
4. The muscle pulls on its tendon.
5. The tendon pulls on the bone.
6. The bone moves around a joint.

### 3. Flexion and Extension

**Flexion**
- Decreases the angle between two bones at a joint.
- Example: bending the elbow.

**Extension**
- Increases the angle between two bones at a joint.
- Example: straightening the elbow.

### 4. Abduction and Adduction

**Abduction**
- Movement of a body part away from the midline of the body.
- Example: raising an arm sideways away from the body.

**Adduction**
- Movement of a body part towards the midline of the body.
- Example: lowering the arm back towards the body.

### 5. Rotation

Rotation is the movement of a bone around its own longitudinal axis.

Examples:
- Turning the head from side to side.
- Rotating the upper arm at the shoulder.

### 6. Movement at the Shoulder and Elbow

**Shoulder joint:**
- A ball-and-socket joint.
- Allows movement in several directions.
- Allows flexion, extension, abduction, adduction and rotation.

**Elbow joint:**
- A hinge joint.
- Allows mainly flexion and extension.

### 7. Movement and Locomotion in Animals

Different animals have different adaptations for movement and locomotion.

These adaptations depend on:
- Habitat
- Body structure
- Type of skeleton
- Muscles
- Method of movement

### A. Locomotion in Fish

Fish move through water using their muscles, body shape and fins.

**Adaptations:**

1. Streamlined body reduces water resistance.
2. Alternating contraction of muscles on both sides of the body produces side-to-side movement.
3. The tail fin provides the main forward thrust in many fish.
4. Other fins help with steering, stability and braking.
5. Scales and mucus help reduce friction and protect the body.

**Function of fins:**
- Caudal fin: produces forward thrust.
- Pectoral fins: help with steering, braking and changes in direction.
- Pelvic fins: help with balance and manoeuvring.
- Dorsal fin: helps prevent rolling.
- Anal fin: helps stabilise the body.

### B. Locomotion in Birds

Birds are adapted for flight.

**Adaptations:**

1. Forelimbs are modified into wings.
2. Flight feathers provide a broad surface for generating aerodynamic forces.
3. A streamlined body reduces air resistance.
4. Strong pectoral muscles power wing movements.
5. Many bones are lightweight and contain air spaces.
6. The sternum has a keel in many flying birds for attachment of flight muscles.
7. Efficient respiratory and circulatory systems support the high energy demands of flight.

**How flight occurs:**
- Wing movements generate forces that support and propel the bird.
- The shape and movement of the wings influence lift and thrust.
- Tail feathers help with steering and balance.

### C. Locomotion in Amphibians

Amphibians such as frogs are adapted for movement on land and in water.

**Adaptations in frogs:**
- Strong hind limbs provide power for jumping.
- Long hind feet help push against the ground.
- Webbed feet help push water during swimming.
- Flexible joints allow limb movement.

### D. Locomotion in Terrestrial Mammals

Terrestrial mammals use limbs to support body weight and move on land.

Examples include humans, horses and cats.

**Adaptations:**
- Strong bones support body weight.
- Skeletal muscles produce movement.
- Joints allow flexible limb movement.
- Tendons transfer muscle force to bones.
- Limb structure differs according to the animal's movement and habitat.

### 8. Comparison of Movement in Different Animals

| Animal | Main movement adaptation |
|---|---|
| Earthworm | Alternating contraction of circular and longitudinal muscles against a hydrostatic skeleton |
| Crab | Muscles attached to an external exoskeleton move jointed appendages |
| Fish | Body muscles and fins produce swimming movement |
| Bird | Wings and strong flight muscles support flight |
| Frog | Powerful hind limbs support jumping and swimming |
| Human | Bones, joints and antagonistic skeletal muscles produce movement |

### 9. Importance of Locomotion

Locomotion allows animals to:
1. Find food.
2. Escape from predators.
3. Find suitable habitats.
4. Find mates.
5. Search for shelter.
6. Reach suitable breeding sites.

## 14.4 Health Issues Related to the Human Musculoskeletal System

### 1. Musculoskeletal Disorders

Musculoskeletal disorders affect bones, muscles, joints, tendons, ligaments or other supporting structures.

They may affect movement, strength, posture and daily activities.

Examples include:
- Osteoporosis
- Arthritis
- Osteoarthritis
- Rheumatoid arthritis
- Scoliosis
- Muscular injuries
- Tendon and ligament injuries

### 2. Osteoporosis

Osteoporosis is a condition in which bone density decreases and bones become more fragile.

This increases the risk of fractures.

**Factors associated with osteoporosis:**
- Ageing
- Reduced oestrogen levels after menopause
- Insufficient calcium intake
- Insufficient vitamin D
- Lack of weight-bearing physical activity
- Some medical conditions and medications
- Family history

**Possible effects:**
- Increased risk of fractures.
- Loss of height over time.
- Back pain when vertebral fractures occur.
- Reduced mobility after fractures.

**Prevention and management:**
1. Consume sufficient calcium.
2. Maintain adequate vitamin D.
3. Perform suitable weight-bearing and muscle-strengthening activities.
4. Avoid smoking.
5. Seek medical assessment when risk factors are present.

### 3. Arthritis

Arthritis refers to conditions that cause inflammation or damage in joints.

It may cause:
- Joint pain.
- Stiffness.
- Swelling.
- Reduced movement.
- Difficulty performing daily activities.

There are different types of arthritis.

### A. Osteoarthritis

Osteoarthritis is a joint disease involving the gradual breakdown and changes of joint structures, especially articular cartilage.

**What happens:**
1. Articular cartilage becomes damaged and thinner.
2. The joint surfaces become less well protected.
3. Changes occur in the underlying bone and surrounding tissues.
4. Joint movement may become painful and stiff.

**Risk factors include:**
- Increasing age.
- Previous joint injury.
- Excessive mechanical stress on a joint.
- Certain genetic and developmental factors.

**Management:**
- Suitable physical activity.
- Physiotherapy.
- Weight management where medically appropriate.
- Pain management under medical guidance.
- Surgery in some severe cases.

### B. Rheumatoid Arthritis

Rheumatoid arthritis is an autoimmune disease in which the immune system attacks tissues in the joints.

**What happens:**
1. The immune system mistakenly attacks joint tissues.
2. The synovial membrane becomes inflamed.
3. Swelling and pain occur.
4. Continued inflammation may damage cartilage and bone.
5. Joint function may become reduced.

It can affect several joints and may also affect other organs.

**Management:**
- Early medical diagnosis.
- Medicines that control immune system activity.
- Physiotherapy and suitable exercise.
- Regular medical monitoring.

### 4. Comparison Between Osteoarthritis and Rheumatoid Arthritis

| Osteoarthritis | Rheumatoid arthritis |
|---|---|
| Involves gradual joint degeneration and structural changes | Autoimmune disease |
| Articular cartilage is commonly affected | Synovial membrane is attacked by the immune system |
| Risk increases with age and previous joint damage | Can occur at different ages |
| Often affects weight-bearing joints | Commonly affects joints on both sides of the body |
| Causes pain and stiffness | Causes inflammation, swelling, pain and stiffness |

### 5. Scoliosis

Scoliosis is an abnormal sideways curvature of the spine.

It may develop during childhood or adolescence, although it can occur at other ages.

**Possible signs:**
- Uneven shoulders.
- One shoulder blade appears more prominent.
- Uneven waist.
- One hip appears higher.
- The spine curves sideways.

Many cases have no clearly identified cause.

**Management depends on severity:**
- Monitoring for mild curves.
- Bracing for some growing adolescents.
- Surgery for selected severe or progressive cases.

### 6. Muscle Cramps and Muscle Strains

**Muscle cramp**
- A sudden, involuntary contraction of a muscle.
- Can cause temporary pain and stiffness.
- May occur during or after physical activity.

**Muscle strain**
- An injury involving overstretching or tearing of muscle fibres or a muscle-tendon unit.
- May cause pain, swelling and reduced movement.

**General prevention:**
- Warm up before strenuous exercise.
- Increase exercise intensity gradually.
- Use appropriate techniques.
- Allow sufficient recovery.
- Maintain suitable hydration.

Persistent or severe symptoms should be assessed by a healthcare professional.

### 7. Sprains and Ligament Injuries

A sprain is an injury to a ligament, usually caused by stretching or twisting a joint beyond its normal range.

Common locations include:
- Ankle
- Knee
- Wrist

**Possible symptoms:**
- Pain.
- Swelling.
- Bruising.
- Reduced joint movement.
- Difficulty bearing weight.

A suspected serious injury should be assessed by a healthcare professional.

### 8. Tendon Injuries

Tendons connect muscles to bones.

A tendon injury may occur because of repeated overuse, sudden force or degeneration.

Examples include tendinitis and tendon tears.

**Possible effects:**
- Pain during movement.
- Tenderness.
- Swelling.
- Reduced strength or function.

### 9. Importance of Maintaining a Healthy Musculoskeletal System

1. Consume a balanced diet containing sufficient protein, calcium and vitamin D.
2. Participate in regular suitable physical activity.
3. Perform appropriate muscle-strengthening and weight-bearing exercises.
4. Maintain good posture.
5. Use suitable techniques when lifting objects.
6. Warm up before strenuous activity.
7. Allow adequate recovery after exercise.
8. Avoid smoking.
9. Seek medical advice for persistent pain or movement problems.

# Important Exam Comparisons

## 1. Hydrostatic Skeleton, Exoskeleton and Endoskeleton

| Hydrostatic skeleton | Exoskeleton | Endoskeleton |
|---|---|---|
| Fluid-filled cavity | External hard structure | Internal supporting structure |
| Earthworm | Crab and insect | Human and fish |
| Muscles act against fluid | Muscles pull on exoskeleton | Muscles pull on bones |
| Flexible body support | Protection and support | Support and protection |

## 2. Tendon vs Ligament

| Tendon | Ligament |
|---|---|
| Connects muscle to bone | Connects bone to bone |
| Transfers force from muscle to bone | Stabilises joints |
| Helps produce movement | Helps prevent excessive joint movement |

## 3. Flexion vs Extension

| Flexion | Extension |
|---|---|
| Decreases angle between bones | Increases angle between bones |
| Bending movement | Straightening movement |
| Example: bending elbow | Example: straightening elbow |

## 4. Skeletal, Smooth and Cardiac Muscles

| Skeletal muscle | Smooth muscle | Cardiac muscle |
|---|---|---|
| Voluntary | Involuntary | Involuntary |
| Striated | Non-striated | Striated |
| Attached to bones | Walls of internal organs | Heart wall |
| Produces body movement | Moves substances through organs | Pumps blood |

## 5. Five Regions of the Vertebral Column

| Region | Number | Main role |
|---|---:|---|
| Cervical | 7 | Supports head and allows neck movement |
| Thoracic | 12 | Articulates with ribs |
| Lumbar | 5 | Supports much of body weight |
| Sacral | 5 fused | Connects vertebral column to pelvis |
| Coccygeal | Usually 4 fused | Provides attachment for muscles and ligaments |

# Common Examination Mistakes

1. **Confusing movement with locomotion:** Movement can involve only one body part, while locomotion changes the organism's location.
2. **Confusing exoskeleton and endoskeleton:** Exoskeleton is external; endoskeleton is internal.
3. **Saying an exoskeleton grows continuously:** Arthropods must moult to increase in size.
4. **Confusing tendons and ligaments:** Tendons connect muscle to bone; ligaments connect bone to bone.
5. **Saying muscles push bones:** Muscles contract and pull on bones.
6. **Forgetting antagonistic muscle pairs:** One muscle contracts while the opposing muscle relaxes.
7. **Confusing actin and myosin:** Both are protein filaments that interact during muscle contraction.
8. **Saying actin and myosin become shorter:** They slide past one another; the sarcomere shortens.
9. **Confusing flexion and extension:** Flexion decreases the angle; extension increases it.
10. **Confusing osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis:** Osteoarthritis involves joint degeneration; rheumatoid arthritis is autoimmune.
11. **Confusing sprain and strain:** Sprain affects a ligament; strain affects a muscle or tendon.
12. **Mixing up vertebral regions:** Remember C7, T12, L5, S5 and usually Co4.
13. **Confusing the atlas and axis:** Atlas supports the skull; axis allows rotation of the head.
14. **Confusing bone classification with vertebral regions:** Long, short, flat, irregular and sesamoid describe bone shapes, not spinal regions.

# SPM-Style Practice Questions

## Section A: Structured Questions

### Question 1: Types of Skeleton

(a) Name THREE types of skeleton found in animals. [3 marks]

(b) State ONE example of an animal for each type of skeleton. [3 marks]

(c) Explain how an earthworm uses its hydrostatic skeleton for movement. [4 marks]

(d) Explain why arthropods need to undergo moulting. [2 marks]

### Question 2: Human Skeleton

(a) State the TWO main divisions of the human skeleton. [2 marks]

(b) Give TWO examples of bones in the axial skeleton. [2 marks]

(c) State TWO functions of the human skeleton. [2 marks]

(d) Explain the function of cartilage at a synovial joint. [2 marks]

### Question 3: Vertebral Column

(a) State the five regions of the vertebral column in order from top to bottom. [5 marks]

(b) State the number of vertebrae in the cervical and thoracic regions. [2 marks]

(c) Explain the functions of the atlas and axis. [4 marks]

(d) State TWO functions of intervertebral discs. [2 marks]

### Question 4: Joints and Muscles

(a) Define a joint. [2 marks]

(b) Name TWO types of synovial joints and give ONE example of each. [4 marks]

(c) Explain the difference between a tendon and a ligament. [2 marks]

(d) Describe the movement of the forearm during flexion at the elbow. [4 marks]

### Question 5: Muscle Contraction

(a) Name the TWO main protein filaments involved in skeletal muscle contraction. [2 marks]

(b) State the role of calcium ions in muscle contraction. [2 marks]

(c) Explain the sliding filament theory. [5 marks]

(d) State TWO roles of ATP in muscle contraction and relaxation. [2 marks]

### Question 6: Movement and Locomotion

(a) Define movement and locomotion. [4 marks]

(b) State TWO adaptations of fish for swimming. [2 marks]

(c) Explain how the fins of fish help in movement. [3 marks]

(d) State THREE adaptations of birds for flight. [3 marks]

### Question 7: Musculoskeletal Health

(a) Define osteoporosis. [2 marks]

(b) State TWO risk factors associated with osteoporosis. [2 marks]

(c) Explain the difference between osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis. [4 marks]

(d) State TWO ways to maintain a healthy musculoskeletal system. [2 marks]

## Section B: Application and HOTS Questions

### Question 8: Antagonistic Muscles

A student bends the forearm towards the upper arm.

(a) Name the movement. [1 mark]

(b) State which muscle contracts and which muscle relaxes. [2 marks]

(c) Explain why the biceps and triceps must work as an antagonistic pair. [2 marks]

### Question 9: Arthropod Growth

An insect sheds its old exoskeleton several times during its growth.

(a) Name this process. [1 mark]

(b) Explain why this process is necessary. [2 marks]

(c) Explain why the insect may be more vulnerable immediately after shedding its old exoskeleton. [2 marks]

### Question 10: Joint Structure

A patient has damage to the articular cartilage in the knee joint.

(a) State TWO functions of articular cartilage. [2 marks]

(b) Explain how damage to this cartilage can affect movement. [3 marks]

(c) Suggest TWO ways to help maintain joint health. [2 marks]

### Question 11: Muscle Contraction

During muscle contraction, ATP becomes available to the muscle fibres.

(a) State the function of ATP in the cross-bridge cycle. [2 marks]

(b) Explain why calcium ions are important in allowing myosin to bind to actin. [3 marks]

(c) Predict what would happen to normal muscle relaxation if calcium ions could not be transported back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum. [2 marks]
