# 13 Homeostasis and the Human Urinary System

## 13.1 Homeostasis

### 1. Meaning of Homeostasis

Homeostasis is the regulation of the physical and chemical conditions inside the body so that they remain relatively constant within a suitable range, even when the external environment changes.

The internal environment includes:
- Body temperature
- Blood glucose concentration
- Water content
- Mineral ion concentration
- pH of body fluids

Cells and enzymes function most effectively when internal conditions remain within suitable limits. If these conditions change too much, normal cell activities may be affected.

**Examples of homeostasis:**
- Maintaining body temperature at approximately 37°C.
- Regulating blood glucose concentration.
- Controlling water and salt balance in the body.

### 2. Importance of Homeostasis

Homeostasis is important because it:
1. Maintains suitable conditions for enzyme activity.
2. Allows cells to function normally.
3. Maintains the balance of water and mineral ions.
4. Prevents major changes in the internal environment.
5. Supports the normal functioning of organs and body systems.

### 3. Negative Feedback Mechanism

Negative feedback is a regulatory mechanism in which a change from the normal condition triggers responses that reverse the original change.

It helps return a condition towards its normal level.

**Main components:**
1. Receptor – detects a change in the internal environment.
2. Control centre – receives information and coordinates a response.
3. Effector – carries out the response.
4. Response – acts to reduce the original change.

**General sequence:**

Change in internal condition → Receptor detects the change → Control centre processes information → Effector responds → Condition moves back towards normal.

Negative feedback does not necessarily maintain a perfectly fixed value. Instead, it keeps conditions within a suitable range.

### 4. Regulation of Body Temperature (Thermoregulation)

Thermoregulation is the regulation of body temperature.

The hypothalamus in the brain acts as the main temperature-regulating control centre. It receives information about body temperature and coordinates responses.

#### When body temperature increases

Examples of causes:
- Hot surroundings
- Physical activity
- High environmental temperature

Responses include:

**a. Increased sweating**
- Sweat glands produce more sweat.
- Sweat spreads over the skin surface.
- Water in the sweat evaporates.
- Evaporation absorbs heat from the skin.
- Heat loss increases and body temperature decreases.

**b. Vasodilation**
- Arterioles supplying the skin widen.
- More blood flows through blood capillaries near the skin surface.
- More heat is transferred from the blood to the surroundings.
- Heat loss increases.

**c. Reduced metabolic heat production**
- Some heat-producing activities may be reduced as the body coordinates its response.

#### When body temperature decreases

Examples of causes:
- Cold surroundings
- Exposure to cold wind
- Immersion in cold water

Responses include:

**a. Reduced sweating**
- Sweat glands produce less sweat.
- Less heat is lost through evaporation.

**b. Vasoconstriction**
- Arterioles supplying the skin narrow.
- Less blood flows near the skin surface.
- Heat loss to the surroundings decreases.

**c. Shivering**
- Skeletal muscles contract and relax rapidly and involuntarily.
- Muscle activity increases cellular respiration.
- More heat is produced.

**d. Hair erection**
- Small muscles attached to hair follicles contract.
- Body hairs stand more upright.
- In furry mammals, trapped air can provide insulation.
- In humans, this response has little effect because body hair is relatively sparse.

**e. Increased metabolic heat production**
- The body can increase heat production through metabolic responses.

### 5. Regulation of Blood Glucose Concentration

Blood glucose concentration must be regulated because glucose is an important respiratory substrate used by cells to release energy.

The pancreas contains groups of endocrine cells called the islets of Langerhans.

Two important hormones are:
- Insulin – lowers blood glucose concentration.
- Glucagon – raises blood glucose concentration.

#### When blood glucose concentration increases

For example, after a meal containing carbohydrates:

1. Blood glucose concentration increases.
2. Beta cells in the pancreas detect the increase.
3. Beta cells secrete insulin.
4. Insulin promotes the uptake of glucose by body cells.
5. Insulin stimulates the liver and muscles to convert excess glucose into glycogen.
6. Blood glucose concentration decreases towards normal.

This process is called glycogenesis when glucose is converted into glycogen.

#### When blood glucose concentration decreases

For example, between meals:

1. Blood glucose concentration decreases.
2. Alpha cells in the pancreas detect the decrease.
3. Alpha cells secrete glucagon.
4. Glucagon stimulates the liver to break down glycogen into glucose.
5. The liver releases glucose into the blood.
6. Blood glucose concentration increases towards normal.

The breakdown of glycogen into glucose is called glycogenolysis.

The liver can also produce glucose from non-carbohydrate sources through gluconeogenesis.

#### Comparison of insulin and glucagon

| Feature | Insulin | Glucagon |
|---|---|---|
| Secreted by | Beta cells | Alpha cells |
| Blood glucose effect | Decreases it | Increases it |
| Main action | Promotes glucose uptake and glycogenesis | Stimulates glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis |
| Main target organ | Liver, muscles and other tissues | Mainly liver |

Insulin and glucagon work antagonistically because they produce opposite effects on blood glucose concentration.

### 6. Regulation of Water Content (Osmoregulation)

Osmoregulation is the regulation of water and dissolved substances in the body.

The kidneys play an important role by adjusting the amount of water reabsorbed from the kidney tubules.

The hormone involved is antidiuretic hormone (ADH).

ADH is produced by the hypothalamus and stored and released by the posterior pituitary gland.

#### When the body has insufficient water

For example, after excessive sweating or insufficient water intake:

1. Blood becomes more concentrated.
2. Osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus detect the increase in blood concentration.
3. More ADH is released from the posterior pituitary gland.
4. ADH increases the permeability of the collecting ducts to water.
5. More water is reabsorbed into the blood.
6. A smaller volume of concentrated urine is produced.

#### When the body has excess water

1. Blood becomes more dilute.
2. Osmoreceptors detect the decrease in blood concentration.
3. Less ADH is released.
4. Collecting ducts become less permeable to water.
5. Less water is reabsorbed into the blood.
6. A larger volume of dilute urine is produced.

#### Summary of ADH action

| Condition | ADH secretion | Water reabsorption | Urine |
|---|---|---|---|
| Insufficient body water | Increases | Increases | Small volume, concentrated |
| Excess body water | Decreases | Decreases | Large volume, dilute |

### 7. Positive Feedback and Negative Feedback

Negative feedback reverses a change and helps maintain homeostasis.

Positive feedback increases or strengthens the original change until a particular event is completed.

| Negative feedback | Positive feedback |
|---|---|
| Reverses the original change | Strengthens the original change |
| Helps maintain stable internal conditions | Drives a process towards completion |
| Example: regulation of body temperature | Example: oxytocin increasing uterine contractions during childbirth |

**Exam tip:** If asked to explain negative feedback, do not only state that the body returns to normal. Explain the sequence involving the receptor, control centre, effector and response.

## 13.2 The Urinary System

### 1. Functions of the Urinary System

The urinary system removes metabolic waste products and helps regulate the composition of blood.

Its main functions are:
1. Excreting urea and other metabolic waste products.
2. Regulating water content in the blood.
3. Regulating mineral ion concentrations.
4. Helping maintain the pH of blood.
5. Contributing to blood pressure regulation.
6. Producing urine.

**Excretion** is the removal of metabolic waste products and substances in excess from the body.

**Urine** is a liquid waste produced by the kidneys.

### 2. Main Organs of the Human Urinary System

The human urinary system consists of:
- Two kidneys
- Two ureters
- One urinary bladder
- One urethra

#### Kidneys
- Two bean-shaped organs located towards the back of the abdominal cavity.
- Filter blood and produce urine.
- Regulate water and mineral ion balance.
- Remove urea and other waste substances.

#### Ureters
- Two narrow muscular tubes.
- Carry urine from the kidneys to the urinary bladder.
- Urine moves through the ureters by peristaltic contractions.

#### Urinary bladder
- A muscular, expandable sac.
- Temporarily stores urine.
- Its muscular wall contracts during urination.

#### Urethra
- A tube that carries urine from the urinary bladder to the outside of the body.
- Urine leaves the body through the urethra during urination.

### 3. Structure of the Kidney

A longitudinal section of a kidney shows three main regions:

**a. Renal cortex**
- Outer region of the kidney.
- Contains the renal corpuscles and parts of the kidney tubules.
- Renal corpuscles consist of the glomerulus and Bowman's capsule.

**b. Renal medulla**
- Inner region of the kidney.
- Contains renal pyramids and parts of the kidney tubules and collecting ducts.

**c. Renal pelvis**
- Central, funnel-shaped region.
- Collects urine from the collecting ducts through the calyces.
- Directs urine into the ureter.

Other important structures:
- Renal artery – carries blood containing metabolic wastes into the kidney.
- Renal vein – carries filtered blood away from the kidney.
- Ureter – carries urine to the urinary bladder.

### 4. Nephron: The Functional Unit of the Kidney

A nephron is the structural and functional unit of the kidney. Each kidney contains a very large number of nephrons.

Each nephron filters blood and selectively reabsorbs useful substances.

**Main structures of a nephron:**

1. Afferent arteriole – carries blood into the glomerulus.
2. Glomerulus – a network of blood capillaries where ultrafiltration occurs.
3. Bowman's capsule – surrounds the glomerulus and collects glomerular filtrate.
4. Efferent arteriole – carries blood away from the glomerulus.
5. Proximal convoluted tubule (PCT) – where selective reabsorption of many useful substances occurs.
6. Loop of Henle – helps establish the concentration gradient in the kidney medulla.
7. Distal convoluted tubule (DCT) – involved in further adjustment of the filtrate.
8. Collecting duct – receives filtrate from nephrons and allows further regulation of water reabsorption.

The nephron is closely associated with blood capillaries that allow substances to move between the blood and kidney tubule.

### 5. Formation of Urine

Urine formation involves three main processes:

1. Ultrafiltration
2. Selective reabsorption
3. Tubular secretion

#### A. Ultrafiltration

Ultrafiltration occurs at the glomerulus and Bowman's capsule.

**How it occurs:**

1. Blood enters the glomerulus through the afferent arteriole.
2. The blood is under relatively high hydrostatic pressure.
3. This pressure forces water and small dissolved substances through the filtration barrier into Bowman's capsule.
4. The fluid collected in Bowman's capsule is called glomerular filtrate.

Substances that normally enter the filtrate include:
- Water
- Glucose
- Amino acids
- Mineral ions
- Urea

Substances that normally remain in the blood include:
- Blood cells
- Most large plasma proteins

**Why do blood cells and large proteins remain in the blood?**

They are generally too large to pass through the filtration barrier.

**Important:** Ultrafiltration is not the same as selective reabsorption. Ultrafiltration is pressure-driven filtration of small substances from blood into Bowman's capsule.

#### B. Selective Reabsorption

Selective reabsorption is the process in which useful substances are reabsorbed from the kidney tubule into the blood.

It occurs mainly in the proximal convoluted tubule.

Substances reabsorbed include:
- All or almost all glucose under normal conditions
- Amino acids
- Much of the water
- Mineral ions

**How selective reabsorption occurs:**

1. Glucose and amino acids are transported from the filtrate into the cells of the tubule wall.
2. Transport proteins help move these substances across cell membranes.
3. Many substances enter the surrounding blood capillaries.
4. Water follows the movement of dissolved substances by osmosis.
5. Useful substances return to the blood.

Selective reabsorption prevents the loss of valuable substances in urine.

**Adaptations of the proximal convoluted tubule:**
- Numerous microvilli provide a large surface area.
- Many mitochondria supply ATP for active transport.
- The tubule wall is thin, allowing efficient movement of substances.
- It is surrounded by blood capillaries for efficient exchange.

#### C. Tubular Secretion

Tubular secretion is the movement of certain substances from the blood in surrounding capillaries into the kidney tubule.

It helps regulate blood composition and remove additional substances.

Substances that may be secreted include:
- Hydrogen ions
- Potassium ions
- Ammonium ions
- Certain drugs and metabolic waste products

**Importance of tubular secretion:**
1. Helps regulate blood pH.
2. Helps regulate mineral ion concentration.
3. Allows additional waste substances to enter the filtrate.
4. Supports the removal of certain drugs and foreign substances.

### 6. Formation of Urine: Overall Summary

| Process | Location | Main event |
|---|---|---|
| Ultrafiltration | Glomerulus and Bowman's capsule | Water and small dissolved substances enter Bowman's capsule |
| Selective reabsorption | Mainly PCT | Useful substances return to the blood |
| Tubular secretion | Mainly PCT and DCT | Certain substances move from blood into the tubule |

The fluid remaining in the collecting duct becomes urine.

Urine normally contains:
- Water
- Urea
- Mineral ions
- Other dissolved waste substances

Urine normally contains no detectable glucose and no significant amount of blood cells or large plasma proteins.

### 7. Composition of Blood, Glomerular Filtrate and Urine

| Substance | Blood | Glomerular filtrate | Normal urine |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blood cells | Present | Absent | Absent |
| Large plasma proteins | Present | Normally absent | Normally absent |
| Water | Present | Present | Present |
| Glucose | Present | Present | Normally absent |
| Amino acids | Present | Present | Normally absent |
| Urea | Present | Present | Present |
| Mineral ions | Present | Present | Present |

**Important:** Glucose is filtered into Bowman's capsule but is normally reabsorbed before urine is produced.

### 8. Regulation of Water Reabsorption by ADH

The kidneys adjust the amount of water reabsorbed according to the body's water needs.

ADH affects the permeability of the collecting ducts and late distal tubules to water.

**When blood is too concentrated:**
- More ADH is released.
- More water channels are inserted into the cell membranes.
- More water is reabsorbed into the blood.
- A smaller volume of concentrated urine is produced.

**When blood is too dilute:**
- Less ADH is released.
- Fewer water channels are present.
- Less water is reabsorbed.
- A larger volume of dilute urine is produced.

This is an example of negative feedback that helps maintain water balance.

### 9. Pathway of Urine

The pathway of urine is:

Collecting ducts → Renal calyces → Renal pelvis → Ureter → Urinary bladder → Urethra → Outside the body.

**Do not confuse:**
- Renal artery: carries blood into the kidney.
- Renal vein: carries blood away from the kidney.
- Ureter: carries urine from the kidney to the bladder.
- Urethra: carries urine from the bladder out of the body.

### 10. Excretion and Egestion

| Excretion | Egestion |
|---|---|
| Removal of metabolic waste products | Removal of undigested food |
| Involves kidneys, lungs and skin | Involves the digestive system |
| Example: removal of urea in urine | Example: removal of faeces |

Urea is formed mainly in the liver from the breakdown of excess amino acids. It is transported in the blood to the kidneys for excretion.

### 11. Other Functions of the Kidneys

Besides urine production, kidneys help maintain internal balance.

**a. Regulation of water balance**
- Adjusts the amount of water reabsorbed.
- Works with ADH.

**b. Regulation of mineral ions**
- Adjusts the excretion and reabsorption of ions such as sodium and potassium.

**c. Regulation of blood pH**
- Secretes hydrogen ions.
- Reabsorbs filtered bicarbonate ions.
- Helps maintain acid-base balance.

**d. Regulation of blood pressure**
- Kidneys influence blood volume and release renin, which contributes to the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system.

**e. Production of erythropoietin**
- Kidneys produce erythropoietin, a hormone that stimulates red blood cell production in the bone marrow when oxygen availability is low.

## 13.3 Health Issues Related to the Urinary System

### 1. Urinary Tract Infection (UTI)

A urinary tract infection occurs when microorganisms, commonly bacteria, infect part of the urinary system.

A UTI may affect:
- Urethra
- Urinary bladder
- Ureters
- Kidneys

**Possible symptoms:**
- Pain or burning sensation during urination
- Frequent urge to urinate
- Cloudy urine
- Lower abdominal discomfort

A kidney infection may cause fever, chills, back or side pain and nausea.

**Prevention and care:**
- Maintain suitable personal hygiene.
- Drink sufficient water.
- Avoid delaying urination for long periods.
- Seek medical assessment when symptoms occur.

Antibiotics may be prescribed by a healthcare professional for bacterial UTIs. Treatment depends on the site and severity of infection.

### 2. Kidney Stones

Kidney stones are hard deposits formed when certain minerals and salts in urine crystallise.

They may form when urine becomes too concentrated or when substances that form crystals are present in high concentrations.

**Possible symptoms:**
- Severe pain in the side or back
- Pain that may spread towards the lower abdomen or groin
- Blood in the urine
- Nausea or vomiting
- Pain during urination

**Factors that may increase risk:**
- Insufficient water intake
- Certain dietary patterns
- Family history
- Some medical conditions

**Prevention and management:**
- Drink sufficient water unless medically advised otherwise.
- Follow medical advice about diet.
- Seek medical assessment for suspected kidney stones.
- Treatment depends on stone size, location and symptoms.

### 3. Kidney Failure

Kidney failure occurs when the kidneys lose their ability to perform their normal functions effectively.

The kidneys may become unable to:
- Remove sufficient metabolic waste.
- Regulate water balance.
- Maintain mineral ion balance.
- Maintain acid-base balance.

As a result, waste products and excess fluid may accumulate in the body.

**Possible causes include:**
- Long-term diabetes mellitus
- High blood pressure
- Severe kidney infections
- Certain kidney diseases
- Serious injury or reduced blood supply to the kidneys

**Possible effects:**
- Accumulation of urea and other waste products in the blood
- Swelling due to fluid retention
- Changes in urine production
- Disturbance of mineral ion levels
- Anaemia and other complications

Kidney failure requires medical management.

### 4. Haemodialysis

Haemodialysis is a treatment that uses a dialysis machine to remove waste products and excess water from the blood when the kidneys cannot perform these functions adequately.

**How haemodialysis works:**

1. Blood is taken from the patient's body through a blood vessel access.
2. Blood flows through a dialyser containing partially permeable membranes.
3. Dialysis fluid flows on the other side of the membrane.
4. The concentration of urea and other waste substances is higher in the blood than in the dialysis fluid.
5. Waste substances diffuse from the blood into the dialysis fluid.
6. Excess water can also be removed by controlled ultrafiltration.
7. The cleaned blood is returned to the patient's body.

**Important features of dialysis fluid:**
- Contains a suitable concentration of glucose.
- Contains mineral ions at suitable concentrations.
- Normally contains no urea.
- Its composition is controlled to encourage removal of waste while preventing excessive loss of useful substances.

**Role of the partially permeable membrane:**
- Allows small molecules to pass through.
- Prevents blood cells and large plasma proteins from passing through.

### 5. Peritoneal Dialysis

Peritoneal dialysis uses the peritoneum, a membrane lining the abdominal cavity, as the exchange surface.

**General process:**
1. Dialysis fluid is introduced into the abdominal cavity through a catheter.
2. Waste products and excess substances move from blood vessels in the peritoneum into the dialysis fluid.
3. The fluid remains in the abdominal cavity for a prescribed period.
4. The used dialysis fluid is drained and replaced.

The exact treatment schedule and method are determined by healthcare professionals.

### 6. Kidney Transplant

A kidney transplant is a surgical procedure in which a healthy kidney from a suitable donor is placed into a person whose kidneys no longer function adequately.

**Advantages:**
- Can provide long-term kidney function.
- May reduce the need for regular dialysis.
- Can improve quality of life for suitable patients.

**Limitations and risks:**
- A suitable donor is required.
- The immune system may reject the transplanted kidney.
- The recipient usually needs immunosuppressant medication.
- Surgery and long-term follow-up are required.

### 7. Comparison Between Haemodialysis and Kidney Transplant

| Haemodialysis | Kidney transplant |
|---|---|
| Uses a dialysis machine to filter blood | Uses a donated functioning kidney |
| Usually requires repeated treatment sessions | May provide long-term kidney function |
| Does not replace all kidney functions | A transplanted kidney can perform many kidney functions |
| Requires access to a dialysis service | Requires a suitable donor and surgery |
| Does not involve transplant rejection | Risk of immune rejection |

### 8. Comparison Between Haemodialysis and Peritoneal Dialysis

| Haemodialysis | Peritoneal dialysis |
|---|---|
| Uses a dialyser outside the body | Uses the peritoneum as the exchange membrane |
| Blood passes through the dialyser | Dialysis fluid enters the abdominal cavity |
| Requires blood vessel access | Requires a catheter in the abdominal cavity |
| Usually performed in scheduled sessions | Can be performed using a prescribed home-based schedule |

### 9. Importance of Maintaining Urinary System Health

Healthy habits can help reduce the risk of some urinary system problems.

1. Drink sufficient water according to individual needs and medical advice.
2. Maintain good personal hygiene.
3. Avoid delaying urination unnecessarily.
4. Maintain a balanced diet.
5. Manage blood pressure and blood glucose.
6. Seek medical attention for persistent urinary symptoms.
7. Avoid taking medicines or supplements without appropriate advice, especially if kidney disease is suspected.

People with existing kidney or heart conditions may need individual fluid or dietary advice from their healthcare team.

# Important Exam Comparisons

## 1. Ultrafiltration vs Selective Reabsorption

| Ultrafiltration | Selective reabsorption |
|---|---|
| Occurs at the glomerulus and Bowman's capsule | Occurs mainly at the PCT |
| Driven by blood pressure | Uses transport processes, including active transport and diffusion |
| Small substances enter Bowman's capsule | Useful substances return to the blood |
| Blood cells and large proteins normally remain in blood | Glucose, amino acids, water and ions are reabsorbed |

## 2. Concentrated Urine vs Dilute Urine

| Concentrated urine | Dilute urine |
|---|---|
| Produced when the body needs to conserve water | Produced when the body has excess water |
| Smaller volume | Larger volume |
| Higher concentration of dissolved waste | Lower concentration of dissolved waste |
| More ADH is released | Less ADH is released |
| More water is reabsorbed | Less water is reabsorbed |

## 3. Vasodilation vs Vasoconstriction

| Vasodilation | Vasoconstriction |
|---|---|
| Arterioles widen | Arterioles narrow |
| More blood flows near the skin surface | Less blood flows near the skin surface |
| Increases heat loss | Reduces heat loss |
| Helps cool the body | Helps conserve heat |

# Common Examination Mistakes

1. **Confusing ureter and urethra:** The ureter carries urine from the kidney to the bladder. The urethra carries urine out of the body.
2. **Saying glucose is not filtered:** Glucose enters Bowman's capsule but is normally reabsorbed.
3. **Saying ultrafiltration occurs in the PCT:** Ultrafiltration occurs at the glomerulus and Bowman's capsule.
4. **Saying all substances in filtrate become urine:** Useful substances are selectively reabsorbed.
5. **Confusing insulin and glucagon:** Insulin lowers blood glucose; glucagon raises it.
6. **Confusing ADH production and release:** ADH is produced in the hypothalamus and stored and released by the posterior pituitary.
7. **Saying sweating cools the body because sweat is cold:** Evaporation of sweat removes heat from the skin.
8. **Confusing excretion and egestion:** Excretion removes metabolic waste; egestion removes undigested food.
9. **Saying dialysis completely replaces every kidney function:** Dialysis removes waste and excess fluid but does not perform every function of healthy kidneys.
10. **Forgetting the negative feedback sequence:** Explain the change, detection, control response and return towards normal.

- Smaller volume of urine.
- More concentrated urine.

## Question 7

(a) Selective reabsorption.

(b) Diabetes mellitus.

(c)
- Uncontrolled diabetes mellitus can cause blood glucose concentration to become very high.
- The amount of glucose filtered may exceed the reabsorption capacity of the kidney tubules.
- Some glucose remains in the filtrate and is excreted in urine.

## Question 8

(a)
- Damaged kidneys cannot filter and remove urea effectively.
- Urea accumulates in the blood.

(b) Haemodialysis.

(c)
- Blood passes through a dialyser.
- The dialyser contains a partially permeable membrane.
- Urea diffuses from the blood into dialysis fluid because of a concentration gradient.
- Blood cells and large plasma proteins remain in the blood.
- Cleaned blood returns to the body.
