# 7.1 ENERGY PRODUCTION THROUGH CELLULAR RESPIRATION

## 1. What is cellular respiration?

Cellular respiration is a metabolic process in which organic molecules such as glucose are broken down in living cells to release energy.

The energy released is used to produce ATP (adenosine triphosphate).

ATP supplies energy for various cellular activities, including:

- Muscle contraction.
- Active transport across plasma membranes.
- Cell division.
- Growth and development.
- Synthesis of proteins.
- Movement of cellular structures.

**Important distinction:**

Cellular respiration is not the same as breathing.

- Breathing is the physical process of taking air into and removing air from the lungs.
- Cellular respiration is a chemical process that breaks down organic compounds to release energy inside cells.

## 2. Main substrate in cellular respiration

The main substrate used in cellular respiration is glucose.

Glucose is an organic compound containing stored chemical energy.

During cellular respiration, glucose is broken down through a series of enzyme-controlled reactions.

The energy released is transferred into ATP, which can be used by cells.

## 3. ATP as an energy carrier

ATP is known as the energy currency of the cell because it transfers usable energy for cellular activities.

ATP consists of:
- Adenine.
- Ribose sugar.
- Three phosphate groups.

When ATP is hydrolysed, the terminal phosphate group is removed.

ATP + H₂O → ADP + Pi + Energy

Where:
- ATP = adenosine triphosphate.
- ADP = adenosine diphosphate.
- Pi = inorganic phosphate.

Energy released from ATP hydrolysis is used for cellular activities.

Examples:

**Active transport**
- ATP supplies energy to transport substances against their concentration gradients.
- This allows cells to absorb or remove substances when necessary.

**Muscle contraction**
- ATP supplies energy for interactions between actin and myosin filaments.
- This enables muscles to contract.

**Protein synthesis**
- Energy is required to join amino acids together to form proteins.

ADP can be converted back into ATP when energy from cellular respiration is available.

## 4. Types of cellular respiration

There are two main processes studied in this chapter:

### A. Aerobic respiration

Aerobic respiration is the breakdown of glucose in the presence of oxygen to release energy.

Characteristics:
- Requires oxygen.
- Glucose is completely oxidised.
- Produces carbon dioxide and water.
- Releases a large amount of energy.
- Occurs in the cytoplasm and mitochondrion.

### B. Fermentation

Fermentation is the incomplete breakdown of glucose without oxygen or under limited oxygen conditions to release energy.

Characteristics:
- Does not require oxygen.
- Occurs in the cytoplasm.
- Glucose is incompletely broken down.
- Releases a small amount of energy.
- Produces ethanol and carbon dioxide, or lactic acid, depending on the type.

## 5. Energy released from glucose

Different cellular respiration processes release different amounts of energy.

| Process | Energy released per mole of glucose |
|---|---:|
| Aerobic respiration | 2,898 kJ |
| Alcohol fermentation | 210 kJ |
| Lactic acid fermentation | 150 kJ |

**Why is there a difference?**

During aerobic respiration, glucose is completely oxidised into carbon dioxide and water.

During fermentation, glucose is incompletely broken down. Some chemical energy remains stored in the products, such as ethanol or lactic acid.

Therefore, aerobic respiration releases much more energy than fermentation.

**Important exam point:** Always include the correct energy value and unit (kJ) when writing the word equations.

# 7.2 AEROBIC RESPIRATION

## 1. Definition of aerobic respiration

Aerobic respiration is the process in which glucose is broken down completely in the presence of oxygen to produce carbon dioxide, water and energy.

Oxygen is used to oxidise glucose.

## 2. Word equation for aerobic respiration

**Word equation:**

Glucose + Oxygen → Carbon dioxide + Water + Energy (2,898 kJ)

**Balanced chemical equation:**

C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + Energy

One mole of glucose releases approximately 2,898 kJ of energy during complete oxidation.

## 3. Process of aerobic respiration

Aerobic respiration begins with glycolysis, followed by the oxidation of pyruvate.

### A. Glycolysis

**Location: Cytoplasm**

Glycolysis is the breakdown of glucose by enzymes.

Main events:

1. One glucose molecule containing six carbon atoms enters glycolysis.
2. Glucose undergoes a series of enzyme-controlled reactions.
3. The glucose molecule is broken down into two pyruvate molecules.
4. A small amount of energy is released and transferred into ATP.
5. Reduced NAD (NADH) is also produced.

Main products per glucose molecule:
- Two pyruvate molecules.
- Net gain of two ATP molecules.
- Two NADH molecules.

**Important:** Glycolysis does not directly require oxygen.

### B. Oxidation of pyruvate

**Location: Mitochondrion**

The pyruvate produced during glycolysis enters the mitochondrion.

Pyruvate undergoes further oxidation through a series of reactions.

During this process:
- Carbon dioxide is released.
- Water is formed during the overall aerobic process.
- Energy is released.
- A large amount of the released energy is used to produce ATP.

At a more advanced level, oxidation of pyruvate includes the link reaction, Krebs cycle and electron transport chain.

### C. Role of the mitochondrion

The mitochondrion is the main organelle involved in aerobic respiration.

Important structures:

**Outer membrane**
- Forms the outer boundary of the mitochondrion.

**Inner membrane**
- Contains proteins involved in the electron transport chain.

**Cristae**
- Folds of the inner membrane.
- Increase the surface area available for reactions involved in ATP production.

**Matrix**
- Fluid-filled region inside the inner membrane.
- Contains enzymes involved in the oxidation of pyruvate.

**Exam tip:** If asked why muscle cells contain many mitochondria, explain that muscle cells require large amounts of ATP for contraction.

## 4. ATP production

ATP is produced when an inorganic phosphate group is added to ADP.

ADP + Pi + Energy → ATP

When ATP is hydrolysed:

ATP + H₂O → ADP + Pi + Energy

The released energy is used for cellular activities.

**Remember:**
- ADP gains a phosphate group to form ATP.
- ATP loses a phosphate group to form ADP.
- Energy is released during ATP hydrolysis.

## 5. Experiment: To study aerobic respiration

### Problem statement

Do living organisms carry out aerobic respiration?

### Hypothesis

Living organisms use oxygen and release carbon dioxide during aerobic respiration.

### Principle of the experiment

A respirometer can be used to investigate oxygen consumption during aerobic respiration.

In a typical experimental setup:
- A living organism is placed in an airtight apparatus.
- Soda lime is used to absorb carbon dioxide released by the organism.
- As oxygen is consumed, the gas volume and pressure inside the apparatus decrease.
- The coloured liquid in a capillary tube moves towards the organism.

### Variables

**Manipulated variable**
- Presence of a living organism.

**Responding variable**
- Change in the level of coloured liquid.

**Fixed variable**
- Initial level of coloured liquid.

### Function of soda lime

Soda lime absorbs carbon dioxide released during respiration.

This ensures that the movement of coloured liquid is mainly due to oxygen consumption.

### Expected observation

The coloured liquid moves towards the chamber containing the living organism.

The control setup without a living organism should show little or no movement.

### Explanation

1. The living organism carries out aerobic respiration.
2. Oxygen is consumed.
3. Carbon dioxide is released.
4. Soda lime absorbs the carbon dioxide.
5. The gas pressure inside the apparatus decreases.
6. The coloured liquid moves towards the organism.

### Conclusion

Living organisms use oxygen during aerobic respiration.

**Practical exam tips:**
- Know the function of soda lime.
- Identify the manipulated, responding and fixed variables.
- Explain why the coloured liquid moves.
- Understand why a control setup is required.

# 7.3 FERMENTATION

## 1. Definition of fermentation

Fermentation is the incomplete breakdown of glucose without oxygen or under limited oxygen conditions to release energy.

Fermentation usually occurs in the cytoplasm.

It begins with glycolysis, during which glucose is converted into pyruvate.

Fermentation releases much less energy than aerobic respiration.

## 2. Factors that cause fermentation

Fermentation can occur when oxygen is absent or available in limited quantities.

Examples:
- Human muscle cells during vigorous physical activity.
- Yeast under anaerobic conditions.
- Certain bacteria.
- Certain plant cells under oxygen-deficient conditions.

The lack of sufficient oxygen prevents cells from depending entirely on aerobic respiration.

## 3. Types of fermentation

There are two main types:

1. Alcohol fermentation.
2. Lactic acid fermentation.

### A. Alcohol fermentation

Alcohol fermentation occurs in yeast and certain microorganisms.

Glucose is incompletely broken down into ethanol and carbon dioxide.

**Word equation:**

Glucose → Ethanol + Carbon dioxide + Energy (210 kJ)

Main events:

1. Glucose undergoes glycolysis in the cytoplasm.
2. Two pyruvate molecules are produced.
3. Each pyruvate molecule loses carbon dioxide.
4. The remaining compound is converted into ethanol.
5. NADH is oxidised to NAD⁺, allowing glycolysis to continue.

Characteristics:
- Does not require oxygen.
- Occurs in the cytoplasm.
- Produces ethanol.
- Releases carbon dioxide.
- Releases approximately 210 kJ per mole of glucose.

### B. Lactic acid fermentation

Lactic acid fermentation occurs in certain bacteria and human muscle cells when oxygen availability is insufficient.

Glucose is incompletely broken down into lactic acid.

**Word equation:**

Glucose → Lactic acid + Energy (150 kJ)

Main events:

1. Glucose undergoes glycolysis in the cytoplasm.
2. Two pyruvate molecules are produced.
3. Pyruvate is converted into lactate.
4. NADH is oxidised to NAD⁺.
5. NAD⁺ can be reused in glycolysis.

Characteristics:
- Does not require oxygen.
- Occurs in the cytoplasm.
- Produces lactic acid (lactate).
- Does not produce carbon dioxide in the simplified pathway.
- Releases approximately 150 kJ per mole of glucose.

## 4. Fermentation in human muscle cells

During vigorous physical activity, muscle cells require ATP rapidly.

Sometimes, the oxygen supplied by the blood is insufficient to meet the oxygen demand of the muscle cells.

When this happens:
1. Muscle cells increase their reliance on glycolysis.
2. Glucose is broken down into pyruvate.
3. Pyruvate is converted into lactate.
4. NAD⁺ is regenerated.
5. Glycolysis continues to produce a small amount of ATP.

This allows muscle cells to continue producing ATP temporarily when oxygen availability is insufficient.

During recovery, lactate can be transported through the blood and metabolised by the body.

**Exam point:** Fermentation allows glycolysis to continue by regenerating NAD⁺.

## 5. Applications of fermentation in daily life

Fermentation is important in food production because microorganisms can convert sugars into useful products.

### A. Bread making

Yeast is used in bread making.

Process:
1. Yeast is added to bread dough.
2. Yeast uses available sugars.
3. Alcohol fermentation occurs.
4. Carbon dioxide is released.
5. Carbon dioxide becomes trapped in the dough.
6. The dough expands and rises.

During baking:
- The ethanol mostly evaporates.
- Heat inactivates the yeast.
- The bread develops its final texture.

### B. Production of alcoholic beverages

Yeast carries out alcohol fermentation using sugars in suitable raw materials.

Ethanol and carbon dioxide are produced.

### C. Yoghurt production

Certain bacteria, such as Lactobacillus, are used in yoghurt production.

Process:
1. Bacteria are added to milk.
2. The bacteria ferment lactose, a sugar found in milk.
3. Lactic acid is produced.
4. The acidity increases.
5. Milk proteins coagulate.
6. The milk becomes thicker and develops a sour taste.

### D. Fermented vegetables

Certain bacteria ferment sugars present in vegetables.

Lactic acid is produced, which contributes to the sour flavour and helps preserve the food.

## 6. Comparison between aerobic respiration and fermentation

| Feature | Aerobic respiration | Fermentation |
|---|---|---|
| Oxygen requirement | Requires oxygen | Does not require oxygen or occurs under limited oxygen |
| Glucose breakdown | Complete | Incomplete |
| Location | Cytoplasm and mitochondrion | Cytoplasm |
| Products | Carbon dioxide and water | Ethanol and carbon dioxide, or lactic acid |
| Water production | Produces water | Does not produce water in the simplified comparison |
| Energy released | 2,898 kJ | 210 kJ or 150 kJ |
| ATP production | Much higher | Net gain of 2 ATP through glycolysis |

## 7. Comparison of the two types of fermentation

| Feature | Alcohol fermentation | Lactic acid fermentation |
|---|---|---|
| Organisms/cells | Yeast and certain microorganisms | Certain bacteria and human muscle cells |
| Main product | Ethanol | Lactic acid (lactate) |
| Carbon dioxide | Produced | Not produced in the simplified pathway |
| Energy released | 210 kJ | 150 kJ |
| Common application | Bread making | Yoghurt production |

## 8. Why does aerobic respiration release more energy?

Aerobic respiration completely oxidises glucose into carbon dioxide and water.

Fermentation only breaks down glucose incompletely.

Therefore:
- More energy is released during aerobic respiration.
- Less energy is released during fermentation.
- More energy is available for ATP production during aerobic respiration.

**Energy comparison:**

Aerobic respiration > Alcohol fermentation > Lactic acid fermentation.

# COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS

**1. Cellular respiration and breathing are the same.**

Correction: Breathing is the movement of air, while cellular respiration is a chemical process that releases energy.

**2. Glycolysis requires oxygen.**

Correction: Glycolysis does not directly require oxygen.

**3. Fermentation produces more energy than aerobic respiration.**

Correction: Fermentation releases much less energy than aerobic respiration.

**4. All aerobic respiration occurs in mitochondria.**

Correction: Glycolysis occurs in the cytoplasm, while further oxidation of pyruvate occurs in the mitochondrion.

**5. Oxygen directly provides energy to cells.**

Correction: Oxygen is used in aerobic respiration and acts as the final electron acceptor in the electron transport chain.

**6. Fermentation only occurs in yeast.**

Correction: Fermentation can occur in yeast, certain bacteria, human muscle cells and certain plant cells.
