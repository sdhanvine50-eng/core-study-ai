# 15.1 REPRODUCTIVE SYSTEM OF HUMANS

## 15.1.1 Introduction to Human Sexual Reproduction

Sexual reproduction is the production of offspring through the fusion of male and female gametes.

In humans:
- The male gamete is the sperm cell.
- The female gamete is the ovum (the mature female gamete).
- The formation of male gametes occurs through spermatogenesis.
- The formation of female gametes occurs through oogenesis.
- Fertilisation involves the fusion of the nuclei of the male and female gametes.

Humans reproduce sexually and are viviparous animals because the embryo develops inside the mother's reproductive system and the baby is born alive.

### Importance of sexual reproduction

1. Produces genetic variation among offspring.
2. Combines genetic material from two parents.
3. Allows characteristics to be inherited from both parents.
4. Contributes to variation within a population.
5. Genetic variation provides the basis for natural selection and evolution.

### Chromosome number in human reproduction

Human body cells normally contain 46 chromosomes arranged in 23 pairs.

These cells are diploid (2n).

Human gametes contain 23 chromosomes and are haploid (n).

During fertilisation:

- Sperm contributes 23 chromosomes.
- Ovum contributes 23 chromosomes.
- The zygote receives 46 chromosomes.
- The diploid chromosome number is restored.

Therefore:

23 + 23 = 46 chromosomes.

The reduction of chromosome number during meiosis prevents the chromosome number from doubling in every generation.

## 15.1.2 Male Reproductive System

The male reproductive system consists of organs that produce sperm, support sperm maturation, transport sperm and produce male sex hormones.

### A. Testes

The testes are a pair of male reproductive organs located in the scrotum.

Functions:

1. Produce sperm through spermatogenesis.
2. Secrete testosterone.
3. Support the development of male secondary sexual characteristics.
4. Testosterone also contributes to sperm production and reproductive function.

**Internal structure of the testes:**

- Each testis contains many tightly coiled seminiferous tubules.
- Spermatogenesis occurs inside the seminiferous tubules.
- Developing sperm cells are supported by Sertoli cells.
- Leydig cells, located between seminiferous tubules, secrete testosterone.

**Sertoli cells:**
- Provide nutrients and support to developing sperm cells.
- Help regulate the environment for sperm development.
- Contribute to the blood-testis barrier.

**Leydig cells:**
- Respond to luteinising hormone (LH).
- Produce testosterone.
- Testosterone supports spermatogenesis and male reproductive development.

### B. Scrotum

The scrotum is a pouch of skin that contains the testes.

Functions:

1. Protects the testes.
2. Maintains the testes at a temperature slightly lower than normal body temperature.
3. Provides suitable conditions for sperm production.

The testes need a temperature approximately 2–3°C below core body temperature for normal spermatogenesis.

Temperature regulation involves:
- Cremaster muscle, which raises or lowers the testes.
- Dartos muscle, which wrinkles or relaxes the scrotal skin.
- Blood vessels that help regulate heat exchange.

When the surrounding temperature is cold:
- The testes move closer to the body.
- The scrotal skin becomes more wrinkled.
- Heat loss decreases.

When the surrounding temperature is warm:
- The testes move further from the body.
- The scrotal skin becomes smoother.
- Heat loss increases.

**Biological importance:** Excessive heat can interfere with normal sperm production.

### C. Epididymis

The epididymis is a long, highly coiled tube located along the back of each testis.

Functions:
1. Stores sperm temporarily.
2. Allows sperm to mature.
3. Enables sperm to develop progressive motility.
4. Transports sperm towards the sperm duct.

Sperm leaving the testes are not fully mature. They undergo further maturation in the epididymis.

### D. Sperm Duct (Vas Deferens)

The sperm duct is a muscular tube that transports sperm from the epididymis towards the ejaculatory duct and urethra.

Its muscular wall contracts through peristaltic movements to help propel sperm.

### E. Seminal Vesicles

The seminal vesicles are glands that produce a fluid containing nutrients such as fructose.

Functions:
1. Supply fructose as an energy source for sperm.
2. Produce fluid that forms a major part of semen.
3. Provide substances that support sperm survival and movement.

### F. Prostate Gland

The prostate gland is located below the urinary bladder and surrounds part of the urethra.

Functions:
1. Produces fluid that contributes to semen.
2. Adds substances that support sperm function.
3. Its secretion helps protect sperm in the reproductive tract.

### G. Bulbourethral Glands

These glands produce a lubricating mucus-like secretion.

Functions:
- Lubricate the urethra.
- Help reduce friction.
- Contribute a small amount of fluid to the reproductive tract.

### H. Urethra

The urethra is a tube that passes through the penis.

Functions:
- Carries urine out of the body.
- Carries semen out of the body during ejaculation.

Urine and semen do not normally pass through the urethra simultaneously because muscular control prevents this.

### I. Penis

The penis is the external male reproductive organ.

It contains erectile tissue that becomes filled with blood during an erection.

Functions:
- Transfers semen into the female reproductive tract during sexual intercourse.
- Provides a passage for urine to leave the body.

### Pathway of sperm

Sperm cells follow this general pathway:

Seminiferous tubules → Epididymis → Sperm duct → Ejaculatory duct → Urethra → Outside the body.

**Important:** Seminal vesicles and the prostate gland contribute secretions to semen, but sperm are produced in the testes.

## 15.1.3 Female Reproductive System

The female reproductive system produces ova, secretes female sex hormones and provides a suitable environment for fertilisation, implantation and foetal development.

### A. Ovaries

The ovaries are a pair of female reproductive organs located in the pelvic cavity.

Functions:

1. Produce female gametes through oogenesis.
2. Secrete oestrogen.
3. Secrete progesterone.
4. Support the menstrual cycle and pregnancy.

**Structure of the ovary:**

- The ovarian cortex contains developing follicles.
- Each follicle contains an immature oocyte surrounded by supporting cells.
- The ovarian medulla contains connective tissue, blood vessels and nerves.
- During a reproductive cycle, a follicle may develop until it releases a secondary oocyte.

### B. Fallopian Tubes (Oviducts)

The fallopian tubes connect the region near the ovaries to the uterus.

Functions:

1. Receive the ovulated secondary oocyte.
2. Transport the oocyte towards the uterus.
3. Provide the usual site of fertilisation.

**Adaptations:**
- Finger-like projections called fimbriae help guide the ovulated oocyte into the tube.
- Cilia help move the oocyte or early embryo.
- Muscular contractions of the tube assist transportation.

Fertilisation usually occurs in the ampulla, the wider region of the fallopian tube.

### C. Uterus

The uterus is a muscular, hollow organ where implantation occurs and the embryo develops.

Functions:
1. Receives the blastocyst during implantation.
2. Supports embryonic and foetal development.
3. Protects the developing embryo and foetus.
4. Contracts during childbirth.

**Layers of the uterus:**

1. Endometrium
   - Inner lining of the uterus.
   - Contains blood vessels and glands.
   - Changes thickness during the menstrual cycle.
   - Provides the site for implantation.
   - Is shed during menstruation when pregnancy does not occur.

2. Myometrium
   - Thick middle layer of smooth muscle.
   - Expands during pregnancy.
   - Contracts during childbirth.

3. Perimetrium
   - Outer covering of the uterus.
   - Provides protection to the uterus.

### D. Cervix

The cervix is the narrow lower part of the uterus.

Functions:
- Connects the uterus to the vagina.
- Helps retain the developing foetus during pregnancy.
- Produces cervical mucus.
- Dilates during childbirth to allow the baby to pass through.

### E. Vagina

The vagina is an elastic muscular canal connecting the cervix to the outside of the body.

Functions:
1. Receives sperm during sexual intercourse.
2. Allows menstrual flow to leave the body.
3. Acts as the birth canal during childbirth.

### Pathway of the ovum

Ovary → Fallopian tube → Uterus.

If fertilisation occurs:
- Fertilisation usually happens in the fallopian tube.
- The early embryo travels towards the uterus.
- Implantation takes place in the endometrium.

## 15.1.4 Comparison Between Male and Female Reproductive Systems

| Male reproductive organ | Female reproductive organ | Main role |
|---|---|---|
| Testes | Ovaries | Produce gametes and sex hormones |
| Epididymis | — | Sperm maturation and storage |
| Sperm duct | Fallopian tube | Transport reproductive cells |
| Seminal vesicles | — | Produce nutrient-rich seminal fluid |
| Prostate gland | — | Adds fluid to semen |
| Penis | Vagina | Reproductive passage |
| — | Uterus | Supports embryonic development |
| — | Cervix | Connects uterus and vagina |

## 15.1.5 Hormonal Regulation in the Male Reproductive System

The hypothalamus and pituitary gland help regulate male reproductive function.

1. The hypothalamus secretes gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH).
2. GnRH stimulates the anterior pituitary gland.
3. The anterior pituitary secretes FSH and LH.
4. LH stimulates Leydig cells to produce testosterone.
5. FSH acts on Sertoli cells.
6. FSH and testosterone work together to support spermatogenesis.
7. Testosterone provides negative feedback to the hypothalamus and pituitary gland.

This feedback mechanism helps regulate hormone levels.

## 15.1.6 Secondary Sexual Characteristics

Secondary sexual characteristics develop during puberty under the influence of sex hormones.

**In males:**
- Voice becomes deeper.
- Facial and body hair develops.
- Muscle mass increases.
- Shoulders generally broaden.
- Reproductive organs mature.

**In females:**
- Breasts develop.
- Hips generally widen.
- Pubic and underarm hair develops.
- Menstruation begins.
- Reproductive organs mature.

These changes are associated with testosterone in males and oestrogen in females, together with other hormonal influences.

# 15.2 GAMETOGENESIS IN HUMANS

## 15.2.1 Definition of Gametogenesis

Gametogenesis is the process of producing haploid reproductive cells called gametes.

It occurs in the reproductive organs:

- Spermatogenesis occurs in the testes.
- Oogenesis occurs in the ovaries.

Gametogenesis involves meiosis and cell differentiation.

### Importance of gametogenesis

1. Produces haploid gametes.
2. Reduces the chromosome number from diploid to haploid.
3. Prevents chromosome number from doubling after each generation.
4. Creates genetic variation.
5. Allows fertilisation to restore the diploid chromosome number.

## 15.2.2 Spermatogenesis

Spermatogenesis is the process of sperm production in the seminiferous tubules of the testes.

It begins at puberty and generally continues throughout adult life.

### Stages of spermatogenesis

**Stage 1: Spermatogonium**

- Spermatogonia are diploid cells (2n).
- They are located near the outer wall of the seminiferous tubules.
- They divide through mitosis.
- Some cells remain as spermatogonia to maintain the supply of germ cells.
- Others differentiate into primary spermatocytes.

**Stage 2: Primary spermatocyte**

- A primary spermatocyte is diploid (2n).
- It replicates its DNA before meiosis.
- It undergoes meiosis I.
- Homologous chromosomes separate.
- Two haploid secondary spermatocytes are produced.

**Stage 3: Secondary spermatocytes**

- Each secondary spermatocyte is haploid (n).
- Each contains duplicated chromosomes at the beginning of meiosis II.
- Sister chromatids separate during meiosis II.
- Two haploid spermatids are formed from each secondary spermatocyte.

**Stage 4: Spermatids**

- Four spermatids are formed from one primary spermatocyte.
- Spermatids are haploid.
- They are not yet fully functional sperm cells.
- They undergo differentiation.

**Stage 5: Spermiogenesis**

Spermiogenesis is the process through which spermatids develop into mature sperm cells.

Changes include:
- The nucleus becomes condensed.
- An acrosome develops from the Golgi apparatus.
- A flagellum develops.
- Excess cytoplasm is removed.
- Mitochondria become concentrated in the middle piece.

**Overall process:**

Spermatogonium (2n) → Primary spermatocyte (2n) → Two secondary spermatocytes (n) → Four spermatids (n) → Four sperm cells (n).

### Structure of a sperm cell

**1. Head**

- Contains the haploid nucleus.
- Contains 23 chromosomes.
- Contains the acrosome.
- The acrosome contains enzymes that help the sperm pass through the outer coverings of the ovum.

**2. Middle piece**

- Contains numerous mitochondria.
- Mitochondria produce ATP through cellular respiration.
- ATP supplies energy for movement of the flagellum.

**3. Tail**

- Also called the flagellum.
- Produces movement.
- Helps propel sperm through the female reproductive tract.

### Adaptations of sperm

| Structure | Adaptation | Advantage |
|---|---|---|
| Head | Haploid nucleus | Contributes one set of chromosomes |
| Acrosome | Contains enzymes | Helps penetrate ovum coverings |
| Middle piece | Many mitochondria | Supplies ATP |
| Tail | Flagellum | Enables movement |
| Overall shape | Streamlined | Reduces resistance during movement |

## 15.2.3 Oogenesis

Oogenesis is the process of producing female gametes in the ovaries.

Unlike spermatogenesis, oogenesis begins before birth.

### Stages of oogenesis

**Stage 1: Oogonium**

- Oogonia are diploid cells (2n).
- They multiply through mitosis during foetal development.
- They develop into primary oocytes.

**Stage 2: Primary oocyte**

- Primary oocytes begin meiosis I before birth.
- They become arrested in prophase I.
- They remain in this state until further development after puberty.

**Stage 3: Completion of meiosis I**

During a reproductive cycle, a selected primary oocyte may complete meiosis I.

It produces:
- One large secondary oocyte.
- One small first polar body.

The division of cytoplasm is unequal.

Most of the cytoplasm is retained in the secondary oocyte because it needs nutrients and organelles to support early development.

**Stage 4: Secondary oocyte**

- The secondary oocyte is haploid (n).
- It begins meiosis II.
- It becomes arrested at metaphase II.
- It is released from the ovary during ovulation.

**Stage 5: Completion of meiosis II**

Meiosis II is completed only if fertilisation occurs.

It produces:
- One mature ovum.
- A second polar body.

The polar bodies usually degenerate.

### Result of oogenesis

One primary oocyte generally produces:
- One functional female gamete.
- Polar bodies that usually degenerate.

### Why does oogenesis produce only one functional ovum?

The cytoplasm divides unequally.

The ovum receives most of the cytoplasm, organelles and stored materials. These support the early stages of embryonic development before implantation.

## 15.2.4 Comparison Between Spermatogenesis and Oogenesis

| Feature | Spermatogenesis | Oogenesis |
|---|---|---|
| Location | Seminiferous tubules of testes | Ovaries |
| Begins | Puberty | Before birth |
| Process | Generally continuous after puberty | Cyclic maturation |
| Cytoplasmic division | Approximately equal | Unequal |
| Products from one primary cell | Four sperm cells | One functional ovum |
| Gamete size | Small | Large |
| Motility | Motile | Non-motile |
| Meiosis completion | Both meiotic divisions occur during sperm formation | Meiosis II completes only after fertilisation |

## 15.2.5 Genetic Variation During Meiosis

Meiosis contributes to genetic variation through two important processes.

**A. Crossing over**

- Occurs during prophase I.
- Homologous chromosomes pair.
- Non-sister chromatids exchange corresponding sections of DNA.
- New combinations of alleles are produced.

**B. Independent assortment**

- Homologous chromosome pairs arrange randomly at metaphase I.
- Their separation during anaphase I creates different combinations of maternal and paternal chromosomes in gametes.

These processes contribute to genetic variation among offspring.

## 15.2.6 Importance of Haploid Gametes

If human gametes were diploid:
- Fertilisation would produce a zygote with 92 chromosomes.
- The chromosome number would increase in every generation.

Meiosis prevents this by producing gametes with 23 chromosomes.

Fertilisation restores the normal diploid number of 46.

# 15.3 MENSTRUAL CYCLE

## 15.3.1 Definition

The menstrual cycle is a series of cyclic changes in the ovaries and uterus that prepares the female reproductive system for possible pregnancy.

An average cycle is approximately 28 days, but normal cycle lengths vary.

Day 1 is the first day of menstrual bleeding.

The menstrual cycle involves:
- Changes in the ovarian follicles.
- Changes in the endometrium.
- Changes in hormone concentrations.
- Ovulation.
- Preparation of the uterus for possible implantation.

## 15.3.2 Main Hormones Involved

Four major hormones regulate the menstrual cycle:

1. Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH).
2. Luteinising hormone (LH).
3. Oestrogen.
4. Progesterone.

### A. Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH)

Source: Anterior pituitary gland.

Functions:
- Stimulates the growth and development of ovarian follicles.
- Supports the development of the oocyte within the follicle.
- Stimulates follicular cells to produce oestrogen.

### B. Luteinising hormone (LH)

Source: Anterior pituitary gland.

Functions:
- A sudden increase in LH causes ovulation.
- Stimulates the formation of the corpus luteum.
- Supports progesterone secretion by the corpus luteum.

### C. Oestrogen

Main source: Developing ovarian follicles.

Functions:
- Repairs the endometrium after menstruation.
- Stimulates thickening of the endometrium.
- Helps regulate FSH and LH secretion.
- Sustained high oestrogen levels before ovulation contribute to the LH surge.

### D. Progesterone

Main source after ovulation: Corpus luteum.

Functions:
- Maintains the thickened endometrium.
- Stimulates secretory activity of the endometrium.
- Prepares the uterus for possible implantation.
- Inhibits further FSH and LH secretion through negative feedback.

## 15.3.3 Ovarian Cycle

The ovarian cycle describes changes occurring in the ovaries.

### Stage 1: Follicular development

- FSH stimulates several ovarian follicles to begin developing.
- The follicles contain developing oocytes.
- Follicular cells produce oestrogen.
- Usually, one follicle becomes dominant and continues to mature.
- The remaining follicles usually undergo atresia.

### Stage 2: Ovulation

- The dominant follicle produces increasing amounts of oestrogen.
- Sustained high oestrogen levels cause positive feedback on the hypothalamus and pituitary gland.
- A large LH surge occurs.
- The LH surge causes the mature follicle to rupture.
- The secondary oocyte is released from the ovary.
- The secondary oocyte enters the fallopian tube.

Ovulation commonly occurs approximately 14 days before the next menstruation, rather than always occurring on Day 14.

### Stage 3: Formation of corpus luteum

- The ruptured follicle develops into the corpus luteum.
- The corpus luteum secretes progesterone and some oestrogen.
- These hormones maintain the endometrium.

### Stage 4: Degeneration of corpus luteum

If pregnancy does not occur:
- The corpus luteum degenerates.
- Progesterone and oestrogen levels decrease.
- The endometrium is no longer maintained.
- Menstruation begins.

## 15.3.4 Uterine Cycle

The uterine cycle describes changes in the endometrium.

### A. Menstrual phase

Approximately Days 1–5 in a typical 28-day cycle.

What happens:
- The corpus luteum from the previous cycle degenerates.
- Progesterone and oestrogen levels fall.
- The endometrium breaks down.
- Blood, mucus and tissue fragments are discharged through the vagina.

Why does menstruation occur?

The endometrium is maintained by ovarian hormones. When progesterone and oestrogen decrease, the endometrium can no longer be maintained and is shed.

### B. Proliferative phase

Approximately Days 6–14 in a typical cycle.

What happens:
- FSH stimulates follicle development.
- Developing follicles secrete oestrogen.
- Oestrogen stimulates regeneration of the endometrium.
- The endometrium becomes thicker.
- Blood vessels and uterine glands develop further.

Purpose:
- Prepares the uterus for possible implantation.

### C. Secretory phase

Approximately Days 15–28 in a typical cycle.

What happens:
- Ovulation has occurred.
- The corpus luteum secretes progesterone.
- The endometrium becomes more vascular.
- Uterine glands become more active and secrete nutrient-rich substances.
- The endometrium is maintained in preparation for implantation.

If pregnancy does not occur:
- The corpus luteum degenerates.
- Progesterone decreases.
- The endometrium breaks down.
- A new menstrual cycle begins.

## 15.3.5 Relationship Between the Ovarian and Uterine Cycles

| Ovarian event | Hormonal change | Uterine response |
|---|---|---|
| Follicular development | Oestrogen increases | Endometrium repairs and thickens |
| Ovulation | LH surge | Endometrium continues developing |
| Corpus luteum formation | Progesterone increases | Endometrium becomes secretory and is maintained |
| Corpus luteum degeneration | Progesterone and oestrogen decrease | Endometrium breaks down |

## 15.3.6 Hormonal Feedback Mechanisms

Hormonal feedback is essential for coordinating the menstrual cycle.

**Negative feedback:**
- Moderate oestrogen levels generally suppress FSH and LH secretion.
- Progesterone suppresses the secretion of FSH and LH after ovulation.
- This helps prevent unnecessary development of additional follicles.

**Positive feedback before ovulation:**
- A sustained high level of oestrogen from the dominant follicle stimulates the hypothalamus and pituitary gland.
- This produces a surge in LH.
- The LH surge triggers ovulation.

This is an important example of positive feedback in the reproductive system.

## 15.3.7 What Happens if Fertilisation Does Not Occur?

1. The secondary oocyte is not fertilised.
2. The corpus luteum eventually degenerates.
3. Progesterone and oestrogen concentrations decrease.
4. The endometrium loses hormonal support.
5. The endometrium breaks down.
6. Menstruation occurs.

## 15.3.8 What Happens if Pregnancy Occurs?

1. Fertilisation occurs in the fallopian tube.
2. The early embryo develops and travels towards the uterus.
3. The blastocyst implants into the endometrium.
4. Developing embryonic tissue produces human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG).
5. hCG helps maintain the corpus luteum during early pregnancy.
6. Progesterone secretion continues.
7. The endometrium is maintained.
8. Menstruation does not occur during an ongoing pregnancy.

Later, the placenta becomes the major source of progesterone and oestrogen.

## 15.3.9 Important Differences

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Menstruation | Shedding of the endometrium |
| Ovulation | Release of a secondary oocyte |
| Fertilisation | Fusion of gamete nuclei |
| Implantation | Embedding of the blastocyst in the endometrium |
| Menstrual cycle | Cyclic ovarian and uterine changes |

# 15.4 DEVELOPMENT OF HUMAN FOETUS

## 15.4.1 Fertilisation

Fertilisation is the fusion of the nucleus of a male gamete with the nucleus of a female gamete.

It usually occurs in the ampulla of the fallopian tube.

### Steps of fertilisation

1. Sperm cells travel through the female reproductive tract.
2. A sperm reaches the secondary oocyte.
3. The acrosome releases enzymes that help the sperm penetrate the outer coverings.
4. The sperm membrane fuses with the oocyte membrane.
5. The sperm nucleus enters the oocyte.
6. Changes to the oocyte membrane and its surrounding structures help prevent additional sperm from entering.
7. The secondary oocyte completes meiosis II.
8. The male and female genetic material combines.
9. A diploid zygote is formed.

**Important:** The secondary oocyte completes meiosis II only after sperm entry.

## 15.4.2 Early Embryonic Development

After fertilisation, the zygote undergoes repeated mitotic divisions.

This series of divisions is called cleavage.

### A. Zygote

- The first diploid cell formed after fertilisation.
- Contains genetic material from both parents.
- Begins dividing by mitosis.

### B. Morula

- A solid ball of cells formed after several cleavage divisions.
- Contains many blastomeres.
- Continues travelling towards the uterus.

### C. Blastocyst

- A hollow, fluid-filled structure.
- Contains an outer layer called the trophoblast.
- Contains an inner cell mass.
- The inner cell mass develops into the embryo.
- The trophoblast contributes to the development of supporting structures, including part of the placenta.

### D. Implantation

Implantation occurs when the blastocyst attaches to and becomes embedded in the endometrium.

Importance:
- Establishes a connection with maternal tissues.
- Allows development of the placenta.
- Supports exchange of nutrients and gases.
- Provides a suitable environment for further development.

## 15.4.3 Embryonic Development and Germ Layers

During early embryonic development, cells differentiate into three primary germ layers.

**1. Ectoderm**

Develops into structures including:
- Epidermis of the skin.
- Brain.
- Spinal cord.
- Many parts of the nervous system.

**2. Mesoderm**

Develops into structures including:
- Muscles.
- Bones.
- Blood.
- Heart.
- Blood vessels.
- Kidneys.
- Reproductive organs.

**3. Endoderm**

Develops into structures including:
- Epithelial lining of much of the digestive tract.
- Epithelial lining of the respiratory tract.
- Liver.
- Pancreas.

The three germ layers provide the foundation for the development of different organs and tissues.

## 15.4.4 Embryo and Foetus

**Embryo:**
- The developing human during the early stages after fertilisation.
- Major body structures and organs begin to form.
- Organ formation is called organogenesis.

**Foetus:**
- The developing human from approximately the ninth week after fertilisation until birth.
- Organs continue growing and maturing.
- Body size and mass increase significantly.

## 15.4.5 Structures Supporting Foetal Development

### A. Placenta

The placenta is a temporary organ that develops between maternal and foetal tissues.

It provides an exchange surface between maternal blood and foetal blood.

**Functions:**

1. Transfers oxygen from maternal blood to foetal blood.
2. Transfers nutrients such as glucose and amino acids to the foetus.
3. Allows carbon dioxide to move from foetal blood to maternal blood.
4. Allows metabolic waste products to move from foetal blood to maternal blood.
5. Produces hormones that help maintain pregnancy.
6. Provides partial protection by acting as a selective barrier.

**Placental exchange:**

- Oxygen moves from maternal blood to foetal blood by diffusion.
- Carbon dioxide moves from foetal blood to maternal blood.
- Glucose and other nutrients cross the placental barrier through appropriate transport mechanisms.
- Waste products pass from foetal blood to maternal blood for removal.

**Important:** Maternal and foetal blood normally do not mix directly. Their substances are exchanged across the placental barrier.

The placenta is not a complete barrier. Some medicines, alcohol, nicotine, harmful chemicals and pathogens can cross it.

### B. Umbilical Cord

The umbilical cord connects the foetus to the placenta.

It contains:
- Two umbilical arteries.
- One umbilical vein.

**Umbilical arteries:**
- Carry blood from the foetus to the placenta.
- Blood is relatively low in oxygen.
- Blood contains carbon dioxide and metabolic waste.

**Umbilical vein:**
- Carries blood from the placenta to the foetus.
- Blood is relatively high in oxygen.
- Blood contains nutrients.

The umbilical vein carries oxygenated blood even though it is a vein.

The umbilical arteries carry deoxygenated blood even though they are arteries.

This is because arteries and veins are classified according to the direction of blood flow relative to the heart, not according to oxygen content.

### C. Amnion

The amnion is a membrane that surrounds the developing embryo and foetus.

It encloses the amniotic cavity containing amniotic fluid.

### D. Amniotic Fluid

Functions:
1. Absorbs shocks and reduces the effects of mechanical impacts.
2. Protects the foetus from physical injury.
3. Allows free movement.
4. Prevents the foetus from sticking to surrounding membranes.
5. Helps maintain a stable environment.
6. Supports normal musculoskeletal development through movement.

### E. Chorion

The chorion is the outer embryonic membrane.

It contributes to the development of the foetal part of the placenta and forms structures involved in exchange between the mother and developing embryo.

### F. Uterus

Functions:
- Houses the developing embryo and foetus.
- Expands as the foetus grows.
- Contains the placenta and amniotic structures during pregnancy.
- Produces powerful muscular contractions during labour.

## 15.4.6 Maternal and Foetal Blood Circulation

Maternal blood enters the spaces surrounding the placental villi.

Foetal blood flows through capillaries inside the chorionic villi.

The thin placental barrier separates the two blood supplies.

Exchange occurs across this barrier.

**Substances moving from mother to foetus:**
- Oxygen.
- Glucose.
- Amino acids.
- Fatty acids.
- Vitamins.
- Water.
- Mineral ions.

**Substances moving from foetus to mother:**
- Carbon dioxide.
- Urea.
- Other metabolic waste products.

## 15.4.7 Foetal Development by Trimesters

Pregnancy is divided into three trimesters.

### First trimester

Approximately Weeks 1–12 of pregnancy.

Major events:
- Fertilisation and implantation occur.
- Embryonic development takes place.
- The three germ layers form.
- Major organs begin developing.
- The heart begins beating early in development.
- Limb structures develop.
- The embryo becomes recognisable as a foetus.

### Second trimester

Approximately Weeks 13–27.

Major events:
- Rapid growth occurs.
- Bones become more developed.
- Muscles strengthen.
- Foetal movement becomes more noticeable.
- External body features become more recognisable.
- Organ systems continue maturing.

### Third trimester

Approximately Week 28 until birth.

Major events:
- The foetus gains considerable body mass.
- The brain develops further.
- The lungs continue maturing.
- Fat accumulates beneath the skin.
- The foetus prepares for life outside the uterus.
- The foetus often moves into a head-down position before birth.

## 15.4.8 Childbirth (Parturition)

Childbirth is the process through which the baby is delivered from the uterus.

It involves coordinated hormonal and muscular changes.

**Stages of childbirth:**

1. Dilation stage
   - Uterine contractions begin.
   - The cervix gradually dilates.
   - The amniotic sac may rupture.

2. Expulsion stage
   - Strong uterine contractions push the baby through the cervix and vagina.
   - The baby is delivered.

3. Placental stage
   - Further uterine contractions detach and expel the placenta and associated membranes.

Oxytocin stimulates uterine contractions and participates in positive feedback during labour.

## 15.4.9 Factors Affecting Foetal Development

### Maternal nutrition

- Protein is needed for tissue formation.
- Iron is needed for haemoglobin production.
- Calcium is needed for bone development.
- Folic acid supports normal neural tube development.
- Iodine supports thyroid hormone production.
- Adequate energy and other nutrients support growth.

### Harmful substances

- Alcohol can interfere with brain and body development.
- Smoking exposes the foetus to harmful substances and reduces oxygen availability.
- Certain drugs and medicines may affect development.
- Some environmental chemicals can harm the developing foetus.

### Maternal health

- Certain infections can affect foetal development.
- Poorly controlled chronic diseases may increase pregnancy risks.
- Regular antenatal care helps identify and manage health problems.

# 15.5 FORMATION OF TWINS

## 15.5.1 Introduction

Twins are two offspring produced from the same pregnancy.

There are two main types:
1. Monozygotic twins (identical twins).
2. Dizygotic twins (fraternal twins).

The main difference is the number of zygotes involved in their formation.

## 15.5.2 Monozygotic Twins (Identical Twins)

Monozygotic twins develop from a single fertilised ovum.

### Formation process

1. One ovum is released from an ovary.
2. One sperm fertilises the ovum.
3. One diploid zygote is formed.
4. During early development, the embryo separates into two groups of cells.
5. Each group develops into an individual embryo.
6. Two offspring develop.

### Characteristics

- Usually have the same biological sex.
- Have almost identical nuclear genetic information.
- Often have similar physical features.
- Usually have very similar blood groups.
- May have different fingerprints because fingerprints are also influenced by developmental conditions.

### Placental arrangements

The arrangement depends on when the embryo separates.

- Early separation may result in two chorions and two amniotic sacs.
- Later separation may result in one shared chorion but two amniotic sacs.
- Even later separation may result in one shared chorion and one shared amniotic sac.
- Very late or incomplete separation can result in conjoined twins.

Not all identical twins share the same placenta or amniotic sac.

## 15.5.3 Dizygotic Twins (Fraternal Twins)

Dizygotic twins develop from two separate ova fertilised by two different sperm cells.

### Formation process

1. Two ova are released during the same reproductive cycle.
2. Each ovum is fertilised by a different sperm.
3. Two separate diploid zygotes are formed.
4. Each zygote develops into an embryo.
5. Two offspring develop in the same pregnancy.

### Characteristics

- Genetically similar to ordinary siblings.
- Share approximately half of their segregating genetic variants on average.
- May be the same biological sex or different biological sexes.
- Usually develop with separate chorions and amniotic sacs.
- Their placentas may appear fused if they implant close together.

## 15.5.4 Comparison Between Monozygotic and Dizygotic Twins

| Feature | Monozygotic | Dizygotic |
|---|---|---|
| Common name | Identical twins | Fraternal twins |
| Number of ova | One | Two |
| Number of sperm | One | Two |
| Number of zygotes | One | Two |
| Genetic similarity | Almost identical | Similar to ordinary siblings |
| Biological sex | Usually the same | Same or different |
| Placental arrangement | Depends on separation timing | Usually separate placentas |

## 15.5.5 Factors Associated with Dizygotic Twinning

The chance of releasing more than one ovum may be influenced by:
- Maternal family history.
- Maternal age.
- Previous pregnancies.
- Certain fertility treatments.

Identical twinning is generally less strongly associated with inherited factors and often occurs without a clear identifiable cause.

## 15.5.6 Higher-Order Multiple Births

Triplets or other multiple births can form through different combinations.

Examples:
- Three separate ova fertilised by three sperm cells.
- One zygote separating into three embryos.
- A combination of monozygotic and dizygotic development.

# 15.6 HEALTH ISSUES RELATED TO THE HUMAN REPRODUCTIVE SYSTEM

## 15.6.1 Infertility

Infertility refers to a reproductive condition in which pregnancy has not occurred after a specified period of regular unprotected sexual intercourse. Clinical definitions commonly use 12 months, although earlier assessment may be appropriate depending on age and medical circumstances.

Infertility can involve male factors, female factors or a combination of both.

### A. Male infertility

Possible causes:

**1. Low sperm count**
- Fewer sperm cells are present in semen.
- This reduces the number of sperm available to reach an ovum.

**2. Poor sperm motility**
- Sperm cells have difficulty moving effectively.
- This reduces their ability to travel through the female reproductive tract.

**3. Abnormal sperm morphology**
- Sperm may have abnormal head, middle-piece or tail structures.
- These abnormalities may affect movement or fertilisation.

**4. Blockage of reproductive ducts**
- Prevents sperm from being transported normally.

**5. Hormonal problems**
- Abnormal FSH, LH or testosterone levels can interfere with sperm production.

**6. Testicular damage**
- Injury, certain infections or excessive heat can affect spermatogenesis.

### B. Female infertility

Possible causes:

**1. Ovulation disorders**
- The ovary may not release an oocyte regularly.
- Hormonal imbalance may interfere with follicular development and ovulation.

**2. Blocked fallopian tubes**
- Prevent sperm and ovum from meeting normally.
- May also interfere with movement of the early embryo.

**3. Endometriosis**
- Tissue similar to the uterine lining grows outside the uterus.
- May cause inflammation, pain and reproductive complications.

**4. Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)**
- A hormonal and metabolic condition.
- May interfere with regular ovulation.

**5. Uterine conditions**
- Certain abnormalities or disorders may affect implantation.

### C. Management of infertility

Depending on the cause:
- Medical investigations.
- Hormonal treatment.
- Treatment of infections or underlying conditions.
- Surgery for selected structural problems.
- Assisted reproductive technologies.

### D. Assisted reproductive technologies

**In vitro fertilisation (IVF)**

IVF is a technique in which fertilisation takes place outside the body under controlled laboratory conditions.

General stages:
1. Medication stimulates the development of ovarian follicles.
2. Mature oocytes are collected.
3. Sperm is collected and prepared.
4. Fertilisation occurs in a laboratory.
5. The resulting embryos are monitored.
6. A suitable embryo may be transferred into the uterus.
7. Implantation may occur if development continues successfully.

**Intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI)**

- A single sperm is injected directly into an oocyte.
- It may be used in selected cases of male-factor infertility.

**Important:** Assisted reproductive technologies do not guarantee pregnancy.

## 15.6.2 Sexually Transmitted Infections (STIs)

STIs are infections that are transmitted mainly through sexual contact.

Some can also spread through infected blood or from an infected mother to her baby.

### A. Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV)

HIV is a virus that attacks the immune system, especially CD4 T lymphocytes.

**Effects:**
- Reduces the number and function of CD4 T cells.
- Weakens immune defence.
- Increases susceptibility to certain opportunistic infections.
- Without effective treatment, HIV can progress to AIDS.

**AIDS:**
Acquired immunodeficiency syndrome is the advanced stage of HIV infection in which severe immune damage has occurred.

**Management:**
- Antiretroviral therapy (ART) suppresses viral replication.
- ART helps preserve immune function.
- People receiving effective treatment can live long and healthy lives.
- Treatment also greatly reduces the risk of sexual transmission.

HIV is not transmitted through ordinary social contact such as sharing food, shaking hands or hugging.

### B. Gonorrhoea

- Caused by the bacterium Neisseria gonorrhoeae.
- Can infect the reproductive tract.
- Symptoms may include unusual discharge and pain during urination.
- Some people have no symptoms.
- Untreated infection may cause pelvic inflammatory disease and infertility.
- Requires appropriate antibiotic treatment.

### C. Syphilis

- Caused by Treponema pallidum.
- May begin with a painless sore.
- Later stages may involve skin, nervous system and organ complications.
- Can be transmitted from an infected pregnant mother to the developing foetus.
- Can be treated with appropriate antibiotics.

### D. Genital herpes

- Commonly caused by herpes simplex virus type 1 or type 2.
- May cause painful blisters or sores.
- Infection can remain in the body and recur.
- Antiviral medicines can reduce symptoms and outbreaks but do not eliminate the virus.

### E. Human papillomavirus (HPV)

- A group of viruses that infect the skin and mucous membranes.
- Some types cause genital warts.
- Certain high-risk types can cause cervical and other cancers.
- HPV vaccination reduces the risk of infection from important cancer-causing types.

### F. Chlamydia

- Caused by Chlamydia trachomatis.
- Frequently causes no noticeable symptoms.
- May cause abnormal discharge or pain during urination.
- Untreated infection can lead to reproductive complications.
- Can be treated with appropriate antibiotics.

## 15.6.3 Prevention of STIs

1. HPV and hepatitis B vaccination according to health recommendations.
2. Correct and consistent condom use reduces the risk of many STIs.
3. STI screening when appropriate.
4. Early diagnosis and treatment.
5. Avoid sharing needles or equipment contaminated with blood.
6. Seeking medical advice after possible exposure.
7. Understanding how infections are transmitted.

Not all STIs have vaccines, and not all infections cause visible symptoms.

## 15.6.4 Menstrual Disorders

Menstrual disorders are conditions involving abnormal menstrual bleeding, timing or pain.

### A. Dysmenorrhoea

Dysmenorrhoea refers to painful menstruation.

Possible causes:
- Prostaglandins stimulate uterine contractions.
- Strong contractions may temporarily reduce blood flow to uterine tissue.
- This can cause pain and cramping.

Management may include medical assessment, suitable pain relief and treatment of underlying causes.

### B. Amenorrhoea

Amenorrhoea is the absence of menstruation.

Possible causes:
- Pregnancy.
- Hormonal disorders.
- Certain medical conditions.
- Major changes in body energy availability.
- Stress or excessive physical demands.

### C. Menorrhagia

Menorrhagia refers to unusually heavy or prolonged menstrual bleeding.

Possible effects:
- Iron deficiency.
- Anaemia.
- Fatigue.
- Reduced quality of life.

### D. Irregular Menstrual Cycles

Possible causes:
- Hormonal changes.
- PCOS.
- Thyroid disorders.
- Stress.
- Certain illnesses.
- Changes in reproductive development.

Persistent or severe menstrual symptoms should be assessed by a healthcare professional.

## 15.6.5 Other Reproductive Health Conditions

### A. Endometriosis

- Tissue similar to endometrium grows outside the uterus.
- Can cause pelvic pain.
- May cause painful menstruation.
- Can contribute to infertility.

### B. Cervical cancer

- Many cases are associated with persistent infection by high-risk HPV types.
- Abnormal cells develop in the cervix.
- Screening can detect precancerous changes.
- HPV vaccination reduces the risk of important HPV-related cancers.

### C. Testicular cancer

- Cancer develops in the testicular tissue.
- May cause a lump, swelling or discomfort.
- Early medical assessment is important.

## 15.6.6 Reproductive Health Protection

- Maintain personal hygiene.
- Understand normal reproductive changes.
- Seek medical attention for unusual symptoms.
- Follow recommended vaccination schedules.
- Attend appropriate health screenings.
- Avoid smoking and harmful substances.
- Obtain accurate health information from qualified professionals.

# 15.7 GROWTH IN HUMANS AND ANIMALS

## 15.7.1 Definition of Growth

Growth is a permanent and irreversible increase in an organism's size, dry mass or number of cells.

Growth involves:
1. Cell division.
2. Cell enlargement.
3. Cell differentiation.
4. Development of tissues and organs.

### Cell division

Mitosis increases the number of cells.

### Cell enlargement

Cells increase in size through the synthesis of cellular components and accumulation of materials.

### Cell differentiation

Unspecialised cells develop specialised structures and functions.

These processes contribute to the formation and growth of tissues and organs.

## 15.7.2 Difference Between Growth and Development

**Growth:**
- Increase in size, dry mass or cell number.
- Can be measured quantitatively.
- Usually involves cell division and enlargement.

**Development:**
- Changes in structure, function and complexity.
- Includes differentiation and maturation.
- Can occur without a large increase in body size.

Example:

A child increasing in height demonstrates growth.

A child developing improved coordination and more specialised body functions demonstrates development.

## 15.7.3 Growth in Humans

Human growth is generally continuous, although its rate varies at different stages of life.

### A. Infancy

- Very rapid increase in body mass and length.
- Brain develops rapidly.
- Muscles and bones grow.
- Motor skills gradually develop.
- The digestive and immune systems continue maturing.

### B. Childhood

- Body size increases.
- Bones lengthen.
- Muscles develop.
- Coordination improves.
- Cognitive and physical development continue.

### C. Adolescence

Adolescence is a period of rapid physical and sexual development.

Important changes:
- Puberty begins.
- Sex hormones increase.
- Secondary sexual characteristics develop.
- Reproductive organs mature.
- A growth spurt occurs.
- Bone and muscle development accelerate.

**Growth hormone:**
- Secreted by the anterior pituitary gland.
- Stimulates growth of bones and other tissues.
- Acts partly through insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1).

**Thyroxine:**
- Secreted by the thyroid gland.
- Supports normal metabolism and growth.
- Is important for normal development.

**Sex hormones:**
- Testosterone and oestrogen contribute to pubertal changes.
- They help produce the adolescent growth spurt.
- They also contribute to the eventual closure of growth plates.

### D. Adulthood

- Most individuals have completed growth in height.
- Epiphyseal growth plates have closed.
- Cells continue to divide for maintenance and repair.
- Bone and muscle tissues continue undergoing remodelling.

### E. Old Age

- Some physiological functions gradually decline.
- Muscle mass may decrease.
- Bone density may decrease.
- Tissue repair may become slower.
- Cells continue to undergo replacement and maintenance.

## 15.7.4 Human Growth at the Bone Level

Long bones increase in length at the epiphyseal growth plates.

The growth process involves:

1. Cartilage cells divide at the growth plate.
2. New cartilage is formed.
3. Older cartilage cells enlarge.
4. Cartilage is gradually replaced by bone tissue.
5. The bone increases in length.

During late adolescence and adulthood, the growth plates close.

Once they close, bones generally cannot increase in length through this process.

Bones can still remodel throughout life.

## 15.7.5 Factors Affecting Human Growth

### A. Genetic factors

- Genes influence height potential.
- Genes influence body structure.
- Genetic conditions can affect growth and development.

### B. Nutrition

**Protein:**
- Needed for the formation of muscles, enzymes and body tissues.

**Calcium and phosphorus:**
- Important for bone mineralisation.

**Vitamin D:**
- Helps the body absorb calcium.
- Supports normal bone development.

**Iron:**
- Needed for haemoglobin production and oxygen transport.

**Iodine:**
- Needed for thyroid hormone production.

**Energy:**
- Supports metabolism, cell division and growth.

### C. Hormonal factors

Important hormones:
- Growth hormone.
- Thyroxine.
- Insulin.
- Oestrogen.
- Testosterone.

An imbalance in these hormones may interfere with normal growth.

### D. Environmental and health factors

- Sleep.
- Physical activity.
- General health.
- Chronic diseases.
- Stress.
- Access to adequate nutrition.

## 15.7.6 Continuous Growth in Animals

Continuous growth refers to growth that occurs gradually over time without requiring the animal to shed an entire rigid external skeleton.

Examples include many mammals and birds.

Characteristics:
- Body size increases through cell division and enlargement.
- The internal skeleton grows along with the body.
- Growth rate is usually higher during early life.
- Growth gradually slows as the animal approaches maturity.

Examples:
- Humans.
- Cats.
- Dogs.
- Cows.
- Birds.

## 15.7.7 Discontinuous Growth in Animals

Discontinuous growth occurs in many arthropods, such as insects and crustaceans.

These animals possess an exoskeleton.

The rigid exoskeleton:
- Provides support.
- Protects internal organs.
- Provides attachment points for muscles.
- Restricts continuous expansion of the body.

Therefore, growth requires the animal to shed its old exoskeleton.

This process is called ecdysis or moulting.

### Stages of moulting

1. The old exoskeleton separates from the underlying epidermis.
2. A new exoskeleton begins forming underneath.
3. The old exoskeleton is shed.
4. The animal expands its body while the new exoskeleton is soft.
5. The new exoskeleton hardens.

The animal is particularly vulnerable during the period when its new exoskeleton is soft.

### Why is moulting necessary?

The rigid exoskeleton cannot expand continuously with the growing animal.

Shedding the old exoskeleton provides space for body expansion.

## 15.7.8 Complete Metamorphosis

Complete metamorphosis involves four stages:

Egg → Larva → Pupa → Adult.

Example: Butterfly.

### A. Egg

- Embryonic development occurs inside the egg.
- The larva emerges after development.

### B. Larva

- The larva is specialised for feeding and growth.
- It consumes food and increases in size.
- It undergoes several moults.
- Its body structure differs significantly from that of the adult.

Example: Caterpillar.

### C. Pupa

- The insect enters a stage of major transformation.
- Adult structures develop.
- Body tissues undergo extensive reorganisation.
- The pupa generally does not feed.

Example: Chrysalis in butterflies.

### D. Adult

- The adult emerges from the pupal case.
- Wings and reproductive structures are developed.
- Adults are responsible for reproduction.

Other examples:
- Mosquitoes.
- Houseflies.
- Beetles.

## 15.7.9 Incomplete Metamorphosis

Incomplete metamorphosis involves three stages:

Egg → Nymph → Adult.

Examples:
- Grasshopper.
- Cockroach.
- Dragonfly.

### A. Egg

Embryonic development occurs inside the egg.

### B. Nymph

- Resembles a smaller version of the adult.
- Does not usually have fully developed wings or reproductive organs.
- Feeds and grows.
- Undergoes repeated moulting.

### C. Adult

- Has fully developed reproductive organs.
- May have fully developed wings.
- Can reproduce.

There is no pupal stage.

## 15.7.10 Comparison Between Complete and Incomplete Metamorphosis

| Feature | Complete metamorphosis | Incomplete metamorphosis |
|---|---|---|
| Number of stages | Four | Three |
| Stages | Egg, larva, pupa, adult | Egg, nymph, adult |
| Larval stage | Present | Absent |
| Pupal stage | Present | Absent |
| Nymph stage | Absent | Present |
| Appearance of immature stage | Different from adult | Resembles adult |
| Example | Butterfly | Grasshopper |

## 15.7.11 Growth Curves

A growth curve shows the relationship between a growth measurement and time.

Growth can be measured using:
- Height.
- Length.
- Body mass.
- Dry mass.
- Cell number.

### A. Human growth curve

Human growth generally shows a sigmoid pattern.

**Phase 1: Infancy**
- Rapid growth.
- Cells divide and enlarge.
- Body mass and length increase quickly.

**Phase 2: Childhood**
- Growth continues at a more moderate rate.
- Height increases steadily.

**Phase 3: Adolescence**
- Rapid growth occurs during puberty.
- Hormones stimulate increased growth of bones and other tissues.

**Phase 4: Adulthood**
- Growth in height slows and stops.
- The growth curve reaches a plateau.

### B. Insect growth curve

Many insects show a step-like growth curve.

Between moults:
- The rigid exoskeleton restricts body expansion.
- Body size increases very little.

During and immediately after moulting:
- The old exoskeleton is shed.
- The body expands.
- The new exoskeleton hardens.

This produces sudden increases in body size separated by periods of limited external growth.

## 15.7.12 How to Interpret Growth Curves

When analysing a graph:

1. Identify the x-axis and y-axis.
2. Identify the growth measurement.
3. Identify periods of rapid growth.
4. Identify periods of slow growth or a plateau.
5. Compare the growth patterns.
6. Explain the biological reason for the pattern.

**Important comparison:**

Human growth:
- Generally continuous.
- No shedding of the internal skeleton.
- Growth slows as maturity is reached.

Insect growth:
- Often discontinuous.
- Rigid exoskeleton restricts expansion.
- Growth increases significantly after moulting.

# CHAPTER 15: IMPORTANT BIOLOGICAL COMPARISONS

## A. Fertilisation vs Implantation

| Fertilisation | Implantation |
|---|---|
| Fusion of gamete nuclei | Embedding of blastocyst |
| Usually occurs in fallopian tube | Occurs in endometrium |
| Produces a zygote | Establishes attachment to maternal tissue |

## B. Menstruation vs Ovulation

| Menstruation | Ovulation |
|---|---|
| Endometrium is shed | Secondary oocyte is released |
| Caused by decreased ovarian hormones | Triggered by LH surge |
| Occurs at the beginning of a typical cycle | Usually occurs around the middle of a typical 28-day cycle |

## C. Sperm vs Ovum

| Sperm | Ovum |
|---|---|
| Small | Large |
| Motile | Non-motile |
| Has a flagellum | Does not have a flagellum |
| Has an acrosome | Has protective outer coverings |
| Very little cytoplasm | Large amount of cytoplasm |
| Produced in testes | Produced in ovaries |

## D. Monozygotic vs Dizygotic Twins

| Monozygotic | Dizygotic |
|---|---|
| One zygote | Two zygotes |
| One ovum fertilised | Two ova fertilised |
| One sperm involved in initial fertilisation | Two sperm involved |
| Almost identical nuclear genes | Genetic similarity like ordinary siblings |
| Usually same biological sex | Same or different biological sex |

## E. Continuous vs Discontinuous Growth

| Continuous growth | Discontinuous growth |
|---|---|
| Gradual increase in size | Increase occurs in stages |
| Common in many vertebrates | Common in arthropods |
| Internal skeleton grows with body | Rigid exoskeleton must be shed |
| No whole-body exoskeleton moulting | Moulting occurs |

# CHAPTER 15: IMPORTANT DEFINITIONS

1. Sexual reproduction: Reproduction involving the fusion of male and female gametes.
2. Gametogenesis: Formation of haploid gametes.
3. Spermatogenesis: Formation of sperm cells.
4. Oogenesis: Formation of female gametes.
5. Fertilisation: Fusion of male and female gamete nuclei.
6. Zygote: Diploid cell formed after fertilisation.
7. Ovulation: Release of a secondary oocyte from the ovary.
8. Menstruation: Shedding of the endometrium.
9. Implantation: Attachment and embedding of the blastocyst in the endometrium.
10. Placenta: Temporary organ that facilitates exchange between maternal and foetal blood.
11. Monozygotic twins: Twins developing from one zygote.
12. Dizygotic twins: Twins developing from two zygotes.
13. Infertility: Difficulty achieving pregnancy.
14. Growth: Permanent and irreversible increase in size, dry mass or cell number.
15. Development: Changes in structure and function as an organism matures.
16. Ecdysis: Shedding of the old exoskeleton.
17. Metamorphosis: Transformation through distinct developmental stages.
18. Cleavage: Rapid mitotic divisions of the early embryo.
19. Morula: Solid ball of cells formed during early embryonic development.
20. Blastocyst: Early embryonic structure containing an inner cell mass and outer trophoblast.
21. Parturition: The process of childbirth.
22. Corpus luteum: Temporary endocrine structure formed from the ruptured ovarian follicle.
23. Spermiogenesis: Differentiation of spermatids into sperm cells.
24. Atresia: Degeneration of ovarian follicles that do not continue development.

1. Fertilisation usually occurs in the fallopian tube, not the uterus.

4. The epididymis is responsible for sperm maturation and temporary storage.
5. The scrotum maintains a suitable temperature for spermatogenesis.
6. One primary spermatocyte produces four haploid sperm cells.
7. One primary oocyte generally produces one functional ovum.
8. Spermatogenesis begins at puberty, whereas oogenesis begins before birth.
9. The LH surge triggers ovulation; FSH stimulates follicular development.
10. Progesterone is important for maintaining the endometrium after ovulation.

12. Menstruation occurs when ovarian hormone levels decrease and the endometrium breaks down.
13. The secondary oocyte completes meiosis II only after fertilisation.
14. The placenta allows exchange but maternal and foetal blood normally do not mix directly.
15. The umbilical vein carries oxygenated blood towards the foetus.
16. The umbilical arteries carry relatively deoxygenated blood towards the placenta.
17. Identical twins develop from one zygote; fraternal twins develop from two.
18. The placenta does not completely prevent harmful substances from reaching the foetus.
19. Growth and development are related but are not identical.
20. Insects with rigid exoskeletons require moulting to increase in size.
21. Complete metamorphosis has a pupal stage; incomplete metamorphosis does not.
22. Human growth is not equally rapid at every life stage.

(a)
- One ovum is fertilised by one sperm.
- One zygote is formed.
- The early embryo separates into two groups of cells, developing into two individuals.

(b)
- Two ova are released.
- Each ovum is fertilised by a different sperm.
- Two separate zygotes develop into two individuals.

(c)
- Monozygotic twins develop from one zygote, whereas dizygotic twins develop from two.
- Monozygotic twins are genetically almost identical, whereas dizygotic twins are genetically similar to ordinary siblings.

### Question 6: Reproductive Health

(a) Explain how HIV affects the human immune system. [3 marks]

(b) State two ways to reduce the transmission of STIs. [2 marks]

(c) Explain how untreated reproductive tract infections may cause infertility. [3 marks]

**Suggested answer guidance:**

(a)
- HIV infects CD4 T lymphocytes.
- It damages immune function.
- This reduces the body's ability to defend itself against infections.

(b)
- Appropriate vaccination.
- Correct and consistent condom use.
- Screening and early treatment are also acceptable.

(c)
- Some infections cause inflammation in reproductive organs.
- Inflammation may cause scarring or blockage of reproductive ducts or fallopian tubes.
- This can interfere with gamete transport or fertilisation.

### Question 7: Growth in Humans and Animals

(a) Define growth. [2 marks]

(b) Explain why insects undergo moulting. [3 marks]

(c) Compare complete and incomplete metamorphosis. [4 marks]

**Suggested answer guidance:**

(a)
- Permanent and irreversible increase in size, dry mass or number of cells.

(b)
- Insects possess a rigid exoskeleton.
- The exoskeleton restricts continuous expansion.
- The old exoskeleton must be shed to allow body expansion.

(c)
- Complete metamorphosis has four stages: egg, larva, pupa and adult.
- Incomplete metamorphosis has three stages: egg, nymph and adult.
- Complete metamorphosis includes a pupal stage.
- Incomplete metamorphosis has no pupal stage.

### Question 8: Hormonal Changes

A woman has a normal menstrual cycle. Her corpus luteum degenerates because fertilisation and implantation have not occurred.

Explain how this affects the endometrium. [4 marks]

**Answer guidance:**
- The corpus luteum stops producing high levels of progesterone.
- Progesterone concentration decreases.
- The endometrium loses hormonal support.
- The endometrium breaks down and menstruation occurs.

### Question 9: Placental Exchange

A pregnant mother is exposed to a harmful substance that can cross the placental barrier.

Explain why the developing foetus may be affected. [3 marks]

**Answer guidance:**
- The placenta is a selective exchange surface.
- It does not completely prevent harmful substances from crossing.
- The substance may enter foetal circulation and interfere with normal development.

### Question 10: Growth Curves

An insect's body mass increases significantly after each moult, while its external body size changes very little between moults.

Explain this pattern. [4 marks]

**Answer guidance:**
- The insect has a rigid exoskeleton.
- The exoskeleton restricts external expansion.
- The old exoskeleton is shed during moulting.
- The insect expands before the new exoskeleton hardens.
