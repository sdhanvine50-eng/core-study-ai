# 8.1 CLASSIFICATION SYSTEM AND NAMING OF ORGANISMS

## 8.1.1 Introduction to Classification

Classification is the process of organising organisms into groups based on their similarities and differences.

Scientists classify organisms to make the study of the enormous diversity of living things easier and more systematic.

There are millions of known species, and many more may still be undiscovered. Without classification, identifying and studying organisms would be difficult.

### Importance of Classification

1. Makes the study of organisms more organised.
2. Helps scientists identify organisms accurately.
3. Shows similarities and differences between organisms.
4. Helps scientists understand evolutionary relationships.
5. Provides a universal system for communicating about organisms.
6. Reduces confusion caused by different common names in different languages and regions.

**Example:**

The organism commonly called a house cat may have different names in different languages. Its scientific name, *Felis catus*, is used internationally.

## 8.1.2 Taxonomy

Taxonomy is the branch of biology concerned with identifying, naming and classifying organisms.

The three main activities in taxonomy are:

- Identification: determining the identity of an organism.
- Nomenclature: assigning scientific names to organisms.
- Classification: arranging organisms into groups according to shared characteristics.

A taxon is a group of organisms at any level of classification.

## 8.1.3 Taxonomic Hierarchy

Organisms are classified into several levels, from the broadest group to the most specific group.

The commonly used hierarchy is:

**Domain → Kingdom → Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species**

For plants, the term division is commonly used instead of phylum.

### Explanation of Each Level

**1. Domain**

- The broadest major classification level in the three-domain system.
- Organisms are grouped according to fundamental cellular and molecular characteristics.
- The three domains are Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya.

**2. Kingdom**

- A major group within a domain.
- Organisms share broad characteristics such as cell organisation and nutritional features.

**3. Phylum**

- Groups organisms that share important structural and organisational features.
- For plants, this level is commonly called division.

**4. Class**

- A subdivision of a phylum.

**5. Order**

- A subdivision of a class.

**6. Family**

- A subdivision of an order.
- Organisms in the same family share more characteristics than organisms that only belong to the same order.

**7. Genus**

- A group of closely related species.
- Organisms in the same genus share many characteristics.

**8. Species**

- The most specific basic classification unit.
- In the biological species concept, a species generally consists of natural populations whose members can interbreed and produce fertile offspring.

### Example: Classification of Humans

| Taxonomic level | Human classification |
|---|---|
| Domain | Eukarya |
| Kingdom | Animalia |
| Phylum | Chordata |
| Class | Mammalia |
| Order | Primates |
| Family | Hominidae |
| Genus | Homo |
| Species | Homo sapiens |

### Important Relationship

As classification moves from domain towards species:

- The number of organisms in each group generally decreases.
- The similarities between organisms generally increase.
- The characteristics shared by organisms become more specific.

**Exam tip:**

If asked why organisms in the same genus are more closely related than organisms in the same family, explain that a genus is a more specific taxonomic level and its members share more characteristics.

## 8.1.4 Three-Domain Classification System

The three-domain system divides organisms into three major domains.

### A. Domain Bacteria

Characteristics:

- Prokaryotic organisms.
- Usually unicellular.
- No membrane-bound nucleus.
- DNA is located in a nucleoid region.
- Most have a cell wall containing peptidoglycan.
- Reproduce mainly through binary fission.

Examples:

- *Escherichia coli*
- *Lactobacillus*
- Cyanobacteria

### B. Domain Archaea

Characteristics:

- Prokaryotic organisms.
- Usually unicellular.
- No membrane-bound nucleus.
- Cell walls do not contain bacterial peptidoglycan.
- Their cell membrane chemistry differs from that of bacteria.
- Some live in extreme environments, although many live in ordinary environments.

Examples:

- Methanogens.
- Halophilic archaea.
- Thermophilic archaea.

**Important:** Not all archaea live in extreme environments.

### C. Domain Eukarya

Characteristics:

- Cells have a membrane-bound nucleus.
- Cells contain membrane-bound organelles.
- Includes unicellular and multicellular organisms.

Examples:

- Animals.
- Plants.
- Fungi.
- Protists.

### Comparison of the Three Domains

| Feature | Bacteria | Archaea | Eukarya |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cell type | Prokaryotic | Prokaryotic | Eukaryotic |
| True nucleus | Absent | Absent | Present |
| Membrane-bound organelles | Absent | Absent | Present |
| Cell wall | Usually peptidoglycan | No peptidoglycan | Varies |
| Organisation | Usually unicellular | Usually unicellular | Unicellular or multicellular |

## 8.1.5 Five-Kingdom Classification System

A commonly taught classification system groups organisms into five kingdoms.

### 1. Kingdom Monera

Traditional classification group containing prokaryotic organisms.

Characteristics:

- Unicellular.
- No true nucleus.
- No membrane-bound organelles.
- Includes many bacteria.

**Note:** Modern classification separates Bacteria and Archaea into different domains. Monera is a traditional grouping.

### 2. Kingdom Protista

Characteristics:

- Eukaryotic.
- Mostly unicellular, although some are multicellular or colonial.
- Some are photosynthetic.
- Others obtain nutrients from organic substances.

Examples:

- Amoeba.
- Paramecium.
- Euglena.

### 3. Kingdom Fungi

Characteristics:

- Eukaryotic.
- Most are multicellular; yeasts are unicellular.
- Cell walls contain chitin.
- Do not contain chlorophyll.
- Obtain nutrients by absorption.
- Many act as decomposers.

Examples:

- Mushrooms.
- Yeast.
- Moulds.

### 4. Kingdom Plantae

Characteristics:

- Eukaryotic.
- Mostly multicellular.
- Cell walls contain cellulose.
- Usually contain chloroplasts.
- Carry out photosynthesis.
- Store carbohydrates mainly as starch.

Examples:

- Mosses.
- Ferns.
- Flowering plants.

### 5. Kingdom Animalia

Characteristics:

- Eukaryotic.
- Multicellular.
- Cells do not have cell walls.
- Obtain nutrients by ingestion.
- Most can move during at least one stage of their life cycle.
- Usually have specialised tissues and organs.

Examples:

- Humans.
- Fish.
- Insects.
- Birds.

## 8.1.6 Binomial Nomenclature

Binomial nomenclature is the scientific system of naming organisms using two words.

It was developed and popularised by Carl Linnaeus.

The two parts are:

1. Genus name.
2. Specific epithet.

Together, they form the scientific name of a species.

**Example:**

*Homo sapiens*

- *Homo* = genus.
- *sapiens* = specific epithet.
- *Homo sapiens* = complete scientific name.

Other examples:

- *Panthera leo* — lion.
- *Panthera tigris* — tiger.
- *Oryza sativa* — rice.
- *Escherichia coli* — bacterium.

### Rules of Binomial Nomenclature

1. Each scientific name consists of two words.
2. The genus name begins with a capital letter.
3. The specific epithet begins with a lowercase letter.
4. Both words are written in italics when typed.
5. When handwritten, each word is underlined separately.
6. Scientific names are generally Latin or Latinised.
7. The scientific name is used internationally.

**Correct:** *Homo sapiens*

**Incorrect:** homo Sapiens

**Incorrect:** *Homo Sapiens*

### Advantages of Binomial Nomenclature

- Provides a universal name.
- Avoids confusion caused by different common names.
- Identifies the genus and species.
- Helps scientists communicate accurately.
- Allows organisms to be organised systematically.

## 8.1.7 Identification of Organisms Using a Dichotomous Key

A dichotomous key is a tool used to identify organisms through a series of paired statements.

The word dichotomous means divided into two parts.

At each step, two contrasting characteristics are presented. The user selects the statement that matches the organism and follows the indicated direction.

### How to Use a Dichotomous Key

1. Observe the organism carefully.
2. Read the first pair of statements.
3. Select the statement that matches the organism.
4. Follow the instruction or number given.
5. Continue selecting between paired statements.
6. Repeat until the organism is identified.

### Example of a Simple Dichotomous Key

Suppose four organisms have these characteristics:

- Organism A: has wings and six legs.
- Organism B: has six legs but no wings.
- Organism C: has eight legs.
- Organism D: has no legs.

A simplified key:

1a. Organism has legs → Go to 2  
1b. Organism has no legs → Organism D

2a. Organism has eight legs → Organism C  
2b. Organism has six legs → Go to 3

3a. Organism has wings → Organism A  
3b. Organism has no wings → Organism B

### Characteristics of a Good Dichotomous Key

- Uses clear and observable characteristics.
- Provides two contrasting choices at each step.
- Avoids vague descriptions.
- Uses characteristics that can be reliably identified.
- Leads to one identification for each organism.

**Exam tip:**

When constructing a dichotomous key, use paired statements based on opposite or contrasting characteristics.

# 8.2 BIODIVERSITY

## 8.2.1 Definition of Biodiversity

Biodiversity refers to the variety of living organisms in an area or on Earth.

It includes variation at three main levels:

1. Genetic diversity.
2. Species diversity.
3. Ecosystem diversity.

Biodiversity is important because organisms interact with one another and with their physical environment.

## 8.2.2 Levels of Biodiversity

### A. Genetic Diversity

Genetic diversity refers to the variation in genetic information among individuals of the same species.

Individuals of the same species may have different inherited characteristics because they possess different combinations of alleles.

Examples:

- Different varieties of rice.
- Different breeds of domestic dogs.
- Differences in flower colour among plants of the same species.
- Differences in inherited characteristics among humans.

**Importance:**

1. Increases the ability of populations to adapt to environmental changes.
2. Provides variation for natural selection.
3. May increase resistance to diseases.
4. Helps maintain the long-term survival of populations.

**Example:**

A crop population with greater genetic variation may contain some individuals that are more resistant to a particular disease.

### B. Species Diversity

Species diversity refers to the variety of species in a particular habitat or ecosystem.

It considers:

- Species richness: the number of different species.
- Species evenness: how evenly individuals are distributed among those species.

An ecosystem with many species and a relatively balanced distribution of individuals generally has higher species diversity than one dominated by only a few species.

**Example:**

A tropical rainforest may contain many species of plants, insects, birds, mammals, fungi and microorganisms.

### C. Ecosystem Diversity

Ecosystem diversity refers to the variety of ecosystems or habitats within a region.

Examples:

- Tropical rainforests.
- Mangrove forests.
- Coral reefs.
- Rivers.
- Lakes.
- Peat swamps.
- Grasslands.

Different ecosystems support different communities of organisms because environmental conditions vary.

### Comparison of the Three Levels

| Level | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Genetic diversity | Variation within a species | Different rice varieties |
| Species diversity | Variety of species in an area | Many species in a rainforest |
| Ecosystem diversity | Variety of ecosystems | Forests, rivers and coral reefs |

## 8.2.3 Importance of Biodiversity

Biodiversity provides ecological, economic, scientific and social benefits.

### A. Ecological Importance

1. Maintains ecosystem stability.
2. Supports food webs and food chains.
3. Helps maintain nutrient cycles.
4. Supports pollination.
5. Helps regulate populations through predator-prey relationships.
6. Supports decomposition and soil formation.

**Example:**

Decomposers break down dead organisms and return nutrients to the environment. These nutrients can then be absorbed by plants.

### B. Economic Importance

Biodiversity provides natural resources that support human activities.

Examples:

- Food from plants and animals.
- Timber and other forest products.
- Natural fibres.
- Medicines and useful biological compounds.
- Materials used in agriculture and industry.

**Example:**

Some medicines are developed from chemical compounds originally discovered in plants, fungi and microorganisms.

### C. Scientific and Educational Importance

1. Provides organisms for scientific research.
2. Helps scientists study evolution and adaptation.
3. Supports the development of new medicines and technologies.
4. Helps researchers understand ecosystem functions.

### D. Social and Recreational Importance

1. Supports ecotourism.
2. Provides opportunities for recreation.
3. Has cultural and educational value.
4. Supports the identity and traditions of some communities.

## 8.2.4 Threats to Biodiversity

Biodiversity can decrease when habitats are destroyed, populations become too small or organisms are removed faster than they can reproduce.

### A. Habitat Destruction

Habitat destruction occurs when natural environments are removed or severely damaged.

Causes include:

- Deforestation.
- Urban development.
- Agricultural expansion.
- Mining.
- Construction of roads and dams.

Effects:

1. Organisms lose food and shelter.
2. Breeding sites may be destroyed.
3. Populations become smaller.
4. Food webs are disrupted.
5. Some species may become endangered or extinct.

### B. Habitat Fragmentation

Habitat fragmentation occurs when a large continuous habitat is divided into smaller isolated areas.

Causes include:

- Roads.
- Farms.
- Buildings.
- Other forms of development.

Effects:

1. Organisms may become separated into small populations.
2. Movement between populations becomes difficult.
3. Gene flow may decrease.
4. Inbreeding may become more likely in small isolated populations.
5. Populations may become more vulnerable to extinction.

**Important distinction:**

Habitat destruction removes or damages habitat, whereas habitat fragmentation divides habitat into separate patches.

### C. Pollution

Pollution is the introduction of harmful substances or forms of energy into the environment.

Types include:

- Water pollution.
- Air pollution.
- Soil pollution.
- Noise pollution.
- Thermal pollution.

Effects:

1. Toxic substances may poison organisms.
2. Water pollution may reduce dissolved oxygen.
3. Fertiliser runoff may cause eutrophication.
4. Air pollution may damage plant tissues.
5. Noise pollution may interfere with animal communication and behaviour.

**Example: Eutrophication**

1. Excess fertilisers enter a water body.
2. Nutrient levels increase.
3. Algae grow rapidly, forming an algal bloom.
4. Algae and other organisms die.
5. Decomposers break down the dead organic matter.
6. Decomposition uses dissolved oxygen.
7. Oxygen levels decrease.
8. Aquatic organisms may die due to insufficient oxygen.

### D. Climate Change

Climate change involves long-term changes in climate patterns.

It can be influenced by increased greenhouse gas concentrations caused by human activities.

Effects on biodiversity include:

1. Changes in temperature and rainfall.
2. Changes in the distribution of species.
3. Loss of suitable habitats.
4. Changes in breeding and migration patterns.
5. Increased stress on organisms that cannot adapt or move.

### E. Overexploitation

Overexploitation occurs when organisms are harvested or removed from their environment at a rate faster than their populations can recover.

Examples:

- Overfishing.
- Excessive hunting.
- Illegal wildlife trade.
- Excessive logging.

Effects:

1. Population sizes decrease.
2. Reproductive populations may become too small.
3. Food webs may be disrupted.
4. Some species may become endangered or extinct.

### F. Invasive Alien Species

An invasive alien species is a non-native organism that becomes established and spreads in a new environment, causing or potentially causing harm.

Effects may include:

- Competition with native organisms.
- Predation on native species.
- Introduction of diseases.
- Changes to habitats and food webs.

**Important:** Not every non-native species becomes invasive.

## 8.2.5 Conservation of Biodiversity

Conservation is the protection, management and sustainable use of biodiversity.

Its purpose is to maintain species, genetic variation and ecosystems for present and future generations.

There are two major approaches.

### A. In Situ Conservation

In situ conservation means conserving organisms in their natural habitats.

Methods include:

**1. National Parks**

- Protect natural ecosystems.
- Restrict activities that damage habitats.
- Provide areas where wildlife populations can survive.

**2. Wildlife Reserves**

- Protect wild animal populations.
- Reduce threats such as hunting and habitat destruction.

**3. Marine Protected Areas**

- Protect marine habitats and organisms.
- Restrict damaging activities in designated areas.
- Help fish populations and coral reef ecosystems recover.

**4. Forest Reserves**

- Protect forest ecosystems.
- Help maintain habitats and ecological processes.

**Advantages of In Situ Conservation**

1. Protects organisms within their natural ecosystems.
2. Conserves interactions between species.
3. Maintains natural breeding and behavioural patterns.
4. Can protect many species at the same time.

**Limitations**

1. Habitats may still be affected by climate change or pollution.
2. Illegal activities may be difficult to control.
3. Large areas may require significant management resources.

### B. Ex Situ Conservation

Ex situ conservation means conserving organisms outside their natural habitats.

Methods include:

**1. Zoos**

- Maintain and care for certain animal populations.
- May support breeding and educational programmes.

**2. Botanical Gardens**

- Maintain collections of living plants.
- Support plant conservation, research and education.

**3. Seed Banks**

- Store seeds under controlled conditions.
- Preserve plant genetic material for future use.

**4. Gene Banks**

- Store genetic material such as seeds, tissues, sperm or embryos, depending on the species and technology used.

**5. Captive Breeding Programmes**

- Breed threatened animals under controlled conditions.
- May support reintroduction into suitable natural habitats.

**Advantages of Ex Situ Conservation**

1. Provides protection from some threats in natural habitats.
2. Allows controlled breeding.
3. Preserves genetic material.
4. Supports research and education.

**Limitations**

1. Can be expensive.
2. Limited space may restrict the number of organisms maintained.
3. Organisms may lose some natural behaviours.
4. Does not conserve the entire natural ecosystem.

### Comparison of In Situ and Ex Situ Conservation

| In situ | Ex situ |
|---|---|
| Conserves organisms in natural habitats | Conserves organisms outside natural habitats |
| Protects natural ecological interactions | Focuses on selected organisms or genetic material |
| Examples: national parks and marine protected areas | Examples: zoos and seed banks |
| May protect entire ecosystems | Usually protects selected species or samples |

## 8.2.6 Sustainable Development and Biodiversity

Sustainable development means meeting present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.

It involves balancing:

- Environmental protection.
- Economic development.
- Social well-being.

Examples of sustainable practices:

1. Replanting trees after logging.
2. Using resources at a rate that allows recovery.
3. Reducing waste and pollution.
4. Protecting natural habitats.
5. Practising sustainable fishing.
6. Using land responsibly.
7. Supporting responsible ecotourism.

**Example: Sustainable Forestry**

Trees can be harvested under a managed system that allows forests to regenerate. This reduces damage compared with uncontrolled logging.

## 8.2.7 Role of Individuals in Biodiversity Conservation

Individuals can help conserve biodiversity by:

- Reducing unnecessary consumption.
- Reusing and recycling materials.
- Avoiding products made from illegally traded wildlife.
- Supporting habitat restoration.
- Planting suitable native species.
- Preventing pollution.
- Respecting protected areas.
- Reporting suspected illegal wildlife activities to the relevant authorities.

**Exam tip:**

For questions asking how humans can conserve biodiversity, give specific actions and explain how each action reduces a threat.

# 8.3 MICROORGANISMS AND VIRUSES

## 8.3.1 Introduction to Microorganisms

Microorganisms are organisms that are generally too small to be seen clearly with the unaided eye.

They are commonly studied using microscopes.

Examples include:

- Bacteria.
- Archaea.
- Protozoa.
- Microscopic fungi.
- Microscopic algae.

Viruses are microscopic infectious agents, but they are generally not considered living cells because they lack cellular organisation and cannot reproduce independently.

Microorganisms are found in many environments:

- Soil.
- Water.
- Air.
- Food.
- The bodies of plants and animals.
- Extreme environments.

## 8.3.2 Main Groups of Microorganisms

### A. Bacteria

Bacteria are unicellular prokaryotic organisms.

Characteristics:

1. Usually unicellular.
2. No true nucleus.
3. DNA is located in the nucleoid region.
4. Usually contain a cell wall made of peptidoglycan.
5. Have ribosomes.
6. Reproduce mainly by binary fission.
7. Some possess flagella for movement.

### Structure of a Typical Bacterial Cell

**Capsule**

- Present in some bacteria.
- Provides additional protection.
- May help bacteria attach to surfaces.

**Cell Wall**

- Maintains cell shape.
- Provides support and protection.
- Most bacterial cell walls contain peptidoglycan.

**Plasma Membrane**

- Controls movement of substances into and out of the cell.
- Contains proteins involved in cellular processes.

**Cytoplasm**

- Contains enzymes and cellular components.
- Site of many metabolic reactions.

**Nucleoid**

- Region containing the main bacterial DNA.
- Not surrounded by a nuclear membrane.

**Plasmids**

- Small circular DNA molecules found in some bacteria.
- May carry genes that provide additional characteristics, such as antibiotic resistance.

**Ribosomes**

- Sites of protein synthesis.

**Flagellum**

- Present in some bacteria.
- Helps the bacterium move.

### Common Bacterial Shapes

1. Coccus: spherical.
2. Bacillus: rod-shaped.
3. Spirillum: spiral-shaped.
4. Vibrio: comma-shaped.

### Reproduction in Bacteria

Bacteria commonly reproduce through binary fission.

Basic sequence:

1. The bacterial DNA is replicated.
2. The cell grows.
3. The DNA copies move apart.
4. The plasma membrane and cell wall begin to divide.
5. The cell separates into two daughter cells.

Under suitable conditions, bacterial populations can increase rapidly.

**Important:** Binary fission is a form of asexual reproduction. It does not normally involve the fusion of gametes.

### Useful Roles of Bacteria

1. Decomposition of dead organic matter.
2. Nitrogen fixation by certain species.
3. Production of fermented foods.
4. Production of useful substances in biotechnology.
5. Normal microbial communities in the digestive systems of some animals.
6. Wastewater treatment.

**Examples:**

- *Lactobacillus* is used in the production of yoghurt.
- Certain bacteria help convert atmospheric nitrogen into compounds that plants can use.
- Decomposer bacteria return nutrients to ecosystems.

### Harmful Roles of Bacteria

Some bacteria cause diseases or food spoilage.

Examples:

- *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* causes tuberculosis.
- *Vibrio cholerae* causes cholera.
- Certain strains of *Salmonella* cause foodborne illness.

**Important:** Not all bacteria are harmful. Many are beneficial or harmless.

## 8.3.3 Archaea

Archaea are unicellular prokaryotic organisms that form a domain separate from Bacteria.

Characteristics:

- No true nucleus.
- No membrane-bound organelles.
- Cell walls do not contain bacterial peptidoglycan.
- Cell membrane composition is chemically distinct from bacteria.
- Some are adapted to extreme environments.

Examples:

**Methanogens**

- Produce methane as part of their metabolism.
- Found in environments such as wetlands and the digestive systems of some animals.

**Halophiles**

- Adapted to environments with high salt concentrations.

**Thermophiles**

- Adapted to high-temperature environments.

**Importance:**

- Contribute to nutrient cycles.
- Some produce methane.
- Help scientists understand the diversity of life and the limits of life in extreme environments.

## 8.3.4 Protozoa

Protozoa are mostly unicellular eukaryotic organisms.

Characteristics:

1. Have a true nucleus.
2. Possess membrane-bound organelles.
3. Mostly live in water or moist environments.
4. Some are free-living.
5. Some are parasites.
6. Some move using specialised structures.

### Types of Movement

**Amoeboid Movement**

- Uses temporary extensions of the cell called pseudopodia.
- Pseudopodia help the organism move and capture food.

Example: Amoeba.

**Cilia**

- Short hair-like structures.
- Beat in coordinated patterns to move the organism or move substances around it.

Example: Paramecium.

**Flagella**

- Longer whip-like structures.
- Help the organism move.

Example: Some protozoa, including *Giardia*.

### Nutrition in Protozoa

Some protozoa obtain food by engulfing particles.

For example, Amoeba uses pseudopodia to surround food particles. The food is enclosed in a food vacuole, where digestion occurs.

Other protozoa absorb nutrients from their surroundings or obtain nutrients from a host.

### Examples of Protozoa

**Amoeba**

- Free-living in many freshwater environments.
- Moves using pseudopodia.
- Engulfs food particles.

**Paramecium**

- Has cilia.
- Uses cilia for movement and feeding.
- Commonly found in freshwater environments.

**Plasmodium**

- A parasitic protozoan.
- Causes malaria.
- Transmitted to humans by infected female *Anopheles* mosquitoes.

**Giardia**

- A parasitic protozoan.
- Can cause intestinal illness.
- Can spread through contaminated water.

## 8.3.5 Microscopic Fungi

Fungi are eukaryotic organisms that obtain nutrients through absorption.

Some fungi are microscopic.

Examples include:

- Yeast.
- Moulds.

### Characteristics of Fungi

1. Eukaryotic cells.
2. Cell walls contain chitin.
3. Do not contain chlorophyll.
4. Obtain nutrients through absorption.
5. Many are decomposers.
6. Some are parasites or pathogens.

### Yeast

Yeast is a unicellular fungus.

Characteristics:

- Has a nucleus.
- Has a cell wall containing fungal cell-wall materials.
- Reproduces commonly by budding.
- Can carry out fermentation under suitable conditions.

**Budding in Yeast**

1. A small outgrowth forms on the parent cell.
2. The nucleus divides.
3. One nucleus moves into the developing bud.
4. The bud grows.
5. The bud may separate and become an independent cell.

**Importance of Yeast**

- Used in bread-making.
- Used in fermentation industries.
- Produces carbon dioxide during alcoholic fermentation.

### Mould

Moulds are multicellular fungi made up of thread-like structures called hyphae.

A mass of hyphae is called a mycelium.

Functions:

- Hyphae grow through the food source.
- They secrete digestive enzymes.
- Digested nutrients are absorbed.

Some moulds reproduce by producing spores.

**Importance of Fungi**

Beneficial roles:

- Decomposition.
- Food production.
- Production of some medicines.
- Industrial fermentation.

Harmful roles:

- Food spoilage.
- Plant diseases.
- Human fungal infections.
- Production of harmful toxins by certain species.

## 8.3.6 Microscopic Algae

Algae are photosynthetic organisms commonly found in aquatic or moist environments.

Some are unicellular, while others are multicellular.

Characteristics:

- Eukaryotic algae have a nucleus.
- Many contain chlorophyll.
- Carry out photosynthesis.
- Release oxygen during oxygenic photosynthesis.
- Form part of aquatic food webs.

Examples:

- Chlamydomonas.
- Chlorella.
- Diatoms.

### Importance of Algae

1. Produce organic food through photosynthesis.
2. Release oxygen.
3. Form the base of many aquatic food webs.
4. Support aquatic ecosystems.
5. Some are used in food and biotechnology.

### Harmful Algal Blooms

Under certain conditions, some algae or cyanobacteria may grow rapidly and form blooms.

Possible effects:

- Reduce water quality.
- Some species produce toxins.
- Dense blooms may block light from reaching submerged plants.
- Decomposition of dead bloom material can reduce dissolved oxygen.

**Important:** Cyanobacteria are bacteria, not eukaryotic algae, even though they are sometimes called blue-green algae.

## 8.3.7 Viruses

Viruses are microscopic infectious agents that can reproduce only inside suitable host cells.

They are not made of cells.

They are generally not classified as living organisms because they cannot carry out independent metabolism or reproduce without a host cell.

### Characteristics of Viruses

1. Acellular: they do not have cellular organisation.
2. Contain genetic material in the form of DNA or RNA.
3. Genetic material is enclosed in a protein coat called a capsid.
4. Some viruses have an outer lipid envelope.
5. Lack independent metabolic machinery.
6. Require host cells for replication.
7. Can infect animals, plants, fungi, bacteria and other organisms.

### Basic Structure of a Virus

**Genetic Material**

- May be DNA or RNA.
- Contains instructions needed to produce new virus particles.

**Capsid**

- Protein coat surrounding the genetic material.
- Protects the viral genome.
- Helps determine the virus's structure.

**Envelope**

- Present in some viruses.
- Outer lipid membrane derived from host-cell membranes.
- May contain viral proteins that help the virus attach to host cells.

**Surface Proteins**

- Help the virus recognise and attach to suitable host cells.

### Common Virus Shapes

- Helical.
- Icosahedral.
- Complex.
- Enveloped spherical or irregular forms.

### Examples of Viruses

- Influenza virus.
- Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV).
- Dengue virus.
- Coronavirus.
- Tobacco mosaic virus.
- Bacteriophages.

## 8.3.8 Viral Replication

Viruses cannot reproduce independently. They use the machinery of a host cell to make new virus particles.

A simplified viral replication sequence is:

**1. Attachment**

- The virus attaches to specific receptors on a suitable host cell.

**2. Entry**

- Viral genetic material or the virus particle enters the host cell, depending on the virus.

**3. Replication and Synthesis**

- The viral genome is copied.
- The host cell's machinery is used to produce viral components.

**4. Assembly**

- Newly produced viral genetic material and proteins are assembled into new virus particles.

**5. Release**

- New virus particles leave the host cell.
- Some viruses cause the host cell to rupture, while others leave through budding or other release processes.

**Important:** The exact replication process differs between viruses. Not every virus follows an identical sequence.

## 8.3.9 Lytic and Lysogenic Cycles of Bacteriophages

A bacteriophage is a virus that infects bacteria.

Some bacteriophages can follow lytic or lysogenic cycles.

### A. Lytic Cycle

In the lytic cycle:

1. The bacteriophage attaches to a bacterial cell.
2. Viral genetic material enters the bacterial cell.
3. The host cell produces viral components.
4. New bacteriophages are assembled.
5. The bacterial cell breaks open.
6. New bacteriophages are released.

The lytic cycle results in the destruction of the host bacterial cell.

### B. Lysogenic Cycle

In the lysogenic cycle:

1. The bacteriophage attaches to a bacterial cell.
2. Viral genetic material enters the cell.
3. Viral DNA becomes integrated into the bacterial chromosome.
4. The integrated viral DNA is called a prophage.
5. The bacterial cell reproduces and copies the prophage along with its own DNA.
6. Under certain conditions, the prophage may become active and enter the lytic cycle.

### Comparison of Lytic and Lysogenic Cycles

| Lytic cycle | Lysogenic cycle |
|---|---|
| Viral components are produced soon after infection | Viral DNA becomes integrated into host DNA |
| New viruses are assembled | Viral DNA is copied with host DNA |
| Host cell usually breaks open | Host cell is not immediately destroyed |
| Produces rapid release of new viruses | May remain inactive for a period |

## 8.3.10 Differences Between Bacteria and Viruses

| Feature | Bacteria | Viruses |
|---|---|---|
| Organisation | Cellular | Acellular |
| Cell type | Prokaryotic | No cell |
| Genetic material | DNA | DNA or RNA |
| Ribosomes | Present | Absent |
| Independent metabolism | Yes | No |
| Reproduction | Mainly binary fission | Replication inside host cells |
| Response to antibiotics | Some bacterial infections can be treated with suitable antibiotics | Antibiotics do not treat viral infections |

**Important exam point:**

Antibiotics act against bacteria by targeting bacterial structures or processes. They do not work against viruses because viruses lack those bacterial targets and reproduce using host-cell machinery.

## 8.3.11 Beneficial and Harmful Effects of Microorganisms

### Beneficial Effects

**1. Decomposition**

- Bacteria and fungi break down dead organisms.
- Nutrients are returned to the environment.
- Nutrient cycling is maintained.

**2. Food Production**

- Yeast is used in bread-making.
- Bacteria are used in yoghurt production.
- Microorganisms are used in other fermentation processes.

**3. Medicine and Biotechnology**

- Some microorganisms produce useful antibiotics or other compounds.
- Microorganisms can be used to manufacture useful biological products.
- Genetically modified microorganisms can produce certain proteins.

**4. Agriculture**

- Nitrogen-fixing bacteria help improve nitrogen availability.
- Some microorganisms act as biological control agents.
- Microorganisms contribute to soil nutrient cycling.

**5. Environmental Management**

- Microorganisms are used in wastewater treatment.
- Some can break down pollutants through bioremediation.

### Harmful Effects

**1. Human Diseases**

Examples include:

- Tuberculosis — caused by *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*.
- Cholera — caused by *Vibrio cholerae*.
- Malaria — caused by *Plasmodium*.
- Influenza — caused by influenza viruses.
- Dengue — caused by dengue viruses.

**2. Plant Diseases**

Some bacteria, fungi and viruses infect crops.

Effects may include:

- Reduced photosynthesis.
- Damaged plant tissues.
- Poor growth.
- Reduced crop yield.

**3. Food Spoilage**

Microorganisms can grow on food and cause it to spoil.

**4. Toxin Production**

Certain microorganisms produce toxins that can harm humans and animals.

**5. Ecosystem Disruption**

Some introduced microorganisms can spread diseases or alter ecological relationships.

## 8.3.12 Prevention and Control of Microbial Diseases

Methods include:

1. Maintaining personal hygiene.
2. Washing hands properly.
3. Drinking safe water.
4. Preparing and storing food safely.
5. Vaccination where available.
6. Proper sanitation.
7. Controlling disease vectors.
8. Using antibiotics only when prescribed for suitable bacterial infections.
9. Following infection-control advice from healthcare professionals.

### Vaccination

Vaccination exposes the immune system to an antigen or instructions for producing an antigen in a controlled way.

This helps the immune system develop an immune response and memory.

If the vaccinated person later encounters the relevant pathogen, the immune system may respond more rapidly.

**Important:**

- Vaccines can prevent or reduce the severity of certain infectious diseases.
- Antibiotics treat certain bacterial infections.
- Antibiotics do not treat viral infections.
- Vaccines and antibiotics have different functions.

# COMMON EXAM MISTAKES

1. Classification and identification are not the same. Classification groups organisms, while identification determines which organism is being examined.

2. A species is the most specific basic taxonomic level.

3. Genus begins with a capital letter, while the specific epithet begins with a lowercase letter.

4. Scientific names are written in italics when typed.

5. Biodiversity is not only the number of species. It includes genetic, species and ecosystem diversity.

6. Species richness and species evenness are different. Richness is the number of species; evenness describes how individuals are distributed among them.

7. Habitat destruction and habitat fragmentation are not the same.

8. In situ conservation takes place in natural habitats, while ex situ conservation takes place outside natural habitats.

9. Not all bacteria are harmful. Many are useful in decomposition, food production and nutrient cycling.

10. Archaea are different from bacteria, even though both are prokaryotic.

11. Fungi do not carry out photosynthesis because they do not contain chlorophyll.

12. Cyanobacteria are bacteria, not eukaryotic algae.

13. Viruses are acellular and cannot reproduce independently.

14. Antibiotics do not treat viral infections.

15. Vaccines help prepare the immune system; they are not the same as antibiotics.

16. A virus may contain either DNA or RNA as its genome.

17. Not all viruses have an envelope.

18. Not every non-native species becomes invasive.

# FINAL REVISION SUMMARY

## 8.1 CLASSIFICATION SYSTEM AND NAMING OF ORGANISMS

- Classification organises organisms based on similarities and differences.
- Taxonomy involves identification, nomenclature and classification.
- Taxonomic hierarchy: Domain → Kingdom → Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species.
- Three domains: Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya.
- Binomial nomenclature uses two words: genus and specific epithet.
- A dichotomous key identifies organisms through paired contrasting statements.

## 8.2 BIODIVERSITY

- Biodiversity is the variety of living organisms.
- Three levels: genetic, species and ecosystem diversity.
- Species diversity includes richness and evenness.
- Biodiversity supports ecological stability, food production, medicine, research and recreation.
- Threats include habitat destruction, fragmentation, pollution, climate change, overexploitation and invasive alien species.
- In situ conservation protects organisms in natural habitats.
- Ex situ conservation protects organisms or genetic material outside natural habitats.
- Sustainable development balances environmental, economic and social needs.

## 8.3 MICROORGANISMS AND VIRUSES

- Microorganisms are generally too small to be seen clearly without a microscope.
- Bacteria and archaea are prokaryotic.
- Protozoa, fungi and eukaryotic algae have eukaryotic cells.
- Bacteria reproduce mainly through binary fission.
- Yeast commonly reproduces through budding.
- Fungi act as decomposers and are also used in food production.
- Algae carry out photosynthesis and support aquatic food webs.
- Viruses are acellular infectious agents.
- Viruses contain DNA or RNA surrounded by a protein capsid.
- Viruses require host cells for replication.
- Bacteriophages may follow lytic or lysogenic cycles.
- Antibiotics do not treat viral infections.

# KEY BIOLOGY TERMS

**Classification:** Organising organisms into groups based on shared characteristics.

**Taxonomy:** The study of identifying, naming and classifying organisms.

**Taxon:** A group of organisms at a particular classification level.

**Species:** The most specific basic taxonomic unit.

**Binomial nomenclature:** A scientific naming system using two words.

**Genus:** A taxonomic group containing closely related species.

**Dichotomous key:** An identification tool using paired contrasting statements.

**Biodiversity:** The variety of living organisms.

**Genetic diversity:** Variation in genetic information within a species.

**Species richness:** The number of different species in an area.

**Species evenness:** How evenly individuals are distributed among species.

**Ecosystem diversity:** The variety of ecosystems in a region.

**Habitat fragmentation:** Division of a continuous habitat into smaller isolated patches.

**Conservation:** Protection and management of biodiversity.

**In situ conservation:** Conservation in natural habitats.

**Ex situ conservation:** Conservation outside natural habitats.

**Microorganism:** An organism generally too small to be seen clearly with the unaided eye.

**Prokaryote:** An organism whose cells lack a membrane-bound nucleus.

**Eukaryote:** An organism whose cells contain a membrane-bound nucleus.

**Binary fission:** A form of asexual reproduction in which one cell divides into two cells.

**Hypha:** A thread-like structure forming part of a fungus.

**Mycelium:** A mass or network of fungal hyphae.

**Capsid:** The protein coat surrounding a virus's genetic material.

**Bacteriophage:** A virus that infects bacteria.

**Prophage:** Bacteriophage DNA integrated into a bacterial chromosome.

**Lytic cycle:** A viral replication cycle that produces new viruses and commonly causes host-cell lysis.

**Lysogenic cycle:** A cycle in which viral DNA is maintained within the host genome and copied as the host reproduces.

**Bioremediation:** Use of organisms or biological processes to reduce or remove environmental pollutants.

**Invasive alien species:** A non-native species that spreads and causes or may cause harm to the environment, economy or human health.
