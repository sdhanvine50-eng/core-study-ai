# 13.1 GENETIC ENGINEERING

## 13.1.1 INTRODUCTION TO GENETIC ENGINEERING

Genetic engineering is the modification of an organism's genetic material using biotechnology.

It involves changing DNA to introduce, remove or modify particular genetic characteristics.

Genetic engineering allows scientists to introduce a useful gene into an organism or modify the activity of an existing gene.

An organism whose genetic material has been modified using genetic engineering is called a genetically modified organism (GMO).

A GMO may contain genetic material that has been modified using techniques such as recombinant DNA technology.

**Examples of genetic engineering applications:**

1. Production of human insulin using genetically modified microorganisms.
2. Development of crops with resistance to certain insect pests.
3. Development of crops with improved nutritional characteristics.
4. Production of certain medicines and vaccines.
5. Research into inherited diseases and gene function.

IMPORTANT:

Genetic engineering is a specific method of modifying genetic material. It is not the same as traditional selective breeding, which involves choosing organisms with desirable inherited characteristics to reproduce.

## 13.1.2 RECOMBINANT DNA TECHNOLOGY

Recombinant DNA technology involves combining DNA from different sources to produce a DNA molecule containing a new combination of genetic material.

A DNA molecule formed by joining DNA from different sources is called recombinant DNA.

For example, a human gene that codes for insulin can be combined with a suitable DNA vector and introduced into a microorganism.

The genetically modified microorganism can then produce human insulin.

### IMPORTANT COMPONENTS

**1. Gene of interest**

A gene of interest is the particular gene selected because it contains information for a desired characteristic or product.

Example:

The human insulin gene contains information used to produce insulin.

**2. Restriction enzymes**

Restriction enzymes are enzymes that recognise particular DNA sequences and cut DNA at or near those sequences.

They are sometimes described as molecular scissors.

Different restriction enzymes recognise different DNA sequences.

**3. DNA ligase**

DNA ligase is an enzyme that joins DNA fragments together by forming bonds in the sugar-phosphate backbone.

It is sometimes described as molecular glue.

**4. Vector**

A vector is a DNA molecule used to carry genetic material into a recipient cell.

Examples include:
- Plasmids.
- Modified viruses used in certain applications.

**5. Host organism**

A host organism is the organism or cell that receives the introduced genetic material.

Examples include:
- Bacteria.
- Yeast cells.
- Plant cells.
- Animal cells.

## 13.1.3 PLASMIDS AS VECTORS

A plasmid is a small, usually circular DNA molecule found in many bacteria.

Plasmids are separate from the main bacterial chromosome and can replicate independently.

They are useful in genetic engineering because they can act as vectors.

**Important characteristics:**

1. Usually circular and double-stranded DNA.
2. Can replicate independently in suitable bacterial cells.
3. May contain genes that provide useful selectable characteristics.
4. Can carry introduced genetic material.

A plasmid used as a vector may contain:
- An origin of replication.
- A suitable region for insertion of genetic material.
- Selectable marker genes.

A selectable marker helps researchers identify cells that have received a particular vector.

IMPORTANT:

Not every plasmid is suitable for every genetic engineering application. A vector must be compatible with the host cell and the intended purpose.

## 13.1.4 GENERAL PRINCIPLE OF RECOMBINANT DNA TECHNOLOGY

The general process can be understood through the following stages.

### STAGE 1: IDENTIFICATION OF THE GENE OF INTEREST

Scientists identify a gene associated with the desired characteristic or product.

Example:

The human insulin gene is selected when the aim is to produce insulin.

### STAGE 2: PREPARATION OF A SUITABLE VECTOR

A suitable vector, such as a plasmid, is selected.

The vector is designed to carry the gene of interest into a host cell.

### STAGE 3: FORMATION OF RECOMBINANT DNA

The selected gene is combined with vector DNA.

Restriction enzymes and DNA ligase may be used in recombinant DNA methods.

The resulting DNA molecule contains genetic material from different sources.

### STAGE 4: INTRODUCTION INTO A HOST CELL

The recombinant DNA is introduced into a suitable host cell.

The host cell may then contain the introduced genetic information.

### STAGE 5: IDENTIFICATION OF SUITABLE CELLS

Researchers identify cells that have successfully received the required genetic material.

This may involve selectable markers and other screening methods.

### STAGE 6: GENE EXPRESSION

If the introduced gene is expressed correctly, the host cell may produce the desired protein or show the intended characteristic.

### STAGE 7: COLLECTION OF THE PRODUCT

Where the aim is to produce a useful substance, the product may be collected and purified.

For example, insulin produced by genetically modified microorganisms is purified for medical use.

IMPORTANT:

The stages above describe the general scientific principle. Actual genetic engineering methods differ depending on the organism, vector, gene and intended application.

## 13.1.5 PRODUCTION OF HUMAN INSULIN USING GENETIC ENGINEERING

Insulin is a peptide hormone produced by beta cells in the pancreas.

It helps regulate blood glucose concentration.

People with diabetes may require insulin treatment when their bodies cannot produce enough insulin or cannot use insulin effectively.

Genetic engineering allows microorganisms to produce human insulin for medical use.

**General explanation:**

1. The human insulin gene is identified.
2. A suitable vector is used to carry the genetic information.
3. Recombinant DNA is introduced into a suitable microorganism.
4. The microorganism expresses the introduced genetic information.
5. Insulin or an insulin precursor is produced.
6. The product is processed and purified.
7. The purified insulin is prepared for medical use under controlled quality and safety standards.

**Advantages:**

1. Allows large-scale production of insulin.
2. Provides a reliable source of insulin.
3. Produces human insulin with the required biological activity when correctly manufactured.
4. Reduces dependence on insulin extracted from animal sources.
5. Supports the treatment of diabetes.

IMPORTANT:

Modern recombinant insulin production involves carefully controlled manufacturing and purification. The final medicine must meet strict quality and safety requirements.

## 13.1.6 GENETICALLY MODIFIED ORGANISMS (GMOs)

A genetically modified organism is an organism whose genetic material has been deliberately altered using genetic engineering.

Examples include genetically modified:
- Bacteria.
- Yeast.
- Crops.
- Research animals.

### A. GENETICALLY MODIFIED CROPS

Some crops are genetically modified to provide useful characteristics.

Examples:

**1. Insect-resistant crops**

Certain crops are modified to produce proteins that protect them against particular insect pests.

Example:

Some varieties of Bt maize contain a gene from the bacterium *Bacillus thuringiensis*.

The introduced gene allows the plant to produce a protein that affects certain insect pests.

Potential advantages:
- Reduced damage caused by target insect pests.
- Reduced need for some insecticide applications.
- Improved crop protection.

IMPORTANT:

The effects depend on the crop, pest species, farming practices and environmental conditions.

**2. Herbicide-tolerant crops**

Some crops are genetically modified to tolerate particular herbicides.

This allows farmers to control certain weeds without damaging the crop when the correct herbicide and application conditions are used.

Potential advantages:
- Easier weed management.
- Reduced competition between weeds and crops.
- More efficient crop management in suitable farming systems.

**3. Nutritionally enhanced crops**

Some crops are modified to improve their nutritional composition.

Example:

Golden Rice has been developed to produce beta-carotene in the rice grain.

Beta-carotene can be converted into vitamin A by the human body.

Potential importance:
- May help improve vitamin A intake in populations where vitamin A deficiency is a concern.
- Demonstrates how genetic engineering can be used to improve nutritional characteristics.

IMPORTANT:

The nutritional benefits depend on consumption, availability, dietary patterns and other public health factors.

## 13.1.7 APPLICATIONS OF GENETIC ENGINEERING

Genetic engineering has applications in several fields.

### A. MEDICINE

Applications include:
- Production of human insulin.
- Production of certain therapeutic proteins.
- Development of some vaccines.
- Research into genetic diseases.
- Development of certain gene-based treatments.

### B. AGRICULTURE

Applications include:
- Insect-resistant crops.
- Herbicide-tolerant crops.
- Crops with improved nutritional characteristics.
- Crops with improved resistance to certain diseases.

### C. INDUSTRY

Genetically modified microorganisms can be used to produce:
- Enzymes.
- Industrial proteins.
- Certain chemicals.
- Useful biological products.

### D. SCIENTIFIC RESEARCH

Genetic engineering allows researchers to:
- Study gene functions.
- Investigate the causes of inherited conditions.
- Understand how proteins work.
- Study the development of organisms.
- Investigate possible medical treatments.

## 13.1.8 ADVANTAGES OF GENETIC ENGINEERING

**1. Medical benefits**

Genetic engineering allows the production of important medicines such as human insulin.

**2. Improved agricultural productivity**

Certain genetically modified crops may experience less damage from specific pests.

**3. Improved nutritional characteristics**

Some crops are developed to contain higher amounts of particular nutrients.

**4. More efficient production**

Microorganisms can be used to manufacture useful biological products on a large scale.

**5. Scientific advancement**

Genetic engineering improves understanding of genes, inherited conditions and biological processes.

## 13.1.9 RISKS AND LIMITATIONS OF GENETIC ENGINEERING

Genetic engineering must be evaluated carefully.

### A. ENVIRONMENTAL CONCERNS

Potential concerns include:

1. Introduced genes may spread to other populations through gene flow.
2. Some target pests may develop resistance over time.
3. Non-target organisms may be affected, depending on the crop and introduced characteristic.
4. Changes in farming practices may affect biodiversity.

### B. HEALTH AND FOOD SAFETY CONCERNS

Genetically modified foods must be assessed for safety.

Relevant considerations include:
- Potential allergenicity.
- Nutritional composition.
- Toxicity.
- Stability of the introduced characteristic.
- Food quality.

A genetically modified food is not automatically harmful or automatically safe simply because it is genetically modified. Safety assessments must consider the specific product.

### C. ECONOMIC CONCERNS

Potential issues include:
- Cost of research and development.
- Patents and ownership of technologies.
- Dependence on particular seed suppliers.
- Unequal access to technology.

### D. ETHICAL CONCERNS

Ethical discussions may involve:
- Modification of living organisms.
- Animal welfare.
- Human genetic modification.
- Access to genetic technologies.
- Privacy of genetic information.

## 13.1.10 BIOSAFETY AND REGULATION

Biosafety refers to measures used to assess and manage potential risks associated with biological research and biotechnology.

Genetically modified organisms may require assessment before release or use.

Risk assessment may consider:
1. The organism being modified.
2. The introduced genetic material.
3. The intended use.
4. Possible effects on human health.
5. Possible effects on the environment.
6. Methods of containment and monitoring.

Regulation helps ensure that biotechnology is developed and used responsibly.

## 13.1.11 GENE THERAPY

Gene therapy is a medical approach that aims to treat or prevent disease by modifying genetic material in a patient's cells.

Depending on the treatment, it may involve:
- Introducing a functional copy of a gene.
- Modifying an existing gene.
- Changing how a gene is expressed.

Gene therapy may be used to address certain inherited conditions and other diseases.

**General categories:**

**1. Somatic gene therapy**

Genetic changes are made in body cells.

These changes are intended to affect the treated individual and are generally not passed to offspring.

**2. Germline genetic modification**

Changes are made in reproductive cells or early embryos in ways that may be inherited by future generations.

This raises major ethical and safety concerns and is subject to strict restrictions in many countries.

IMPORTANT:

Gene therapy is not a universal cure for genetic diseases. Its suitability depends on the condition, treatment method, safety and effectiveness.

# 13.2 BIOTECHNOLOGY

## 13.2.1 INTRODUCTION TO BIOTECHNOLOGY

Biotechnology is the application of living organisms, cells, biological systems or biological processes to develop useful products and services.

Biotechnology has been used for thousands of years.

Traditional biotechnology includes:
- Bread making.
- Yoghurt production.
- Cheese production.
- Fermentation of food.

Modern biotechnology includes:
- Genetic engineering.
- Recombinant DNA technology.
- Tissue culture.
- Production of therapeutic proteins.
- Molecular diagnosis.

Biotechnology is important in:
1. Agriculture.
2. Medicine.
3. Food production.
4. Industry.
5. Environmental management.

## 13.2.2 TRADITIONAL BIOTECHNOLOGY

Traditional biotechnology uses organisms or their biological processes without necessarily modifying their DNA through genetic engineering.

A major example is fermentation.

### A. FERMENTATION

Fermentation is a metabolic process in which microorganisms break down organic substances and produce useful products.

Different microorganisms produce different fermentation products.

### B. YEAST IN BREAD MAKING

Yeast is a microorganism commonly used in bread making.

During fermentation, yeast breaks down glucose and produces:
- Ethanol.
- Carbon dioxide.

The carbon dioxide forms bubbles in the dough.

These bubbles cause the dough to expand and rise.

During baking:
- The gas expands as the temperature increases.
- The yeast is inactivated by heat.
- Ethanol evaporates or is removed during baking.

**Biological importance:**

Carbon dioxide produced by yeast helps give bread its soft, porous texture.

### C. LACTIC ACID FERMENTATION

Certain bacteria convert sugars into lactic acid.

Examples include bacteria used in yoghurt production.

During yoghurt production:
1. Bacteria use lactose in milk.
2. Lactic acid is produced.
3. The pH of the milk decreases.
4. Milk proteins, especially casein, coagulate.
5. The milk becomes thicker and develops a sour taste.

**Applications:**
- Yoghurt production.
- Certain cheese production processes.
- Preservation and development of food flavour.

IMPORTANT:

Different yoghurt products use different bacterial cultures and manufacturing conditions.

## 13.2.3 MODERN BIOTECHNOLOGY

Modern biotechnology uses advanced biological techniques to develop products and solve problems.

Examples include:

1. Genetic engineering.
2. Recombinant DNA technology.
3. Tissue culture.
4. DNA profiling.
5. Molecular diagnosis.
6. Production of biological medicines.

## 13.2.4 TISSUE CULTURE

Tissue culture is a technique used to grow cells, tissues or organs under controlled conditions.

In plant biotechnology, plant tissue culture can be used to produce new plants from small pieces of plant tissue.

A small piece of plant tissue used to start a culture is called an explant.

Plant tissue culture relies on the ability of many plant cells to develop into complete plants under suitable conditions.

This ability is associated with cellular totipotency.

**Totipotency** is the ability of a cell to develop into a complete organism under suitable conditions.

### GENERAL PRINCIPLE OF PLANT TISSUE CULTURE

The process can be understood through the following stages:

**1. Selection of plant material**

A suitable piece of plant tissue is selected.

**2. Preparation under sterile conditions**

The plant material and equipment are prepared to reduce contamination by microorganisms.

**3. Placement on a nutrient medium**

The explant is placed on a suitable nutrient medium containing substances required for growth.

**4. Cell division and development**

Cells divide and may form a mass of undifferentiated cells called a callus.

**5. Development of shoots and roots**

Suitable growth conditions and plant hormones encourage the development of shoots and roots.

**6. Formation of plantlets**

Small plantlets develop.

**7. Acclimatisation**

Plantlets are gradually adapted to conditions outside the controlled culture environment.

**8. Transfer to growing conditions**

The plantlets may be transferred to soil or another suitable growing medium.

### ADVANTAGES OF PLANT TISSUE CULTURE

1. Allows rapid production of many plants.
2. Produces genetically similar plants when produced through ordinary clonal propagation.
3. Can be used to propagate plants that are difficult to grow from seeds.
4. Requires relatively little starting plant material.
5. Can support conservation of rare plant varieties.
6. Can produce disease-free planting material when suitable healthy material and procedures are used.

### LIMITATIONS OF PLANT TISSUE CULTURE

1. Requires sterile conditions.
2. Contamination can destroy cultures.
3. Requires specialised equipment and trained workers.
4. Can be expensive.
5. Some plants are difficult to culture.
6. Somaclonal variation may occur, producing differences among regenerated plants.

IMPORTANT:

Plant tissue culture does not automatically produce genetically identical plants in every case. Genetic or epigenetic changes may occur during culture.

## 13.2.5 DNA PROFILING

DNA profiling is a technique used to analyse selected regions of DNA to produce a pattern that can help distinguish individuals.

DNA profiles are used in:
- Forensic investigations.
- Paternity testing.
- Identification of human remains.
- Population genetics research.

Except for identical twins, individuals generally have different DNA profiles when suitable variable regions are examined.

IMPORTANT:

DNA profiling does not normally examine every part of a person's DNA. It analyses selected genetic regions.

### APPLICATIONS OF DNA PROFILING

**1. Forensic investigation**

DNA from biological evidence may be compared with DNA profiles from individuals.

A close match may support an investigation, but interpretation must consider the quality of the sample, statistical evidence and possible contamination.

**2. Paternity testing**

DNA profiles from a child and potential biological parents can be compared to assess biological relationships.

**3. Identification of human remains**

DNA profiling can help identify individuals when other identification methods are unavailable.

## 13.2.6 BIOTECHNOLOGY IN MEDICINE

Biotechnology has improved the production and development of medical products.

Examples include:

### A. RECOMBINANT INSULIN

Genetically modified microorganisms can produce human insulin.

The insulin is purified and prepared for medical use.

### B. VACCINE DEVELOPMENT

Biotechnology is used in the development and manufacture of some vaccines.

Different vaccine technologies may use:
- Recombinant proteins.
- Modified viruses.
- Genetic instructions for producing a target antigen.

Vaccines stimulate the immune system to develop protection against particular infectious diseases.

### C. DIAGNOSTIC TESTING

Molecular biotechnology allows scientists to detect genetic material associated with particular organisms or inherited conditions.

Examples include:
- PCR-based testing.
- Genetic testing.
- Molecular detection of pathogens.

### D. THERAPEUTIC PROTEINS

Biotechnology allows the production of useful proteins for medical treatment.

Examples include:
- Certain hormones.
- Some blood-clotting factors.
- Certain antibodies.

## 13.2.7 BIOTECHNOLOGY IN AGRICULTURE

Biotechnology supports agricultural production through several methods.

### A. PLANT TISSUE CULTURE

Used to produce large numbers of selected plants.

Examples:
- Banana.
- Orchid.
- Pineapple.
- Certain ornamental plants.

### B. GENETICALLY MODIFIED CROPS

Used to introduce desired characteristics such as:
- Resistance to certain insect pests.
- Tolerance to particular herbicides.
- Improved nutritional composition.

### C. BIOFERTILISERS

Biofertilisers contain beneficial microorganisms that improve nutrient availability or support plant growth.

Examples include:
- Rhizobium bacteria that form nitrogen-fixing associations with leguminous plants.
- Certain free-living nitrogen-fixing bacteria.
- Mycorrhizal fungi that help plants absorb mineral nutrients, especially phosphorus.

### D. BIOLOGICAL PEST CONTROL

Biological control uses living organisms or their products to manage pest populations.

Examples:
- Predators.
- Parasitoids.
- Pathogens that infect specific pests.

Advantages:
- May reduce dependence on chemical pesticides.
- Can provide targeted pest management.
- May support more sustainable agriculture when properly managed.

Limitations:
- Effectiveness may depend on environmental conditions.
- Control may take longer than chemical methods.
- Introduced biological control organisms require careful assessment.

## 13.2.8 BIOTECHNOLOGY IN ENVIRONMENTAL MANAGEMENT

Biotechnology can help reduce pollution and manage waste.

### A. BIOREMEDIATION

Bioremediation is the use of living organisms, especially microorganisms, to remove, break down or reduce environmental pollutants.

Microorganisms may break down certain organic pollutants into simpler substances.

Applications include:
- Treatment of certain industrial wastes.
- Treatment of sewage.
- Cleaning up some oil-contaminated environments.
- Decomposition of organic waste.

IMPORTANT:

Not all pollutants can be broken down by microorganisms. Some substances require other treatment methods.

### B. WASTEWATER TREATMENT

Microorganisms are used in wastewater treatment to break down organic waste.

This helps reduce the amount of biodegradable organic matter in wastewater before it is released or reused.

### C. BIOGAS PRODUCTION

Biogas can be produced when microorganisms break down organic matter under anaerobic conditions.

Anaerobic means occurring without oxygen.

Biogas commonly contains:
- Methane.
- Carbon dioxide.
- Small quantities of other gases.

Methane can be used as a fuel.

Examples of materials used in biogas production include:
- Animal manure.
- Food waste.
- Agricultural waste.
- Sewage sludge.

## 13.2.9 COMPARISON BETWEEN TRADITIONAL AND MODERN BIOTECHNOLOGY

| Traditional biotechnology | Modern biotechnology |
|---|---|
| Uses established biological processes | Uses advanced biological techniques |
| Often relies on natural fermentation | May involve molecular and genetic techniques |
| Does not necessarily modify DNA directly | May directly modify or analyse genetic material |
| Examples: bread and yoghurt production | Examples: genetic engineering and DNA profiling |

IMPORTANT:

Traditional and modern biotechnology are not completely separate categories. Modern industrial biotechnology may also use fermentation.

## 13.2.10 ADVANTAGES OF BIOTECHNOLOGY

**1. Medical applications**

Allows production of medicines, vaccines and diagnostic tools.

**2. Agricultural improvement**

Supports crop propagation, crop protection and improved agricultural productivity.

**3. Food production**

Microorganisms are used to produce bread, yoghurt, cheese and other fermented foods.

**4. Environmental protection**

Bioremediation and wastewater treatment can help reduce pollution.

**5. Industrial production**

Microorganisms can produce enzymes and other useful substances.

**6. Conservation**

Tissue culture and other biotechnological methods may help conserve valuable plant varieties.

## 13.2.11 LIMITATIONS AND CHALLENGES OF BIOTECHNOLOGY

1. Some technologies require expensive equipment.
2. Skilled workers and specialised facilities may be needed.
3. Biological contamination may affect production.
4. Some processes require strict environmental control.
5. Genetic technologies may raise ethical concerns.
6. Some applications may have environmental risks.
7. Access to biotechnology may differ between communities and countries.
8. Regulations and safety assessments are required for certain applications.

## 13.2.12 ETHICAL AND SOCIAL ISSUES

Biotechnology raises important ethical and social questions.

Examples include:

**1. Genetic privacy**

Genetic information can reveal personal biological characteristics. It should be handled responsibly.

**2. Human genetic modification**

Changing human genetic material may raise questions about safety, consent, fairness and possible effects on future generations.

**3. Animal welfare**

The use of animals in biotechnology research must consider animal welfare and ethical standards.

**4. Environmental effects**

The release of modified organisms requires appropriate risk assessment.

**5. Access and affordability**

Some biotechnology products may be expensive or unavailable to certain communities.

**6. Ownership and patents**

Patents and ownership of biological technologies may affect access to seeds, medicines and research tools.

# 13.3 COMPARISON AND EXAM REVISION

## 13.3.1 GENETIC ENGINEERING VS BIOTECHNOLOGY

| Genetic engineering | Biotechnology |
|---|---|
| Involves modifying genetic material | Uses organisms, cells or biological processes for useful purposes |
| A specific area of biotechnology | A broader field |
| May involve recombinant DNA technology | Includes fermentation, tissue culture, genetic engineering and more |
| Example: production of recombinant insulin | Example: yoghurt production |

## 13.3.2 GENETIC ENGINEERING VS SELECTIVE BREEDING

| Genetic engineering | Selective breeding |
|---|---|
| Directly modifies genetic material | Selects organisms with desired characteristics to reproduce |
| Can introduce specific genetic changes | Combines existing inherited characteristics through reproduction |
| Uses molecular and genetic techniques | Uses controlled reproduction over generations |
| May produce GMOs | Does not necessarily produce GMOs |

## 13.3.3 PLANT TISSUE CULTURE VS SELECTIVE BREEDING

| Plant tissue culture | Selective breeding |
|---|---|
| Produces plants from cultured cells or tissues | Produces offspring through selected parent organisms |
| Can rapidly produce many plants | Usually requires multiple generations |
| Often produces genetically similar clones | Produces genetically varied offspring |
| Uses controlled culture conditions | Uses controlled reproduction |

## 13.3.4 IMPORTANT EXAM MISCONCEPTIONS

1. Genetic engineering is part of biotechnology, not the whole field.
2. Recombinant DNA contains genetic material combined from different sources.
3. Restriction enzymes cut DNA, while DNA ligase joins DNA fragments.
4. A plasmid can act as a vector.
5. Genetically modified organisms are not automatically harmful.
6. Genetically modified foods must be assessed for safety.
7. Fermentation is a form of biotechnology.
8. Yeast produces carbon dioxide during bread making.
9. Lactic acid bacteria produce lactic acid during yoghurt production.
10. Plant tissue culture requires suitable nutrient media and controlled conditions.
11. DNA profiling analyses selected regions of DNA.
12. Bioremediation uses living organisms to reduce or remove certain pollutants.
13. Biogas is produced under anaerobic conditions.
14. Gene therapy is not a guaranteed cure for every genetic condition.

# 13.5 KEY TERMS

**Biotechnology**
- Application of organisms, cells or biological processes to develop useful products and services.

**Genetic engineering**
- Modification of genetic material using biotechnology.

**Genetically modified organism (GMO)**
- An organism whose genetic material has been modified using genetic engineering.

**Recombinant DNA**
- DNA formed by combining genetic material from different sources.

**Gene of interest**
- A selected gene associated with a desired characteristic or product.

**Restriction enzyme**
- An enzyme that cuts DNA at or near particular recognition sequences.

**DNA ligase**
- An enzyme that joins DNA fragments.

**Plasmid**
- A small DNA molecule, commonly circular, found in many bacteria.

**Vector**
- A DNA molecule used to carry genetic material into a host cell.

**Host organism**
- An organism or cell that receives introduced genetic material.

**Fermentation**
- A metabolic process in which microorganisms break down organic substances and produce useful products.

**Tissue culture**
- The growth of cells, tissues or organs under controlled conditions.

**Explant**
- A piece of plant tissue used to start a tissue culture.

**Totipotency**
- The ability of a cell to develop into a complete organism under suitable conditions.

**DNA profiling**
- Analysis of selected DNA regions to produce a profile that can help distinguish individuals.

**Bioremediation**
- Use of living organisms to remove, break down or reduce environmental pollutants.

**Biofertiliser**
- A product containing beneficial microorganisms that improve nutrient availability or support plant growth.

**Biogas**
- A gas mixture produced by microorganisms during anaerobic breakdown of organic matter.

**Gene therapy**
- A medical approach that modifies genetic material in cells to treat or prevent disease.

**Biosafety**
- Measures used to assess and manage potential risks associated with biological research and applications.
