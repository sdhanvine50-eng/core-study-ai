# 11.1 MONOHYBRID INHERITANCE

## 11.1.1 Introduction to Inheritance

Inheritance is the transmission of genetic characteristics from parents to offspring through genes.

Characteristics inherited from parents are called inherited characteristics or hereditary traits.

Examples include:

- Blood group.
- Natural hair texture.
- Certain eye characteristics.
- Biological sex.
- Some inherited genetic conditions.

Inheritance explains why offspring resemble their parents but are not necessarily identical to them.

Genetic information is carried by DNA, which is organised into chromosomes.

Genes are sections of DNA that influence inherited characteristics.

## 11.1.2 Important Genetic Terms

**Character**

A characteristic that can be inherited.

Example: flower colour.

**Trait**

A particular form of a character.

Example: purple flowers or white flowers.

**Gene**

A section of DNA that contains information influencing a particular characteristic or functional product.

**Allele**

An alternative form of a gene.

Example: an allele associated with purple flowers and an allele associated with white flowers.

**Dominant allele**

An allele that is expressed in the phenotype of a heterozygote in a simple complete-dominance pattern.

It is represented by a capital letter.

Example: T.

**Recessive allele**

An allele whose effect is masked in a heterozygote under complete dominance.

It is represented by a lowercase letter.

Example: t.

**Genotype**

The genetic constitution or allele combination of an organism for a particular gene.

Examples:

- TT
- Tt
- tt

**Phenotype**

The observable characteristic of an organism resulting from its genotype and environmental influences.

Examples:

- Tall plant.
- Short plant.
- Purple flower.
- White flower.

**Homozygous**

An organism has two identical alleles for a particular gene.

Examples:

- TT: homozygous dominant.
- tt: homozygous recessive.

**Heterozygous**

An organism has two different alleles for a particular gene.

Example:

- Tt.

**Gamete**

A reproductive cell containing one allele for each gene.

Examples:

- Sperm cell.
- Ovum.

**Fertilisation**

The fusion of male and female gametes to form a diploid zygote.

## 11.1.3 Mendel's Experiments

Gregor Mendel is known as the father of genetics because his experiments on pea plants helped establish fundamental principles of inheritance.

He studied characteristics such as:

- Plant height.
- Seed shape.
- Seed colour.
- Flower colour.
- Pod shape.
- Pod colour.
- Flower position.

Mendel selected pea plants because:

1. They have clear contrasting characteristics.
2. They can reproduce through self-pollination.
3. Cross-pollination can be controlled.
4. They produce many offspring.
5. They have a relatively short generation time.

### Example: Plant Height

Consider two contrasting traits:

- Tall plants.
- Short plants.

For this example:

- T = allele for tallness.
- t = allele for shortness.

Assume complete dominance, where T is dominant over t.

### Parental Generation (P)

A homozygous tall plant is crossed with a homozygous short plant.

Genotypes:

- Tall parent = TT.
- Short parent = tt.

Gametes:

- Tall parent produces T gametes.
- Short parent produces t gametes.

All offspring receive T from one parent and t from the other.

### First Filial Generation (F₁)

Cross:

TT × tt

Punnett square:

| | T | T |
|---|---|---|
| t | Tt | Tt |
| t | Tt | Tt |

Results:

- Genotype: 100% Tt.
- Phenotype: 100% tall.

All F₁ offspring are heterozygous tall plants.

### Second Filial Generation (F₂)

When two F₁ plants are crossed:

Tt × Tt

Punnett square:

| | T | t |
|---|---|---|
| T | TT | Tt |
| t | Tt | tt |

Genotypic ratio:

1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt

Phenotypic ratio:

3 tall : 1 short

Explanation:

- TT is tall.
- Tt is tall because T is dominant.
- tt is short because both alleles are recessive.

Therefore, three out of four possible offspring genotypes show the tall phenotype.

**IMPORTANT:**

The 3:1 phenotypic ratio applies to a typical monohybrid cross between two heterozygous parents under complete dominance, with normal segregation and random fertilisation.

## 11.1.4 Mendel's Law of Segregation

The Law of Segregation states that the two alleles of a gene separate during gamete formation, so each gamete receives only one allele.

For an organism with genotype Tt:

- Some gametes receive T.
- Other gametes receive t.

Each type is expected in equal proportions under normal segregation.

During fertilisation, gametes combine randomly to restore two alleles for the gene in the offspring.

**Why is this important?**

It explains why offspring can inherit different combinations of alleles from the same parents.

## 11.1.5 How to Solve Monohybrid Inheritance Questions

Follow these steps:

1. Identify the characteristic being studied.
2. Identify the dominant and recessive alleles.
3. Assign suitable symbols.
4. Write the parental genotypes.
5. Determine the possible gametes.
6. Draw a Punnett square.
7. Determine the offspring genotypes.
8. Calculate the genotypic ratio.
9. Calculate the phenotypic ratio.
10. State the required probability or percentage.

### Worked Example 1

In a plant species, purple flowers (P) are dominant over white flowers (p).

Two heterozygous purple-flowered plants are crossed.

Determine:

a) The possible offspring genotypes.  
b) The genotypic ratio.  
c) The phenotypic ratio.  
d) The probability of producing a white-flowered plant.

**Step 1: Parental genotypes**

Pp × Pp

**Step 2: Gametes**

First parent: P and p.

Second parent: P and p.

**Step 3: Punnett square**

| | P | p |
|---|---|---|
| P | PP | Pp |
| p | Pp | pp |

**Step 4: Genotypes**

- PP
- Pp
- Pp
- pp

**Answers:**

a) PP, Pp and pp.

b) Genotypic ratio = 1 PP : 2 Pp : 1 pp.

c) Phenotypic ratio = 3 purple : 1 white.

d) Probability of white flowers = 1/4 = 25%.

### Worked Example 2: Test Cross

A plant has a dominant phenotype, but its genotype is unknown.

It could be:

- TT.
- Tt.

A test cross is performed by crossing the organism with a homozygous recessive organism.

Unknown genotype × tt

**Case 1: Unknown plant is TT**

TT × tt

All offspring are Tt.

All offspring show the dominant phenotype.

**Case 2: Unknown plant is Tt**

Tt × tt

| | T | t |
|---|---|---|
| t | Tt | tt |
| t | Tt | tt |

Results:

- 50% Tt.
- 50% tt.
- Phenotypic ratio = 1 dominant : 1 recessive.

**Conclusion:**

A recessive-phenotype offspring indicates that the dominant-phenotype parent carries a recessive allele, assuming the stated inheritance model.

# 11.2 DIHYBRID INHERITANCE

## 11.2.1 Introduction to Dihybrid Inheritance

Dihybrid inheritance is the inheritance of two characteristics controlled by two different genes.

A dihybrid cross studies how alleles of two genes are passed from parents to offspring.

Example:

In pea plants:

- Seed shape: round or wrinkled.
- Seed colour: yellow or green.

For this example:

- R = round seed.
- r = wrinkled seed.
- Y = yellow seed.
- y = green seed.

Assume:

- R is dominant over r.
- Y is dominant over y.
- The genes assort independently.

## 11.2.2 Gametes in Dihybrid Inheritance

A heterozygous dihybrid organism has genotype:

RrYy

During gamete formation, each gamete receives one allele from each gene.

Possible gametes:

1. RY
2. Ry
3. rY
4. ry

Under independent assortment and equal segregation, these four gamete types occur in equal proportions.

**IMPORTANT:**

The possible gametes are not RR, Rr, YY or Yy. A gamete receives only one allele from each gene.

## 11.2.3 Mendel's Law of Independent Assortment

The Law of Independent Assortment states that alleles of different genes can assort independently during gamete formation.

This applies when the genes behave as independently assorting genes, such as genes on different chromosomes or sufficiently far apart on the same chromosome.

It explains how different combinations of alleles can occur in offspring.

## 11.2.4 Dihybrid Cross: RrYy × RrYy

Both parents are heterozygous for both characteristics.

Parent genotypes:

RrYy × RrYy

Possible gametes from each parent:

RY, Ry, rY, ry.

### Punnett Square

| | RY | Ry | rY | ry |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| RY | RRYY | RRYy | RrYY | RrYy |
| Ry | RRYy | RRyy | RrYy | Rryy |
| rY | RrYY | RrYy | rrYY | rrYy |
| ry | RrYy | Rryy | rrYy | rryy |

### Phenotypic Classification

Under complete dominance:

**Round yellow:**

- R_Y_

**Round green:**

- R_yy

**Wrinkled yellow:**

- rrY_

**Wrinkled green:**

- rryy

The underscore represents either possible allele at that position.

### Phenotypic Ratio

For a typical cross of RrYy × RrYy with independent assortment and complete dominance:

9 round yellow : 3 round green : 3 wrinkled yellow : 1 wrinkled green

This is the classic 9:3:3:1 phenotypic ratio.

### Why Does the 9:3:3:1 Ratio Occur?

Each gene separately produces a 3:1 phenotypic ratio.

For seed shape:

- 3/4 round.
- 1/4 wrinkled.

For seed colour:

- 3/4 yellow.
- 1/4 green.

Because the genes assort independently, the probabilities can be multiplied:

Round yellow:

3/4 × 3/4 = 9/16.

Round green:

3/4 × 1/4 = 3/16.

Wrinkled yellow:

1/4 × 3/4 = 3/16.

Wrinkled green:

1/4 × 1/4 = 1/16.

Therefore, the ratio is:

9 : 3 : 3 : 1.

## 11.2.5 Dihybrid Test Cross

A dihybrid test cross involves crossing an organism with a dominant phenotype for both characteristics with an organism that is homozygous recessive for both genes.

Example:

RrYy × rryy

Gametes:

- RrYy parent: RY, Ry, rY, ry.
- rryy parent: ry only.

Offspring:

| Gamete from first parent | Gamete from second parent | Offspring |
|---|---|---|
| RY | ry | RrYy |
| Ry | ry | Rryy |
| rY | ry | rrYy |
| ry | ry | rryy |

Expected phenotypic ratio under independent assortment:

1 round yellow : 1 round green : 1 wrinkled yellow : 1 wrinkled green.

## 11.2.6 Comparing Monohybrid and Dihybrid Inheritance

| Monohybrid inheritance | Dihybrid inheritance |
|---|---|
| Studies one characteristic | Studies two characteristics |
| Involves one gene | Involves two genes |
| Example: plant height | Example: seed shape and colour |
| Heterozygous parent Tt produces T and t gametes | Heterozygous parent RrYy produces RY, Ry, rY and ry gametes |
| Typical F₂ phenotypic ratio is 3:1 | Typical F₂ phenotypic ratio is 9:3:3:1 |

## 11.2.7 Conditions for Mendelian Ratios

The classic Mendelian ratios depend on certain assumptions:

1. Complete dominance occurs.
2. Alleles segregate normally.
3. The genes assort independently for a dihybrid cross.
4. Fertilisation is random.
5. The offspring survive equally.
6. There are sufficiently many offspring for observed results to approach expected ratios.

Actual offspring numbers may differ from expected ratios because of chance.

# 11.3 GENES AND ALLELES

## 11.3.1 Genes and DNA

Genes are sections of DNA that contain instructions for producing functional products, such as proteins or functional RNA.

DNA is the molecule that stores hereditary information.

DNA is made up of nucleotides.

Each nucleotide consists of:

- A phosphate group.
- A deoxyribose sugar.
- A nitrogenous base.

The four bases in DNA are:

- Adenine (A).
- Thymine (T).
- Cytosine (C).
- Guanine (G).

DNA is organised into chromosomes.

A chromosome contains a long DNA molecule associated with proteins.

Genes occupy particular positions on chromosomes called loci.

## 11.3.2 Relationship Between DNA, Genes and Chromosomes

The relationship can be represented as:

DNA → contains genes → organised into chromosomes.

More precisely:

- A chromosome contains a long DNA molecule.
- A gene is a specific section of DNA.
- Different genes are located at different positions on chromosomes.
- Genes contain information that contributes to inherited characteristics.

In diploid organisms, chromosomes generally occur in homologous pairs.

One homologous chromosome is inherited from the mother and the other from the father.

Homologous chromosomes have corresponding gene loci, although the alleles at those loci may differ.

## 11.3.3 Genes and Alleles

A gene may exist in different forms called alleles.

For example, a gene influencing flower colour may have different alleles associated with different flower colours.

An individual typically inherits one allele of an autosomal gene from each biological parent.

The combination of alleles determines the genotype.

Example:

| Genotype | Description |
|---|---|
| AA | Homozygous dominant |
| Aa | Heterozygous |
| aa | Homozygous recessive |

**IMPORTANT:**

An allele is not the same as a gene.

A gene is a section of DNA associated with a characteristic or functional product. An allele is one of the alternative forms of that gene.

## 11.3.4 Dominant and Recessive Alleles

### Dominant Allele

A dominant allele is expressed in a heterozygote under complete dominance.

Example:

A = dominant allele.

a = recessive allele.

Both AA and Aa show the dominant phenotype.

### Recessive Allele

A recessive allele is expressed in the phenotype under complete dominance only when the organism has two recessive alleles.

Example:

aa shows the recessive phenotype.

**IMPORTANT:**

Dominant does not mean:

- More common.
- Better.
- Stronger.
- More beneficial.
- More likely to be inherited.

Dominance describes how an allele is expressed in a particular genotype.

A recessive allele can be common in a population.

## 11.3.5 Homozygous and Heterozygous

### Homozygous

An organism has two identical alleles at a gene locus.

Examples:

- AA.
- aa.

A homozygous organism generally produces one allele type for that gene in its gametes.

### Heterozygous

An organism has two different alleles at a gene locus.

Example:

Aa.

A heterozygous organism can produce gametes carrying either A or a.

## 11.3.6 Genotype and Phenotype

**Genotype** refers to the allele combination of an organism.

**Phenotype** refers to the observable characteristics resulting from the genotype and environmental influences.

Example under complete dominance:

- AA = dominant phenotype.
- Aa = dominant phenotype.
- aa = recessive phenotype.

Different genotypes can produce the same phenotype.

Therefore, an organism's phenotype alone may not reveal its exact genotype.

## 11.3.7 Multiple Alleles

Multiple alleles occur when a gene has more than two alternative alleles in a population.

However, a diploid individual still carries only two alleles of that gene, one on each homologous chromosome.

Example: ABO blood group.

The ABO blood group system involves three common alleles:

- Iᴬ
- Iᴮ
- i

The relationships are:

- Iᴬ and Iᴮ are codominant.
- Both Iᴬ and Iᴮ are dominant over i.

Possible genotypes:

| Genotype | Blood group |
|---|---|
| IᴬIᴬ | A |
| Iᴬi | A |
| IᴮIᴮ | B |
| Iᴮi | B |
| IᴬIᴮ | AB |
| ii | O |

## 11.3.8 Codominance

Codominance occurs when both alleles in a heterozygous individual are expressed clearly in the phenotype.

Example:

AB blood group.

An individual with genotype IᴬIᴮ expresses both A and B antigens on red blood cells.

Neither allele completely masks the other.

## 11.3.9 Incomplete Dominance

Incomplete dominance occurs when the heterozygote has an intermediate phenotype between the two homozygous phenotypes.

Illustrative example:

- RR = red flowers.
- rr = white flowers.
- Rr = pink flowers.

Cross:

Rr × Rr

Punnett square:

| | R | r |
|---|---|---|
| R | RR | Rr |
| r | Rr | rr |

Genotypic ratio:

1 RR : 2 Rr : 1 rr.

Phenotypic ratio:

1 red : 2 pink : 1 white.

**Difference between codominance and incomplete dominance:**

- Codominance: both alleles are expressed in the heterozygote.
- Incomplete dominance: the heterozygote shows an intermediate phenotype.

## 11.3.10 Mutation and Genetic Variation

A mutation is a change in the genetic material.

Mutations can occur in DNA sequences or chromosome structure and number.

Some mutations produce new alleles.

**Causes of mutations:**

- Errors during DNA replication.
- Certain chemicals.
- Ionising radiation.
- Ultraviolet radiation.

**Types of gene mutations:**

1. Substitution: one nucleotide is replaced by another.
2. Insertion: one or more nucleotides are added.
3. Deletion: one or more nucleotides are removed.

Mutations may:

- Have no noticeable effect.
- Alter protein structure or function.
- Affect an organism's phenotype.
- Cause genetic disorders.
- Sometimes produce variation that contributes to evolution.

Mutations in cells that give rise to gametes may be passed to offspring. Mutations in ordinary body cells are generally not inherited by offspring.

# 11.4 INHERITANCE IN HUMANS

## 11.4.1 Introduction to Human Inheritance

Human inheritance refers to the transmission of genetic characteristics from biological parents to their offspring.

Human characteristics are influenced by:

1. Genes inherited from parents.
2. Interactions between genes.
3. Environmental factors.
4. Interactions between genes and the environment.

Examples of inherited human characteristics include:

- ABO blood group.
- Rh blood group.
- Certain inherited disorders.
- Biological sex.
- Some aspects of eye and hair characteristics.

Many human characteristics are controlled by multiple genes and may also be influenced by environmental factors.

## 11.4.2 Human Chromosomes

Most human body cells are diploid and contain 46 chromosomes arranged in 23 pairs.

These include:

- 22 pairs of autosomes.
- 1 pair of sex chromosomes.

Autosomes are chromosomes that are not classified as sex chromosomes.

In the typical human chromosome pattern:

- XX is associated with female biological sex.
- XY is associated with male biological sex.

The 23rd pair consists of sex chromosomes.

**Gametes:**

- Human ovum contains 23 chromosomes, including an X chromosome.
- Human sperm contains 23 chromosomes, including either an X or a Y chromosome.

When fertilisation occurs, the chromosomes combine to form a diploid zygote with 46 chromosomes.

## 11.4.3 Sex Determination in Humans

In typical human sex determination:

- The ovum contributes an X chromosome.
- The sperm contributes either an X or a Y chromosome.

Possible combinations:

| Sperm | Ovum | Zygote chromosome pattern |
|---|---|---|
| X | X | XX |
| Y | X | XY |

Punnett square:

| | X (ovum) | X (ovum) |
|---|---|---|
| X (sperm) | XX | XX |
| Y (sperm) | XY | XY |

Expected probability under this simplified model:

- 50% XX.
- 50% XY.

This is an expected probability, not a guarantee for every pregnancy.

**IMPORTANT EXAM POINT:**

The sperm determines whether the fertilising gamete contributes an X or Y chromosome in the typical XX/XY system.

## 11.4.4 Autosomal Inheritance

Autosomal genes are located on autosomes.

An autosomal characteristic can be inherited by individuals of any biological sex.

### A. Autosomal Dominant Inheritance

A dominant allele can produce the characteristic in a heterozygous individual.

Illustrative example:

- A = dominant allele associated with a particular inherited characteristic.
- a = alternative recessive allele.

Cross:

Aa × aa

Punnett square:

| | A | a |
|---|---|---|
| a | Aa | aa |
| a | Aa | aa |

Results:

- 50% Aa.
- 50% aa.

Expected phenotypic ratio:

1 affected : 1 unaffected, assuming the allele causes the stated characteristic and complete dominance applies.

**Typical pattern:**

- The characteristic may appear in successive generations.
- An affected heterozygous parent can pass the allele to offspring.
- Both sexes can be affected and transmit the allele.

### B. Autosomal Recessive Inheritance

A recessive inherited condition is expressed when an individual inherits two recessive alleles.

Illustrative example:

- A = normal-function allele.
- a = recessive disease-associated allele.

For a simplified recessive condition:

- AA = unaffected, non-carrier.
- Aa = unaffected carrier.
- aa = affected.

Cross between two carriers:

Aa × Aa

Punnett square:

| | A | a |
|---|---|---|
| A | AA | Aa |
| a | Aa | aa |

Expected results:

- 25% AA.
- 50% Aa.
- 25% aa.

Therefore:

- 75% are unaffected.
- 25% are affected.

The unaffected offspring include both carriers and non-carriers.

**IMPORTANT:**

These percentages describe probabilities for each pregnancy under the stated assumptions. They do not mean that exactly one out of every four children in a family must be affected.

## 11.4.5 Sex-Linked Inheritance

Sex-linked inheritance occurs when a gene is located on a sex chromosome.

Many commonly studied sex-linked traits involve genes on the X chromosome.

Males with one X chromosome have only one copy of most X-linked genes.

Therefore, a recessive allele on their X chromosome may be expressed because there is no corresponding allele on the Y chromosome to mask it.

### A. X-Linked Recessive Inheritance

Examples include:

- Red-green colour vision deficiency.
- Haemophilia A.
- Haemophilia B.

For an illustrative X-linked recessive characteristic:

- Xᴺ = allele associated with normal function.
- Xⁿ = recessive allele associated with the condition.
- Y = Y chromosome.

Possible genotypes:

| Genotype | Description |
|---|---|
| XᴺXᴺ | Unaffected female, non-carrier |
| XᴺXⁿ | Unaffected carrier female |
| XⁿXⁿ | Affected female |
| XᴺY | Unaffected male |
| XⁿY | Affected male |

**Why are X-linked recessive conditions often more common in males?**

1. Males typically have one X chromosome.
2. They have only one allele for most X-linked genes.
3. A recessive allele on that X chromosome can be expressed.
4. Females typically have two X chromosomes and usually need two recessive alleles to express a fully recessive X-linked condition.

### B. Example: Carrier Mother and Unaffected Father

Cross:

XᴺXⁿ × XᴺY

Gametes:

- Mother: Xᴺ and Xⁿ.
- Father: Xᴺ and Y.

Punnett square:

| | Xᴺ (father) | Y (father) |
|---|---|---|
| Xᴺ (mother) | XᴺXᴺ | XᴺY |
| Xⁿ (mother) | XᴺXⁿ | XⁿY |

Expected offspring:

- 25% XᴺXᴺ: unaffected non-carrier female.
- 25% XᴺXⁿ: unaffected carrier female.
- 25% XᴺY: unaffected male.
- 25% XⁿY: affected male.

Among sons:

- 50% are expected to be affected.
- 50% are expected to be unaffected.

Among daughters:

- None are expected to be affected in this particular cross.
- 50% are expected to be carriers.

**IMPORTANT:**

An affected father does not pass his X chromosome to his sons. He passes his Y chromosome to his sons and his X chromosome to his daughters.

## 11.4.6 Haemophilia

Haemophilia is an inherited bleeding disorder in which blood clotting is impaired because certain clotting factors are deficient or do not function properly.

Examples:

- Haemophilia A: deficiency of clotting factor VIII.
- Haemophilia B: deficiency of clotting factor IX.

Many common forms of haemophilia A and B follow X-linked recessive inheritance.

**Effects:**

- Bleeding may continue longer than usual.
- Internal bleeding can occur.
- Bleeding into joints can cause pain and damage.
- Severe cases may require regular medical treatment.

**Inheritance example:**

A carrier mother may pass the affected allele to her children.

A son who inherits the affected allele on his X chromosome may have the condition.

A daughter who inherits one affected allele is commonly a carrier, although symptoms can occur in some female carriers.

**IMPORTANT:**

Haemophilia is not caused by blood failing to contain platelets. It is mainly associated with problems involving blood-clotting factors.

## 11.4.7 Red-Green Colour Vision Deficiency

Red-green colour vision deficiency is a condition in which a person has difficulty distinguishing certain shades of red and green.

Common forms are associated with genes on the X chromosome.

**Inheritance:**

- It is commonly inherited as an X-linked recessive characteristic.
- Males are more frequently affected.
- Females may be carriers.
- An affected father passes his X chromosome to all daughters and his Y chromosome to all sons.

The exact colour vision pattern depends on the particular genetic variation.

## 11.4.8 ABO Blood Group Inheritance

The ABO blood group system is controlled by three common alleles:

- Iᴬ
- Iᴮ
- i

Iᴬ and Iᴮ are codominant.

Both are dominant over i.

### Genotypes and Phenotypes

| Blood group | Possible genotype |
|---|---|
| A | IᴬIᴬ or Iᴬi |
| B | IᴮIᴮ or Iᴮi |
| AB | IᴬIᴮ |
| O | ii |

### Example 1: Parent with Blood Group A (Iᴬi) and Parent with Blood Group B (Iᴮi)

Cross:

Iᴬi × Iᴮi

Punnett square:

| | Iᴮ | i |
|---|---|---|
| Iᴬ | IᴬIᴮ | Iᴬi |
| i | Iᴮi | ii |

Possible offspring:

- 25% blood group AB.
- 25% blood group A.
- 25% blood group B.
- 25% blood group O.

This shows that parents with blood groups A and B can have a child with blood group O if both parents carry the i allele.

### Example 2: Parent with Blood Group AB and Parent with Blood Group O

Cross:

IᴬIᴮ × ii

Gametes:

- AB parent: Iᴬ or Iᴮ.
- O parent: i only.

Possible offspring:

- Iᴬi = blood group A.
- Iᴮi = blood group B.

Expected results:

- 50% blood group A.
- 50% blood group B.
- No blood group AB.
- No blood group O.

## 11.4.9 Rh Blood Group System

The Rh blood group system includes several antigens. The D antigen is commonly used to classify a person as Rh-positive or Rh-negative.

For a simplified Mendelian model:

- D = allele associated with the D antigen.
- d = allele associated with absence of the D antigen.

Under this simplified model:

| Genotype | Rh phenotype |
|---|---|
| DD | Rh-positive |
| Dd | Rh-positive |
| dd | Rh-negative |

Rh-positive is dominant over Rh-negative in this simplified model.

### Rh Incompatibility in Pregnancy

Rh incompatibility may occur when:

- The mother is Rh-negative.
- The foetus is Rh-positive.

If foetal red blood cells enter the mother's circulation, the mother's immune system may become sensitised and produce anti-D antibodies.

In a later Rh-positive pregnancy, maternal antibodies may cross the placenta and destroy foetal red blood cells.

This can cause haemolytic disease of the foetus and newborn.

**Prevention:**

Anti-D immunoglobulin can be given to eligible Rh-negative mothers to reduce the risk of sensitisation.

**IMPORTANT:**

Rh incompatibility does not automatically mean that every pregnancy will be affected. Medical assessment and prevention are available.

## 11.4.10 Chromosomal Mutations and Disorders

A chromosome mutation or chromosomal abnormality involves a change in chromosome structure or number.

Some conditions occur when an individual has an extra or missing chromosome.

### A. Down Syndrome

Down syndrome is usually caused by trisomy 21.

This means that a person has three copies of chromosome 21 instead of the usual two.

Possible characteristics include:

- Distinctive facial features.
- Variable learning and developmental differences.
- Low muscle tone in infancy.
- Increased likelihood of certain medical conditions.

The characteristics and support needs vary between individuals.

### B. Turner Syndrome

Turner syndrome commonly involves a person with one complete X chromosome and absence of all or part of the second sex chromosome.

A common chromosome pattern is 45,X.

Possible characteristics include:

- Short stature.
- Reduced ovarian function.
- Delayed or absent puberty without treatment.
- Possible heart or kidney conditions.

### C. Klinefelter Syndrome

Klinefelter syndrome commonly involves a male with an additional X chromosome.

A common chromosome pattern is 47,XXY.

Possible characteristics include:

- Reduced testosterone production.
- Small testes.
- Reduced fertility.
- Taller-than-average stature in some individuals.
- Differences in language or learning development in some individuals.

**IMPORTANT:**

These conditions are not caused by ordinary dominant or recessive inheritance at a single gene locus. They involve chromosome-number abnormalities.

## 11.4.11 Genetic Counselling and Genetic Testing

Genetic counselling is a process in which trained healthcare professionals help individuals and families understand inherited conditions and genetic risks.

It may involve:

- Family medical history.
- Discussion of inheritance patterns.
- Explanation of possible risks to offspring.
- Information about available genetic tests.
- Discussion of medical and reproductive options.

Genetic testing examines DNA, chromosomes or gene products to identify certain genetic variations or chromosome abnormalities.

**Importance:**

1. Helps identify certain inherited conditions.
2. Supports informed medical decisions.
3. Helps assess the possibility of passing certain conditions to offspring.
4. Can support early diagnosis and management.
5. Helps families understand genetic risks.

Genetic testing has limitations. A result may not always predict exactly how severe a condition will be or whether symptoms will occur.

# EXAM TIPS AND COMMON MISTAKES

## Exam Tips

1. Always define the dominant and recessive alleles before drawing a Punnett square.
2. Write the parental genotypes clearly.
3. List all possible gametes before completing the cross.
4. Remember that each gamete receives one allele for each gene.
5. Separate genotypic ratios from phenotypic ratios.
6. For probability questions, count the relevant outcomes and divide by the total number of equally likely outcomes.
7. In dihybrid crosses, list all four possible gametes from a RrYy parent when independent assortment applies.
8. For human inheritance questions, identify whether the gene is autosomal or sex-linked.
9. Use the correct X-linked notation, such as XᴺXⁿ and XⁿY.
10. In ABO blood group questions, remember that Iᴬ and Iᴮ are codominant.
11. In chromosome-number questions, identify the chromosome involved and state whether it is an extra or missing chromosome.
12. Always state the assumptions when a question uses simplified inheritance patterns.

## Common Mistakes

**Mistake 1:** Saying that a dominant allele is always more common.

Correction: Dominance describes expression in a heterozygote, not how common an allele is in a population.

**Mistake 2:** Confusing genotype and phenotype.

Correction: Genotype is the allele combination, while phenotype is the observable characteristic.

**Mistake 3:** Writing two alleles in a gamete.

Correction: A gamete contains only one allele for each gene.

**Mistake 4:** Giving a 3:1 genotypic ratio for Tt × Tt.

Correction: The genotypic ratio is 1:2:1, while the phenotypic ratio under complete dominance is 3:1.

**Mistake 5:** Giving a 9:3:3:1 ratio without checking independent assortment.

Correction: This ratio applies under the usual Mendelian assumptions, including independent assortment and complete dominance.

**Mistake 6:** Saying that a carrier of an autosomal recessive condition is affected.

Correction: A heterozygous carrier is usually unaffected under a simple recessive inheritance model.

**Mistake 7:** Saying that fathers pass their X chromosome to their sons.

Correction: In typical XY inheritance, fathers pass their Y chromosome to sons and their X chromosome to daughters.

**Mistake 8:** Saying that blood group AB is recessive.

Correction: Iᴬ and Iᴮ are codominant.

**Mistake 9:** Confusing Down syndrome with a single-gene inherited condition.

Correction: Down syndrome is usually caused by an extra copy of chromosome 21.

# SPM-STYLE PRACTICE QUESTIONS

## QUESTION 1 — 4 MARKS

Define the following terms:

a) Gene.  
b) Allele.  
c) Genotype.  
d) Phenotype.

**Suggested answer:**

a) A gene is a section of DNA that contains information for a functional product or influences an inherited characteristic.

b) An allele is an alternative form of a gene.

c) Genotype is the genetic constitution or allele combination of an organism.

d) Phenotype is the observable characteristic resulting from the genotype and environmental influences.

## QUESTION 2 — 4 MARKS

A heterozygous tall plant (Tt) is crossed with another heterozygous tall plant (Tt). Tallness is dominant over shortness.

Determine:

a) The genotypic ratio.  
b) The phenotypic ratio.

**Suggested answer:**

Cross: Tt × Tt.

| | T | t |
|---|---|---|
| T | TT | Tt |
| t | Tt | tt |

a) Genotypic ratio = 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt.

b) Phenotypic ratio = 3 tall : 1 short.

## QUESTION 3 — 3 MARKS

Explain Mendel's Law of Segregation.

**Suggested answer:**

1. An organism has two alleles for a gene.
2. The alleles separate during gamete formation.
3. Each gamete receives only one allele.

## QUESTION 4 — 4 MARKS

In a plant, purple flower colour (P) is dominant over white flower colour (p). Two heterozygous plants are crossed.

Calculate the probability of producing white-flowered offspring.

**Suggested answer:**

Cross: Pp × Pp.

Possible offspring:

- PP.
- Pp.
- Pp.
- pp.

Only pp produces white flowers.

Probability = 1/4 × 100% = 25%.

## QUESTION 5 — 4 MARKS

A dihybrid plant has genotype RrYy.

State all possible gametes produced by this plant, assuming independent assortment.

**Suggested answer:**

1. RY.
2. Ry.
3. rY.
4. ry.

## QUESTION 6 — 4 MARKS

Two plants with genotype RrYy are crossed. Round seeds (R) are dominant over wrinkled seeds (r), while yellow seeds (Y) are dominant over green seeds (y).

State the expected phenotypic ratio.

**Suggested answer:**

The expected phenotypic ratio is:

9 round yellow : 3 round green : 3 wrinkled yellow : 1 wrinkled green.

This ratio applies under complete dominance and independent assortment.

## QUESTION 7 — 4 MARKS

Differentiate between codominance and incomplete dominance.

**Suggested answer:**

1. Codominance occurs when both alleles are expressed in the heterozygote.
2. An example is AB blood group.
3. Incomplete dominance occurs when the heterozygote has an intermediate phenotype.
4. An illustrative example is a pink flower produced from red and white flower parents.

## QUESTION 8 — 4 MARKS

Explain why males are more frequently affected by some X-linked recessive conditions.

**Suggested answer:**

1. Males typically have only one X chromosome.
2. They possess only one allele for most X-linked genes.
3. A recessive allele on their X chromosome can therefore be expressed.
4. Females usually need two recessive alleles to express a fully recessive X-linked condition.

## QUESTION 9 — 4 MARKS

A mother is a carrier of an X-linked recessive condition, while the father is unaffected.

Determine the expected proportion of affected sons.

**Suggested answer:**

Cross:

XᴺXⁿ × XᴺY.

Among sons:

- XᴺY = unaffected.
- XⁿY = affected.

Expected proportion of affected sons = 1/2 = 50%.

## QUESTION 10 — 4 MARKS

A couple has genotypes Iᴬi and Iᴮi for the ABO blood group system.

State the possible blood groups of their children.

**Suggested answer:**

Cross:

Iᴬi × Iᴮi.

Possible genotypes:

- IᴬIᴮ = AB.
- Iᴬi = A.
- Iᴮi = B.
- ii = O.

All four blood groups are possible, each with an expected probability of 25%.

## QUESTION 11 — 4 MARKS

Explain the difference between autosomal and sex-linked inheritance.

**Suggested answer:**

1. Autosomal inheritance involves genes located on autosomes.
2. Autosomal characteristics can affect individuals of any biological sex.
3. Sex-linked inheritance involves genes located on sex chromosomes.
4. Sex-linked inheritance patterns may differ between males and females.

## QUESTION 12 — 4 MARKS

Explain how Down syndrome occurs.

**Suggested answer:**

1. Down syndrome is usually caused by trisomy 21.
2. The individual has three copies of chromosome 21.
3. This results in an additional copy of genetic material from chromosome 21.
4. The additional genetic material affects development and may cause characteristic physical and learning differences.

## QUESTION 13 — 5 MARKS

Compare haemophilia and red-green colour vision deficiency.

**Suggested answer:**

1. Both commonly follow X-linked recessive inheritance.
2. Both are associated with genes on the X chromosome.
3. Haemophilia affects the blood-clotting process.
4. Red-green colour vision deficiency affects the ability to distinguish certain colours.
5. Both conditions are more frequently expressed in males than females.

## QUESTION 14 — 5 MARKS

Explain why two unaffected parents may have a child with an autosomal recessive inherited condition.

**Suggested answer:**

1. Both parents may be heterozygous carriers.
2. Each parent has one dominant and one recessive allele.
3. Each parent can pass either allele to their offspring.
4. The offspring may inherit the recessive allele from both parents.
5. The offspring then has a homozygous recessive genotype and may express the condition.

## QUESTION 15 — 6 MARKS

A plant breeder crosses two heterozygous plants for seed shape and seed colour.

Explain how independent assortment produces different combinations of characteristics in the offspring.

**Suggested answer:**

1. Each parent has genotype RrYy.
2. The alleles separate during gamete formation.
3. Each gamete receives one allele for seed shape and one allele for seed colour.
4. Four gamete combinations can form: RY, Ry, rY and ry.
5. Fertilisation combines gametes from the two parents.
6. Different allele combinations produce different offspring genotypes and phenotypes.

# FINAL CHAPTER 11 REVISION SUMMARY

## 11.1 Monohybrid Inheritance

- Studies one characteristic controlled by one gene.
- A gene may have different alleles.
- Dominant alleles are expressed in heterozygotes under complete dominance.
- Recessive alleles are expressed when no dominant allele masks them.
- Tt × Tt gives a typical genotypic ratio of 1:2:1.
- Under complete dominance, the phenotypic ratio is 3:1.
- Mendel's Law of Segregation explains how alleles separate during gamete formation.

## 11.2 Dihybrid Inheritance

- Studies two characteristics controlled by two genes.
- A RrYy parent can produce RY, Ry, rY and ry gametes.
- Independent assortment produces different allele combinations.
- RrYy × RrYy gives the typical phenotypic ratio 9:3:3:1 under the usual Mendelian assumptions.
- A dihybrid test cross can produce a 1:1:1:1 ratio under independent assortment.

## 11.3 Genes and Alleles

- Genes are sections of DNA.
- Alleles are alternative forms of a gene.
- Genes are located at specific loci on chromosomes.
- Genotype describes allele combinations.
- Phenotype describes observable characteristics.
- Homozygous means two identical alleles.
- Heterozygous means two different alleles.
- Codominance expresses both alleles.
- Incomplete dominance produces an intermediate heterozygous phenotype.
- Multiple alleles means more than two allele forms exist in a population.

## 11.4 Inheritance in Humans

- Most human body cells contain 46 chromosomes.
- Human gametes contain 23 chromosomes.
- Autosomal genes are located on autosomes.
- Sex-linked genes are located on sex chromosomes.
- Typical human sex determination involves XX and XY chromosome patterns.
- Haemophilia and common red-green colour vision deficiencies often follow X-linked recessive inheritance.
- ABO blood groups are controlled by Iᴬ, Iᴮ and i alleles.
- Iᴬ and Iᴮ are codominant.
- Rh incompatibility can occur when an Rh-negative mother carries an Rh-positive foetus.
- Down syndrome is usually caused by trisomy 21.
- Genetic counselling helps individuals understand inherited conditions and genetic risks.

**FINAL EXAM REMINDER:**

For every inheritance question:

1. Identify the genes and alleles.
2. Determine the parental genotypes.
3. List the gametes.
4. Draw the Punnett square.
5. Identify offspring genotypes.
6. Determine phenotypes.
7. Calculate the requested ratio or probability.
8. State the conclusion clearly.

Always check whether the question involves complete dominance, codominance, incomplete dominance, autosomal inheritance or sex-linked inheritance before choosing your method.
