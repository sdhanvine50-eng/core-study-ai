# 2.1 STRUCTURE OF A LEAF

## 2.1.1 INTRODUCTION TO LEAVES

A leaf is a plant organ that is mainly adapted for:

1. Gaseous exchange.
2. Transpiration.
3. Photosynthesis.

Most leaves are broad and flat. This provides a large surface area for absorbing sunlight and exchanging gases.

A typical leaf consists of:

- Leaf blade (lamina).
- Petiole.
- Midrib.
- Veins.
- Leaf margin.
- Leaf apex.
- Leaf base.

## 2.1.2 EXTERNAL STRUCTURE OF A LEAF

**A. LEAF BLADE (LAMINA)**

The leaf blade is the broad, flat part of the leaf.

Functions:

1. Provides a large surface area for absorbing sunlight.
2. Allows gaseous exchange with the surrounding air.
3. Contains photosynthetic tissues where photosynthesis occurs.

**B. PETIOLE**

The petiole is the stalk that attaches the leaf blade to the stem.

Functions:

1. Holds the leaf blade in a suitable position to receive sunlight.
2. Connects the leaf to the stem.
3. Contains vascular tissues that transport substances.

**C. MIDRIB**

The midrib is the main central vein of the leaf.

Functions:

1. Provides mechanical support.
2. Contains vascular tissues.
3. Helps distribute water and dissolved substances through the leaf.

**D. VEINS**

Veins are vascular structures that extend throughout the leaf blade.

They contain:

- Xylem vessels.
- Phloem tissues.

Functions:

1. Xylem transports water and mineral salts to the leaf.
2. Phloem transports organic substances, including sucrose, from photosynthetic tissues to other parts of the plant.
3. Veins provide support to the leaf blade.

## 2.1.3 INTERNAL STRUCTURE OF A LEAF

A typical leaf consists of several layers of specialised tissues.

These include:

1. Upper epidermis.
2. Palisade mesophyll.
3. Spongy mesophyll.
4. Lower epidermis.
5. Vascular bundle.
6. Stomata and guard cells.

**A. UPPER EPIDERMIS**

The upper epidermis is the outermost cell layer on the upper surface of the leaf.

Characteristics:

- Usually consists of a single layer of closely packed cells.
- Cells are generally transparent.
- Often covered by a waxy cuticle.

Functions:

1. Protects internal leaf tissues.
2. Allows light to pass through to photosynthetic cells.
3. The cuticle reduces excessive water loss.

**B. CUTICLE**

The cuticle is a thin, waxy protective layer covering the epidermis.

Functions:

1. Reduces water loss through evaporation.
2. Protects the leaf surface.
3. Helps prevent excessive dehydration.

The cuticle is generally thicker in plants adapted to dry environments.

**C. PALISADE MESOPHYLL**

Palisade mesophyll is found beneath the upper epidermis.

Characteristics:

- Cells are elongated.
- Cells are closely packed.
- Cells contain many chloroplasts.
- Usually the main photosynthetic tissue in a typical leaf.

Functions:

1. Absorbs light energy.
2. Carries out a large proportion of photosynthesis.
3. Produces carbohydrates during photosynthesis.

**D. SPONGY MESOPHYLL**

Spongy mesophyll is usually found below the palisade mesophyll.

Characteristics:

- Cells are irregularly shaped.
- Cells are loosely arranged.
- Large intercellular air spaces are present.
- Cells generally contain fewer chloroplasts than palisade mesophyll cells.

Functions:

1. Provides air spaces for gaseous exchange.
2. Allows carbon dioxide to diffuse towards photosynthetic cells.
3. Allows oxygen and water vapour to diffuse through the leaf.

**E. LOWER EPIDERMIS**

The lower epidermis forms the outermost cell layer on the lower surface of the leaf.

Characteristics:

- Usually consists of closely packed cells.
- Contains many stomata in many terrestrial plants.
- Protects internal leaf tissues.

Functions:

1. Protects the leaf.
2. Contains stomata that regulate gaseous exchange and water loss.

**F. VASCULAR BUNDLE**

The vascular bundle is the transport system found within the leaf veins.

It consists mainly of xylem and phloem.

**Xylem:**

- Usually located towards the upper side of the vascular bundle in a typical dorsiventral leaf.
- Transports water and mineral salts from the roots to the leaves.
- Also provides mechanical support.

**Phloem:**

- Usually located towards the lower side of the vascular bundle in a typical dorsiventral leaf.
- Transports sucrose and other organic substances from sources to sinks.

IMPORTANT:

The exact arrangement of vascular tissues may differ between plant species and leaf types.

## 2.1.4 STOMATA AND GUARD CELLS

A stoma is a small pore found in the epidermis of a leaf.

The opening is surrounded by two specialised guard cells.

**GUARD CELLS**

Guard cells control the opening and closing of stomata.

Characteristics:

- Usually kidney-shaped in many dicotyledonous plants.
- Contain chloroplasts.
- Have unevenly thickened cell walls.
- The inner wall, facing the stomatal pore, is thicker than the outer wall.

**FUNCTIONS OF STOMATA**

1. Allow carbon dioxide to enter the leaf.
2. Allow oxygen to leave the leaf.
3. Allow water vapour to diffuse out during transpiration.
4. Help regulate water loss.

**OPENING OF STOMATA**

When guard cells take up water by osmosis:

1. Guard cells become turgid.
2. Their outer walls expand more than their thicker inner walls.
3. The guard cells curve away from one another.
4. The stomatal pore opens.

**CLOSING OF STOMATA**

When guard cells lose water:

1. Guard cells become flaccid.
2. Their pressure decreases.
3. The guard cells move closer together.
4. The stomatal pore closes.

Stomatal movement is influenced by factors such as light, carbon dioxide concentration, water availability and environmental conditions.

## 2.1.5 ADAPTATIONS OF LEAVES FOR THEIR FUNCTIONS

| Leaf structure | Adaptation and function |
|---|---|
| Broad leaf blade | Large surface area for absorbing light |
| Thin leaf blade | Short diffusion distance for gases |
| Transparent upper epidermis | Allows light to reach mesophyll cells |
| Palisade mesophyll | Contains many chloroplasts for photosynthesis |
| Spongy mesophyll | Contains air spaces for gaseous exchange |
| Stomata | Allow gaseous exchange and water vapour loss |
| Guard cells | Regulate stomatal opening |
| Veins | Transport substances and support the leaf |
| Cuticle | Reduces excessive water loss |

## 2.1.6 EXAM TIPS

**Question: Explain why palisade mesophyll cells contain many chloroplasts.**

Suggested answer:

1. Chloroplasts contain chlorophyll.
2. Chlorophyll absorbs light energy.
3. Light energy is used during photosynthesis.

**Question: Explain why spongy mesophyll contains large air spaces.**

Suggested answer:

1. Air spaces allow gases to diffuse through the leaf.
2. Carbon dioxide can diffuse towards photosynthetic cells.
3. Oxygen and water vapour can diffuse towards the stomata.

# 2.2 LEAF AS THE MAIN ORGAN FOR GASEOUS EXCHANGE

## 2.2.1 INTRODUCTION TO GASEOUS EXCHANGE

Gaseous exchange is the movement of gases between an organism and its surrounding environment.

In plants, gaseous exchange mainly occurs through stomata in leaves.

The gases involved include:

- Carbon dioxide.
- Oxygen.
- Water vapour.

Gaseous exchange in plants is important for photosynthesis and cellular respiration.

## 2.2.2 GASEOUS EXCHANGE DURING PHOTOSYNTHESIS

During photosynthesis:

1. Carbon dioxide from the atmosphere enters the leaf through the stomata.
2. Carbon dioxide diffuses through the intercellular air spaces.
3. Carbon dioxide reaches mesophyll cells.
4. Carbon dioxide is used during photosynthesis.
5. Oxygen produced during photosynthesis diffuses out of the leaf through the stomata.

## 2.2.3 GASEOUS EXCHANGE DURING CELLULAR RESPIRATION

During cellular respiration:

1. Oxygen diffuses into plant cells.
2. Oxygen is used to release energy from organic substances.
3. Carbon dioxide is produced.
4. Carbon dioxide diffuses out of the cells.

Cellular respiration occurs continuously in living plant cells, during both day and night.

IMPORTANT:

Photosynthesis requires light, whereas cellular respiration does not directly require light.

During daylight, photosynthesis and cellular respiration can occur simultaneously.

## 2.2.4 MECHANISM OF GASEOUS EXCHANGE

Gases move through the leaf mainly by diffusion.

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration.

The direction of gas movement depends on the concentration gradient.

**WHEN PHOTOSYNTHESIS IS ACTIVE:**

- Carbon dioxide is used by photosynthetic cells.
- Carbon dioxide concentration inside the leaf may decrease.
- Carbon dioxide diffuses into the leaf.
- Oxygen produced during photosynthesis may diffuse out.

**WHEN PHOTOSYNTHESIS IS NOT OCCURRING:**

- Cellular respiration continues.
- Oxygen is used by cells.
- Carbon dioxide is produced.
- Oxygen may diffuse into the leaf.
- Carbon dioxide may diffuse out.

The actual direction and rate of gas exchange depend on the balance between photosynthesis, respiration and environmental conditions.

## 2.2.5 IMPORTANCE OF STOMATA IN GASEOUS EXCHANGE

Stomata provide openings that allow gases to move between the leaf and the atmosphere.

Guard cells regulate the opening and closing of the stomata.

This helps plants balance:

- Carbon dioxide uptake.
- Oxygen release.
- Water loss through transpiration.

Closing stomata can reduce water loss, but it also restricts carbon dioxide entry.

## 2.2.6 EXAM TIPS

**Question: Explain how carbon dioxide enters a leaf during photosynthesis.**

Suggested answer:

1. Photosynthetic cells use carbon dioxide during photosynthesis.
2. This creates a concentration gradient.
3. Carbon dioxide diffuses from the atmosphere through the stomata.
4. It moves through the intercellular air spaces towards mesophyll cells.

**Question: Why do plants still need oxygen at night?**

Suggested answer:

1. Cellular respiration occurs continuously in living plant cells.
2. Oxygen is required for aerobic respiration.
3. Respiration releases energy needed for cellular activities.

# 2.3 LEAF AS THE MAIN ORGAN FOR TRANSPIRATION

## 2.3.1 INTRODUCTION TO TRANSPIRATION

Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from the aerial parts of a plant, mainly through the stomata of leaves.

Water is absorbed from the soil by root hairs.

It moves through the xylem vessels towards the leaves.

At the leaf:

1. Water moves from the xylem into mesophyll cells.
2. Water evaporates from the moist surfaces of mesophyll cells.
3. Water vapour enters the intercellular air spaces.
4. Water vapour diffuses out through the stomata.

## 2.3.2 MECHANISM OF TRANSPIRATION

The process can be divided into several stages.

**STAGE 1: WATER TRANSPORT TO THE LEAF**

- Water is absorbed by root hair cells.
- Water moves through the root tissues.
- Water enters the xylem vessels.
- Water is transported upwards towards the leaves.

**STAGE 2: EVAPORATION**

- Water reaches the leaf through the xylem.
- Water moves into mesophyll cells.
- Water evaporates from the moist cell walls into the air spaces.

**STAGE 3: DIFFUSION**

- Water vapour accumulates in the intercellular air spaces.
- Water vapour diffuses out through open stomata.
- Water is lost to the atmosphere.

**STAGE 4: TRANSPIRATION PULL**

- Water loss from the leaf reduces the water potential of the leaf tissues.
- Water is drawn from neighbouring cells and the xylem.
- Tension develops in the water column inside the xylem.
- This tension helps pull water upwards from the roots.

## 2.3.3 TRANSPIRATION PULL

Transpiration pull is the pulling force created by water loss from the leaves.

It is an important mechanism responsible for the upward movement of water in plants.

The process depends partly on two properties of water:

**COHESION**

Cohesion is the attraction between molecules of the same substance.

Hydrogen bonds help water molecules remain connected.

Biological importance:

- Maintains a continuous column of water inside the xylem.
- Allows tension to be transmitted along the water column.

**ADHESION**

Adhesion is the attraction between molecules of different substances.

Water molecules are attracted to the inner walls of xylem vessels.

Biological importance:

- Helps water remain in contact with xylem walls.
- Supports the movement of water through narrow vessels.

## 2.3.4 FACTORS AFFECTING TRANSPIRATION RATE

**A. LIGHT INTENSITY**

Generally, higher light intensity increases the rate of transpiration.

Explanation:

1. Light stimulates stomatal opening in many plants.
2. More stomata become open.
3. More water vapour can diffuse out.
4. Transpiration rate increases.

**B. TEMPERATURE**

Generally, higher temperature increases the rate of transpiration.

Explanation:

1. Water molecules gain more kinetic energy.
2. Evaporation from mesophyll surfaces becomes faster.
3. Water vapour diffuses out more rapidly.
4. Transpiration rate increases.

**C. HUMIDITY**

Humidity is the amount of water vapour present in the air.

Generally, high humidity decreases the rate of transpiration.

Explanation:

1. The difference in water vapour concentration between the leaf and the surrounding air becomes smaller.
2. The diffusion gradient decreases.
3. Water vapour diffuses out more slowly.
4. Transpiration rate decreases.

**D. WIND SPEED**

Generally, higher wind speed increases the rate of transpiration.

Explanation:

1. Wind removes water vapour that accumulates around the leaf surface.
2. The water vapour concentration around the leaf decreases.
3. A steeper diffusion gradient is maintained.
4. Transpiration rate increases.

However, extremely strong wind may cause stomata to close in some plants.

**E. WATER AVAILABILITY**

When water is limited:

1. Plant cells may lose turgor.
2. Abscisic acid (ABA) may promote stomatal closure.
3. Less water vapour diffuses out.
4. Transpiration rate decreases.

## 2.3.5 IMPORTANCE OF TRANSPIRATION

1. Helps create transpiration pull for water transport.
2. Supports the transport of mineral salts from roots to leaves.
3. Helps cool plant surfaces through evaporation.
4. Maintains the movement of water through the plant.
5. Supports the supply of water needed for photosynthesis and other metabolic processes.

## 2.3.6 EXCESSIVE TRANSPIRATION

When water loss is faster than water absorption, a plant may experience excessive water loss.

Possible effects:

- Loss of turgor.
- Wilting.
- Reduced stomatal opening.
- Reduced photosynthesis.
- Slower growth.

Wilting occurs when plant cells lose sufficient water and become less turgid.

## 2.3.7 EXPERIMENTAL STUDY OF TRANSPIRATION

A potometer is an apparatus used to estimate the rate of water uptake by a plant shoot.

It does not measure transpiration directly because some absorbed water may be used or retained by the plant.

**BASIC PRINCIPLE:**

1. A leafy shoot is connected to a water-filled apparatus.
2. An air bubble is introduced into a capillary tube.
3. Water uptake causes the bubble to move.
4. The distance travelled by the bubble is measured over a period of time.

**CALCULATION:**

Rate of water uptake can be estimated using:

Rate = Distance moved by air bubble ÷ Time taken

If the capillary tube has a known cross-sectional area:

Volume of water taken up = Cross-sectional area × Distance moved

Rate of water uptake = Volume of water taken up ÷ Time taken

Units must be stated correctly.

IMPORTANT:

A potometer estimates water uptake, which is used as an indirect estimate of transpiration rate.

## 2.3.8 EXAM TIPS

**Question: Explain why transpiration increases when wind speed increases.**

Suggested answer:

1. Wind removes water vapour surrounding the leaf.
2. Water vapour concentration around the leaf decreases.
3. The diffusion gradient becomes steeper.
4. Water vapour diffuses out more rapidly.
5. Transpiration rate increases.

**Question: Explain how transpiration helps water move from roots to leaves.**

Suggested answer:

1. Water evaporates from mesophyll cell surfaces.
2. Water vapour diffuses out through the stomata.
3. Water loss creates tension in the xylem.
4. Cohesion maintains a continuous water column.
5. Water is pulled upwards from the roots towards the leaves.

# 2.4 LEAF AS THE MAIN ORGAN FOR PHOTOSYNTHESIS

## 2.4.1 INTRODUCTION TO PHOTOSYNTHESIS

Photosynthesis is the process by which green plants use light energy to synthesise organic substances from carbon dioxide and water.

Oxygen is released as a by-product.

Photosynthesis mainly takes place in chloroplasts found in green plant cells.

The leaf is the main organ for photosynthesis because it contains many chloroplasts, especially in the palisade mesophyll.

## 2.4.2 PHOTOSYNTHESIS EQUATION

Word equation:

Carbon dioxide + Water → Glucose + Oxygen

Light energy and chlorophyll are required.

Balanced chemical equation:

6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

This is a simplified overall representation of photosynthesis.

## 2.4.3 STRUCTURE AND FUNCTION OF CHLOROPLASTS

Chloroplasts are specialised organelles where photosynthesis occurs.

Main structures:

**A. OUTER AND INNER MEMBRANES**

- Surround the chloroplast.
- Separate the chloroplast contents from the cytoplasm.
- Help regulate the movement of substances.

**B. STROMA**

The stroma is the fluid-filled region inside the inner membrane.

Functions:

- Contains enzymes involved in the Calvin cycle.
- Provides the environment for carbon fixation and sugar production.

**C. THYLAKOIDS**

Thylakoids are flattened membrane sacs inside chloroplasts.

Functions:

- Contain chlorophyll and other photosynthetic pigments.
- Contain components of the electron transport chain.
- Are the sites of the light-dependent reactions.

**D. GRANA**

Grana are stacks of thylakoids.

Functions:

- Increase the surface area available for light-dependent reactions.
- Contain photosynthetic pigments.

**E. CHLOROPHYLL**

Chlorophyll is a green pigment found in the thylakoid membranes.

Function:

- Absorbs light energy required for photosynthesis.

## 2.4.4 ADAPTATIONS OF LEAVES FOR PHOTOSYNTHESIS

1. Broad leaf blade provides a large surface area to absorb sunlight.
2. Thin leaf structure allows gases to diffuse over a short distance.
3. Transparent upper epidermis allows light to reach photosynthetic cells.
4. Palisade mesophyll contains many chloroplasts.
5. Spongy mesophyll provides air spaces for carbon dioxide diffusion.
6. Stomata allow carbon dioxide to enter.
7. Xylem transports water to the leaf.
8. Phloem transports sugars away from photosynthetic tissues.

## 2.4.5 FACTORS AFFECTING THE RATE OF PHOTOSYNTHESIS

**A. LIGHT INTENSITY**

Light provides energy for photosynthesis.

Generally:

1. Increasing light intensity increases the rate of photosynthesis.
2. More light energy is absorbed by photosynthetic pigments.
3. The rate increases until another factor becomes limiting.

At high light intensity, the rate may reach a maximum and stop increasing significantly.

**B. CARBON DIOXIDE CONCENTRATION**

Carbon dioxide is a raw material used in photosynthesis.

Generally:

1. Increasing carbon dioxide concentration increases the rate of photosynthesis.
2. More carbon dioxide is available for carbon fixation.
3. The rate increases until another factor becomes limiting.

**C. TEMPERATURE**

Photosynthesis involves enzyme-controlled reactions.

Generally:

1. Increasing temperature within a suitable range increases enzyme activity.
2. The rate of photosynthesis increases.
3. Beyond the optimum temperature, enzymes may lose their normal structure.
4. The rate of photosynthesis decreases.

**D. WATER AVAILABILITY**

Water is a raw material for photosynthesis.

When water is insufficient:

1. Stomata may close.
2. Carbon dioxide entry decreases.
3. Photosynthesis becomes slower.
4. Prolonged water shortage may damage plant tissues.

## 2.4.6 LIMITING FACTORS OF PHOTOSYNTHESIS

A limiting factor is a factor that restricts the rate of a process when it is in insufficient supply.

The main limiting factors of photosynthesis include:

- Light intensity.
- Carbon dioxide concentration.
- Temperature.
- Water availability.

The rate of photosynthesis is restricted by the factor that is most limiting at that time.

For example:

If light intensity is very low, increasing carbon dioxide concentration may have little effect because light is still limiting.

If carbon dioxide concentration is low, increasing light intensity may not significantly increase the rate until more carbon dioxide becomes available.

## 2.4.7 FATE OF GLUCOSE PRODUCED DURING PHOTOSYNTHESIS

Glucose produced during photosynthesis can be used in several ways.

1. **Cellular respiration:** Glucose is broken down to release energy.
2. **Starch formation:** Glucose is converted into starch for storage.
3. **Sucrose formation:** Glucose is used to produce sucrose for transport.
4. **Cellulose formation:** Glucose is used to build plant cell walls.
5. **Lipid synthesis:** Glucose can contribute to the production of lipids.
6. **Amino acid synthesis:** Glucose-derived compounds can be used to produce amino acids when nitrogen is available.

## 2.4.8 IMPORTANCE OF PHOTOSYNTHESIS

1. Produces organic substances that provide energy and materials for growth.
2. Releases oxygen into the atmosphere.
3. Removes carbon dioxide from the atmosphere.
4. Forms the basis of many food chains.
5. Converts light energy into chemical energy stored in organic molecules.

## 2.4.9 EXAM TIPS

**Question: Explain why leaves are suitable organs for photosynthesis.**

Suggested answer:

1. Leaves have a broad surface area to absorb light.
2. The upper epidermis is transparent, allowing light to reach the mesophyll.
3. Palisade mesophyll cells contain many chloroplasts.
4. Stomata allow carbon dioxide to enter.
5. Veins transport water to the leaf.

**Question: Explain why increasing light intensity does not always increase the rate of photosynthesis.**

Suggested answer:

1. Light intensity may no longer be the limiting factor.
2. Another factor, such as carbon dioxide concentration or temperature, may limit photosynthesis.
3. Therefore, increasing light intensity does not significantly increase the rate.

# 2.5 COMPENSATION POINT

## 2.5.1 DEFINITION OF COMPENSATION POINT

The compensation point is the light intensity at which the rate of photosynthesis is equal to the rate of cellular respiration.

At the compensation point:

- The rate of carbon dioxide uptake through photosynthesis equals the rate of carbon dioxide release through respiration.
- The rate of oxygen production through photosynthesis equals the rate of oxygen consumption through respiration.
- There is no net exchange of carbon dioxide or oxygen due to these two processes.

IMPORTANT:

Photosynthesis and cellular respiration still occur at the compensation point. They do not stop.

## 2.5.2 PHOTOSYNTHESIS AND RESPIRATION AT DIFFERENT LIGHT INTENSITIES

**A. BELOW THE COMPENSATION POINT**

When light intensity is below the compensation point:

1. The rate of photosynthesis is lower than the rate of respiration.
2. More carbon dioxide is released through respiration than is absorbed through photosynthesis.
3. There is a net release of carbon dioxide.
4. The plant uses more stored organic material than it produces through photosynthesis.

**B. AT THE COMPENSATION POINT**

When light intensity reaches the compensation point:

1. The rate of photosynthesis equals the rate of respiration.
2. Carbon dioxide uptake equals carbon dioxide release.
3. Oxygen production equals oxygen consumption.
4. There is no net gaseous exchange caused by the two processes.

**C. ABOVE THE COMPENSATION POINT**

When light intensity is above the compensation point:

1. The rate of photosynthesis is higher than the rate of respiration.
2. More carbon dioxide is absorbed than released.
3. There is a net uptake of carbon dioxide.
4. More organic material is produced than is used in respiration.

## 2.5.3 LIGHT INTENSITY AND THE COMPENSATION POINT

The compensation point can be represented using a graph.

- The horizontal axis represents light intensity.
- The vertical axis represents the rate of photosynthesis and respiration, or net carbon dioxide exchange.
- The compensation point occurs where the photosynthesis and respiration rates are equal.

When plotting net photosynthesis:

- Below the compensation point, net photosynthesis is negative.
- At the compensation point, net photosynthesis is zero.
- Above the compensation point, net photosynthesis is positive.

## 2.5.4 IMPORTANCE OF THE COMPENSATION POINT

The compensation point helps explain:

1. Why plants need sufficient light for net production of organic substances.
2. How light intensity affects plant growth.
3. Why plants growing in shaded environments may have different adaptations.
4. Why a plant may use stored food when photosynthesis is lower than respiration.

A plant generally needs to receive enough light over time to produce more organic material through photosynthesis than it consumes through respiration if it is to increase its biomass.

## 2.5.5 EXAM TIPS

**Question: Define the compensation point.**

Suggested answer:

The compensation point is the light intensity at which the rate of photosynthesis is equal to the rate of cellular respiration.

**Question: Explain what happens below the compensation point.**

Suggested answer:

1. The rate of photosynthesis is lower than the rate of respiration.
2. Carbon dioxide release through respiration exceeds carbon dioxide uptake through photosynthesis.
3. There is a net loss of organic material because respiration uses more organic substances than photosynthesis produces.

**Question: Explain why a plant may not grow well when light intensity remains below its compensation point.**

Suggested answer:

1. The rate of photosynthesis is lower than the rate of respiration.
2. The plant produces less organic material than it uses.
3. Less organic material is available for growth.
4. The plant may lose biomass over time.

# CHAPTER 2 — COMPARISON AND REVISION

## 1. MAIN LEAF STRUCTURES

| Structure | Main function |
|---|---|
| Upper epidermis | Protection and light transmission |
| Cuticle | Reduces water loss |
| Palisade mesophyll | Main photosynthetic tissue |
| Spongy mesophyll | Gaseous exchange |
| Lower epidermis | Protection and stomatal location |
| Stomata | Gaseous exchange and water vapour loss |
| Guard cells | Regulate stomatal opening |
| Xylem | Transports water and mineral salts |
| Phloem | Transports organic substances |
| Veins | Transport and support |

## 2. GASEOUS EXCHANGE, TRANSPIRATION AND PHOTOSYNTHESIS

**GASEOUS EXCHANGE**

- Movement of gases by diffusion.
- Mainly occurs through stomata.
- Involves carbon dioxide and oxygen.

**TRANSPIRATION**

- Loss of water vapour from aerial parts of plants.
- Mainly occurs through stomata.
- Creates transpiration pull.

**PHOTOSYNTHESIS**

- Production of organic substances using light energy.
- Mainly occurs in chloroplasts.
- Uses carbon dioxide and water.
- Releases oxygen.

## 3. IMPORTANT DEFINITIONS

- **Diffusion:** Net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration.
- **Stoma:** A pore in the leaf epidermis that allows gaseous exchange.
- **Guard cells:** Specialised cells that control the opening and closing of stomata.
- **Transpiration:** Loss of water vapour from the aerial parts of a plant.
- **Transpiration pull:** Tension created by water loss from leaves that helps draw water upwards through xylem.
- **Photosynthesis:** Process by which plants use light energy to synthesise organic substances from carbon dioxide and water.
- **Limiting factor:** A factor that restricts the rate of a process when it is in insufficient supply.
- **Compensation point:** Light intensity at which photosynthesis and cellular respiration occur at equal rates.

# COMMON EXAM MISTAKES

1. The stomata are pores, while guard cells are the cells that control their opening.
2. Xylem transports water and mineral salts; phloem transports organic substances.
3. Palisade mesophyll is usually the main photosynthetic tissue.
4. Spongy mesophyll contains air spaces that support gaseous exchange.
5. Transpiration is the loss of water vapour, not the absorption of water by roots.
6. A potometer estimates water uptake, not transpiration directly.
7. Photosynthesis and cellular respiration can occur simultaneously during daylight.
8. Plants carry out cellular respiration during both day and night.
9. Closing stomata reduces water loss but also restricts carbon dioxide entry.
10. The compensation point does not mean photosynthesis and respiration stop.
11. Light intensity is not always the limiting factor for photosynthesis.
12. Glucose produced during photosynthesis can be used for respiration, storage and synthesis of other substances.

1. High humidity means the surrounding air contains more water vapour.
2. The difference in water vapour concentration between the leaf and the air decreases.
3. The diffusion gradient becomes smaller.
4. Water vapour diffuses out more slowly.
5. The rate of transpiration decreases.

## QUESTION 6

Differentiate between cohesion and adhesion.

**SUGGESTED ANSWER:**

1. Cohesion is the attraction between molecules of the same substance.
2. Adhesion is the attraction between molecules of different substances.

## QUESTION 7

Explain why leaves are suitable organs for photosynthesis.

**SUGGESTED ANSWER:**

1. Leaves have a broad surface area to absorb sunlight.
2. The upper epidermis is transparent.
3. Palisade mesophyll cells contain many chloroplasts.
4. Stomata allow carbon dioxide to enter.
5. Xylem transports water to the leaves.

## QUESTION 8

State three factors that affect the rate of photosynthesis.

**SUGGESTED ANSWER:**

1. Light intensity.
2. Carbon dioxide concentration.
3. Temperature.

Water availability is another important factor.

## QUESTION 9

Explain why increasing carbon dioxide concentration may not always increase the rate of photosynthesis.

**SUGGESTED ANSWER:**

1. Carbon dioxide may no longer be the limiting factor.
2. Another factor, such as light intensity or temperature, may be limiting.
3. Therefore, increasing carbon dioxide concentration does not significantly increase the rate.

## QUESTION 10

Define the compensation point.

**SUGGESTED ANSWER:**

The compensation point is the light intensity at which the rate of photosynthesis is equal to the rate of cellular respiration.

## QUESTION 11

Explain what happens when light intensity is above the compensation point.

**SUGGESTED ANSWER:**

1. The rate of photosynthesis is higher than the rate of respiration.
2. Carbon dioxide uptake exceeds carbon dioxide release.
3. More organic substances are produced than are used in respiration.
4. The plant has a net gain of organic material.

## QUESTION 12

A student uses a potometer to investigate water uptake by a leafy shoot.

Explain why a potometer does not measure the exact rate of transpiration.

**SUGGESTED ANSWER:**

1. A potometer measures water uptake by the shoot.
2. Some absorbed water may be used in photosynthesis and other metabolic processes.
3. Some water may be retained in plant tissues.
4. Therefore, water uptake is only an indirect estimate of transpiration.
