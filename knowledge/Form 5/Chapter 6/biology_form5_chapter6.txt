# 6.1 STRUCTURE OF A FLOWER

A flower is the reproductive structure of an angiosperm, or flowering plant.

The main functions of a flower are to:

1. Produce male and female gametes.
2. Attract pollinating agents.
3. Allow pollination to occur.
4. Provide a site for fertilisation.
5. Develop into fruits and seeds after fertilisation.

A typical flower contains four main floral parts:

1. Sepals
2. Petals
3. Stamens
4. Carpels

---

## 6.1.1 SEPALS

Sepals are usually green, leaf-like structures surrounding the developing flower bud.

A group of sepals is called the calyx.

Functions:

- Protect the developing flower bud.
- Carry out limited photosynthesis when green.
- Support the flower before it opens.

---

## 6.1.2 PETALS

Petals are usually brightly coloured structures surrounding the reproductive organs.

A group of petals is called the corolla.

Functions:

- Attract pollinating agents such as insects and birds.
- Provide visual signals that help pollinators locate flowers.
- In some flowers, produce or help display nectar guides.

Some flowers have small or inconspicuous petals because they are mainly pollinated by wind.

---

# 6.1.3 STAMEN

The stamen is the male reproductive structure of a flower.

A stamen consists of:

1. Anther
2. Filament

### A. ANTHER

The anther is the structure where pollen grains develop.

It contains pollen sacs, also called microsporangia.

Pollen grains contain the male gametophyte of the flowering plant.

### B. FILAMENT

The filament is a stalk that supports the anther.

Its position helps expose the anther so pollen can be released and transferred.

---

# 6.1.4 CARPEL

The carpel is the female reproductive structure of a flower.

A carpel consists of:

1. Stigma
2. Style
3. Ovary

Several carpels may be fused together to form a compound pistil.

### A. STIGMA

The stigma is the upper part of the carpel.

Functions:

- Receives pollen grains.
- Provides a surface where compatible pollen can germinate.
- May have a sticky surface that helps pollen grains adhere.

### B. STYLE

The style is the stalk connecting the stigma to the ovary.

Functions:

- Provides a pathway for the pollen tube.
- Connects the stigma with the ovary.

### C. OVARY

The ovary is the enlarged basal part of the carpel.

It contains one or more ovules.

After fertilisation:

- Ovules develop into seeds.
- The ovary develops into a fruit or part of a fruit.

---

# 6.1.5 OVULE

An ovule is a structure inside the ovary that contains the female gametophyte.

The embryo sac develops inside the ovule.

After fertilisation, the ovule develops into a seed.

---

# 6.1.6 NECTAR

Nectar is a sugary liquid produced by specialised structures called nectaries in many flowers.

Functions:

- Provides a food reward for pollinators.
- Encourages animals to visit flowers.
- Increases the likelihood of pollen transfer.

---

# 6.1.7 COMPLETE FLOWER STRUCTURE

A simplified structure can be remembered as:

**Male reproductive structure:**

Anther → Filament → Stamen

**Female reproductive structure:**

Stigma → Style → Ovary → Ovule → Carpel

---

# 6.1.8 COMPLETE AND INCOMPLETE FLOWERS

A complete flower contains all four major floral parts:

- Sepals
- Petals
- Stamens
- Carpels

An incomplete flower lacks one or more of these parts.

---

# 6.1.9 BISEXUAL AND UNISEXUAL FLOWERS

### BISEXUAL FLOWER

A bisexual flower contains both:

- Stamens
- Carpels

Example:

Hibiscus flowers are commonly used as an example of bisexual flowers.

### UNISEXUAL FLOWER

A unisexual flower contains either:

- Stamens only, or
- Carpels only.

Examples include male and female flowers in maize.

---

# 6.1.10 WIND-POLLINATED AND ANIMAL-POLLINATED FLOWERS

Different flower structures are adapted to different pollinating agents.

| Feature | Wind-pollinated | Animal-pollinated |
|---|---|---|
| Petals | Usually small or inconspicuous | Usually conspicuous |
| Scent | Usually absent | Often present |
| Nectar | Usually absent | Often present |
| Pollen | Small, light and produced abundantly | Often larger/heavier and produced in smaller quantities |
| Anthers | Often exposed | Usually positioned to contact pollinator |
| Stigma | Often large and feathery | Usually sticky |
| Main pollinating agent | Wind | Animals |

These are general patterns rather than rules applying to every species.

---

# 6.2 DEVELOPMENT OF POLLEN GRAINS AND EMBRYO SAC

Sexual reproduction in flowering plants involves the production of male and female reproductive structures.

The male reproductive structure develops pollen grains.

The female reproductive structure develops an embryo sac containing the egg cell.

---

# 6.2.1 DEVELOPMENT OF POLLEN GRAINS

Pollen grains develop inside the anthers.

The process is called **microsporogenesis** when microspore mother cells undergo meiosis to produce haploid microspores.

### STEP 1: MICROSPOROCYTE

A diploid microspore mother cell, also called a microsporocyte, is formed in the pollen sac.

It has:

**2n chromosomes**

### STEP 2: MEIOSIS

The microspore mother cell undergoes meiosis.

This produces four genetically different haploid microspores.

Each microspore contains:

**n chromosomes**

### STEP 3: POLLEN GRAIN DEVELOPMENT

Each microspore develops into a pollen grain.

A mature pollen grain contains the male gametophyte.

In many flowering plants, the pollen grain contains:

- A vegetative cell.
- A generative cell.

The generative cell later divides by mitosis to produce two male gametes.

---

# 6.2.2 STRUCTURE OF A POLLEN GRAIN

A pollen grain usually has:

### EXINE

The outer wall of the pollen grain.

It provides protection and is often made of a highly resistant substance called sporopollenin.

### INTINE

The inner wall of the pollen grain.

### VEGETATIVE CELL

The vegetative cell forms the pollen tube after pollen germination.

### GENERATIVE CELL

The generative cell divides to produce two male gametes.

---

# 6.2.3 DEVELOPMENT OF THE EMBRYO SAC

The female gametophyte of a flowering plant is called the embryo sac.

It develops inside the ovule.

The process begins with a diploid megaspore mother cell.

This cell is also called a megasporocyte.

---

## STEP 1: MEIOSIS

The diploid megaspore mother cell undergoes meiosis.

This produces four haploid megaspores.

Usually:

- Three megaspores degenerate.
- One functional megaspore remains.

---

## STEP 2: MITOTIC DIVISIONS

The functional megaspore undergoes repeated mitotic divisions.

This produces nuclei that organise into the embryo sac.

---

## STEP 3: MATURE EMBRYO SAC

A typical mature embryo sac contains:

- One egg cell.
- Two synergids.
- Three antipodal cells.
- Two polar nuclei in the central cell.

This gives a total of seven cells containing eight nuclei in the typical Polygonum-type embryo sac.

The egg cell is the female gamete.

---

# 6.2.4 IMPORTANT COMPARISON

| Pollen development | Embryo sac development |
|---|---|
| Occurs in anther | Occurs in ovule |
| Begins with microspore mother cell | Begins with megaspore mother cell |
| Meiosis produces microspores | Meiosis produces megaspores |
| Usually four microspores develop | Usually one functional megaspore remains |
| Produces male gametophyte | Produces female gametophyte |

---

# 6.3 POLLINATION AND FERTILISATION

## 6.3.1 POLLINATION

Pollination is the transfer of pollen grains from the anther to a stigma of a flower of a compatible plant.

Pollination is **not** the same as fertilisation.

Pollination happens before fertilisation.

---

# 6.3.2 TYPES OF POLLINATION

There are two major types:

1. Self-pollination
2. Cross-pollination

---

## A. SELF-POLLINATION

Self-pollination occurs when pollen is transferred to the stigma of:

- The same flower, or
- Another flower on the same plant.

Advantages:

- Does not require another plant of the same species nearby.
- Can allow reproduction when pollinators are limited.
- Helps ensure reproductive success.

Disadvantages:

- Produces less genetic variation.
- May increase the expression of harmful recessive alleles in some populations.
- Offspring are genetically more similar to the parent.

---

## B. CROSS-POLLINATION

Cross-pollination occurs when pollen is transferred from a flower on one plant to the stigma of a flower on another plant of the same species.

Advantages:

- Increases genetic variation.
- Can produce offspring with new combinations of alleles.
- May improve the ability of populations to adapt to environmental changes.

Disadvantages:

- Requires suitable pollinating agents or mechanisms.
- Depends on another compatible plant being available.
- Pollen may be wasted.

---

# 6.3.3 POLLINATING AGENTS

Pollinating agents include:

- Wind
- Insects
- Birds
- Other animals

---

# 6.3.4 POLLEN GERMINATION

When a compatible pollen grain lands on a suitable stigma, it may germinate.

The pollen grain forms a pollen tube.

The pollen tube grows through:

**Stigma → Style → Ovary → Ovule**

The pollen tube carries the male gametes towards the embryo sac.

---

# 6.3.5 ROLE OF THE POLLEN TUBE

The pollen tube:

1. Grows from the pollen grain.
2. Penetrates the stigma.
3. Grows through the style.
4. Enters the ovary.
5. Grows towards an ovule.
6. Enters the embryo sac.
7. Delivers two male gametes.

The growth of the pollen tube is guided by chemical signals from tissues around the ovule.

---

# 6.3.6 FERTILISATION

Fertilisation is the fusion of male and female gametes to form a zygote.

In flowering plants, a special process called **double fertilisation** occurs.

---

# 6.3.7 DOUBLE FERTILISATION

Two fertilisation events occur.

### FIRST FUSION

One male gamete fuses with the egg cell.

This produces a:

**Diploid zygote**

The zygote develops into the embryo.

### SECOND FUSION

The second male gamete fuses with the two polar nuclei in the central cell.

This produces the:

**Triploid primary endosperm nucleus**

The endosperm develops and provides nutrients to the developing embryo.

---

# 6.3.8 WHY IS IT CALLED DOUBLE FERTILISATION?

It is called double fertilisation because:

1. One male gamete fuses with the egg cell.
2. Another male gamete fuses with the polar nuclei.

Both events occur during the same reproductive process.

---

# 6.3.9 PLOIDY DURING DOUBLE FERTILISATION

Remember:

- Male gamete = n
- Egg cell = n
- Zygote = 2n
- Polar nucleus = n each
- Two polar nuclei = 2n total
- Second male gamete = n
- Endosperm nucleus = 3n

Memory pattern:

**n + n → 2n**

**n + n + n → 3n**

---

# 6.3.10 SELF-INCOMPATIBILITY

Some flowering plants have mechanisms that prevent self-fertilisation.

Self-incompatibility allows a plant to recognise genetically similar or incompatible pollen and prevent successful fertilisation.

This promotes cross-pollination and increases genetic variation.

---

# 6.4 DEVELOPMENT OF SEEDS AND FRUITS

After fertilisation, major changes occur inside the flower.

The fertilised ovule develops into a seed.

The ovary develops into a fruit.

---

# 6.4.1 DEVELOPMENT OF THE ZYGOTE

The zygote undergoes mitotic divisions.

It develops into an embryo.

The embryo eventually develops structures such as:

- Radicle
- Plumule
- Cotyledons

### RADICLE

The radicle develops into the embryonic root.

### PLUMULE

The plumule develops into the embryonic shoot.

### COTYLEDONS

Cotyledons are embryonic leaves.

Depending on the plant, they may:

- Store food.
- Absorb nutrients from endosperm.
- Become the first seed leaves.

---

# 6.4.2 DEVELOPMENT OF THE ENDOSPERM

The primary endosperm nucleus develops into endosperm.

The endosperm provides nutrients to the developing embryo in many flowering plants.

It may remain in the mature seed or be largely absorbed by the developing embryo.

---

# 6.4.3 DEVELOPMENT OF SEED COAT

The integuments of the ovule develop into the seed coat.

The seed coat protects the embryo from:

- Physical damage.
- Pathogens.
- Excessive water loss.

The seed coat may become hard or tough.

---

# 6.4.4 OVULE → SEED

A key relationship to memorise:

**Ovule → Seed**

The seed contains:

- Embryo.
- Food reserve or nutritive tissue.
- Protective seed coat.

---

# 6.4.5 OVARY → FRUIT

Another key relationship:

**Ovary → Fruit**

The ovary wall develops into the pericarp.

The fruit protects the seeds and can help with seed dispersal.

---

# 6.4.6 FRUIT FUNCTIONS

Fruits can:

1. Protect developing seeds.
2. Assist seed dispersal.
3. Attract animals that may disperse seeds.
4. Help prevent seeds from being deposited too close to the parent plant.

---

# 6.4.7 SIMPLE SEED DEVELOPMENT SEQUENCE

A useful sequence is:

**Pollination**

↓

**Pollen germination**

↓

**Pollen tube growth**

↓

**Double fertilisation**

↓

**Zygote formation**

↓

**Embryo development**

↓

**Seed development**

↓

**Fruit development**

---

# 6.4.8 CHANGES AFTER FERTILISATION

| Flower structure | Fate after fertilisation |
|---|---|
| Ovule | Seed |
| Ovary | Fruit |
| Ovary wall | Pericarp |
| Zygote | Embryo |
| Integuments | Seed coat |
| Primary endosperm nucleus | Endosperm |
| Petals | Usually wither and fall |
| Stamens | Usually wither and fall |

Not every flower follows exactly the same visible pattern, but these are the major relationships.

---

# 6.4.9 PARTHENOCARPY

Parthenocarpy is the development of fruit without fertilisation.

Such fruits are often seedless or contain very few developed seeds.

Parthenocarpic fruit production can be useful in agriculture because consumers may prefer seedless fruits.

Plant hormones can be involved in inducing fruit development without normal fertilisation.

---

# 6.5 IMPORTANCE OF SEEDS FOR SURVIVAL

Seeds are important reproductive structures that help flowering plants survive and spread.

---

# 6.5.1 SEEDS PROTECT THE EMBRYO

The seed coat protects the developing embryo from environmental conditions.

Protection may include:

- Physical damage.
- Pathogens.
- Desiccation.
- Unfavourable environmental conditions.

---

# 6.5.2 SEEDS CONTAIN FOOD RESERVES

Seeds contain stored nutrients that support the embryo during germination.

Food reserves may be stored in:

- Endosperm.
- Cotyledons.

Common stored substances include:

- Starch.
- Lipids.
- Proteins.

These reserves provide energy and materials for growth before the young plant becomes fully photosynthetic.

---

# 6.5.3 SEED DORMANCY

Seed dormancy is a period during which a viable seed does not germinate even when some environmental conditions appear suitable.

Dormancy can prevent seeds from germinating at an unsuitable time.

Importance:

1. Helps seeds survive unfavourable conditions.
2. Prevents all seeds from germinating simultaneously.
3. Allows germination to occur when conditions are more favourable.

---

# 6.5.4 SEED DISPERSAL

Seed dispersal is the movement of seeds away from the parent plant.

Dispersal agents include:

- Wind.
- Water.
- Animals.
- Explosive mechanisms.

### IMPORTANCE OF SEED DISPERSAL

Seed dispersal:

1. Reduces competition between parent and offspring plants.
2. Reduces competition among seedlings.
3. Allows plants to colonise new areas.
4. Helps spread the species.
5. Reduces the concentration of offspring in one location.

---

# 6.5.5 ADAPTATIONS FOR SEED DISPERSAL

Different fruits and seeds have different adaptations.

### WIND DISPERSAL

Seeds may have:

- Wings.
- Hairs.
- Lightweight structures.

These increase their ability to be carried by air currents.

### WATER DISPERSAL

Some seeds or fruits have:

- Air spaces.
- Fibrous tissues.
- Waterproof outer layers.

These allow them to float.

### ANIMAL DISPERSAL

Seeds may be dispersed when:

- Animals eat fleshy fruits and later deposit seeds elsewhere.
- Seeds attach to animal fur.
- Animals carry or store seeds.

### EXPLOSIVE DISPERSAL

Some fruits build up mechanical tension and suddenly split open.

The seeds are projected away from the parent plant.

---

# 6.5.6 GENETIC VARIATION

Sexual reproduction produces genetic variation because:

1. Meiosis produces genetically different gametes.
2. Crossing over and independent assortment occur during meiosis.
3. Random fertilisation combines genetic material from two gametes.
4. Cross-pollination can bring together genetic material from different plants.

Genetic variation is important because it increases the chance that some individuals can survive environmental changes.

---

# 6.5.7 SEEDS AND UNFAVOURABLE CONDITIONS

Seeds can survive periods when conditions are unsuitable for plant growth.

Examples:

- Drought.
- Low temperature.
- Lack of suitable water.
- Seasonal changes.

Seeds may remain dormant until suitable conditions return.

---

# 6.5.8 IMPORTANCE OF SEEDS FOR PLANT SURVIVAL

Seeds help plants:

- Reproduce sexually.
- Protect embryos.
- Store food.
- Survive unfavourable conditions.
- Disperse to new habitats.
- Generate genetically varied offspring.
- Continue the species.

---

# IMPORTANT COMPARISONS

## SELF-POLLINATION VS CROSS-POLLINATION

| Self-pollination | Cross-pollination |
|---|---|
| Pollen transferred within the same flower or same plant | Pollen transferred between different plants of the same species |
| Usually less genetic variation | Greater genetic variation |
| Less dependent on another plant | Requires another compatible plant |
| Can occur when pollinators are limited | Often requires effective pollen transfer |
| Greater genetic similarity among offspring | Greater genetic diversity |

---

## POLLINATION VS FERTILISATION

| Pollination | Fertilisation |
|---|---|
| Transfer of pollen to stigma | Fusion of gametes |
| Occurs before fertilisation | Occurs after pollen tube growth |
| Does not produce a zygote | Produces a zygote |
| Involves pollen transfer | Involves male and female gametes |

---

## POLLEN GRAIN VS EMBRYO SAC

| Pollen grain | Embryo sac |
|---|---|
| Male gametophyte | Female gametophyte |
| Develops in anther | Develops in ovule |
| Produces/carries male gametes | Contains egg cell |
| Involved in pollen tube formation | Site of fertilisation |

---

## OVULE VS OVARY

| Ovule | Ovary |
|---|---|
| Located inside ovary | Part of carpel |
| Contains embryo sac | Contains one or more ovules |
| Develops into seed | Develops into fruit |

---

# COMMON EXAM TRAPS

## 1. Pollination is NOT fertilisation

Do not write that pollination is the fusion of gametes.

Correct:

**Pollination = transfer of pollen to stigma.**

**Fertilisation = fusion of male and female gametes.**

---

## 2. Ovule becomes seed

Remember:

**OVULE → SEED**

Not:

**Ovule → fruit**

---

## 3. Ovary becomes fruit

Remember:

**OVARY → FRUIT**

---

## 4. Double fertilisation involves two fusion events

One male gamete:

**Egg → zygote**

Second male gamete:

**Polar nuclei → endosperm nucleus**

---

## 5. Endosperm is not the embryo

The embryo develops from the zygote.

The endosperm develops from the primary endosperm nucleus and mainly provides nutrition for the developing embryo.

---

## 6. Pollen grain is not the male gamete itself

The pollen grain contains the male gametophyte and gives rise to or carries the male gametes.

---

## 7. Cross-pollination is not the same as cross-fertilisation

Cross-pollination describes pollen transfer between different plants of the same species.

Fertilisation is the actual fusion of gametes.

---

# SPM-STYLE PRACTICE QUESTIONS

## QUESTION 1

State two functions of petals.

### SUGGESTED ANSWER

1. Attract pollinating agents.
2. Help facilitate pollen transfer by providing visual signals or structures associated with pollination.

---

## QUESTION 2

Differentiate between the stamen and carpel.

### SUGGESTED ANSWER

1. Stamen is the male reproductive structure, whereas carpel is the female reproductive structure.
2. Stamen consists of anther and filament, whereas carpel consists of stigma, style and ovary.

---

## QUESTION 3

Explain the development of pollen grains from a microspore mother cell.

### SUGGESTED ANSWER

1. A diploid microspore mother cell undergoes meiosis.
2. Four haploid microspores are produced.
3. The microspores develop into pollen grains.
4. The generative cell later divides by mitosis to form two male gametes.

---

## QUESTION 4

Explain how an embryo sac develops.

### SUGGESTED ANSWER

1. A diploid megaspore mother cell undergoes meiosis.
2. Four haploid megaspores are produced.
3. Usually three megaspores degenerate.
4. One functional megaspore remains.
5. The functional megaspore undergoes mitotic divisions.
6. A mature embryo sac develops containing the egg cell and other cells/nuclei.

---

## QUESTION 5

Differentiate between self-pollination and cross-pollination.

### SUGGESTED ANSWER

1. Self-pollination involves transfer of pollen within the same flower or plant, whereas cross-pollination involves transfer between different plants of the same species.
2. Self-pollination generally produces less genetic variation, whereas cross-pollination generally produces greater genetic variation.

---

## QUESTION 6

Explain the pathway of a pollen tube after pollen lands on a compatible stigma.

### SUGGESTED ANSWER

1. The pollen grain germinates on the stigma.
2. A pollen tube grows through the stigma.
3. The pollen tube grows down the style.
4. It enters the ovary.
5. It grows towards an ovule and enters the embryo sac.
6. The male gametes are released for fertilisation.

---

## QUESTION 7

Explain double fertilisation in flowering plants.

### SUGGESTED ANSWER

1. The pollen tube delivers two male gametes into the embryo sac.
2. One male gamete fuses with the egg cell.
3. A diploid zygote is formed.
4. The second male gamete fuses with the two polar nuclei.
5. A triploid primary endosperm nucleus is formed.

---

## QUESTION 8

State the structures that develop into the following after fertilisation:

a) Ovule  
b) Ovary  
c) Zygote  
d) Integuments

### SUGGESTED ANSWER

a) Seed  
b) Fruit  
c) Embryo  
d) Seed coat

---

## QUESTION 9

Explain why seeds are important for the survival of flowering plants.

### SUGGESTED ANSWER

1. The seed protects the embryo.
2. It contains food reserves for the developing embryo.
3. Dormancy allows seeds to survive unfavourable conditions.
4. Seed dispersal reduces competition.
5. Seeds allow plants to colonise new habitats.
6. Sexual reproduction produces genetic variation that can improve adaptation.

---

## QUESTION 10

A student observes that pollen grains from one plant are transferred to the stigma of another plant of the same species.

Identify the type of pollination and explain one advantage.

### SUGGESTED ANSWER

The type of pollination is cross-pollination.

An advantage is that it increases genetic variation in the offspring.

---

## QUESTION 11

A flower is brightly coloured, produces nectar and has a strong scent.

Suggest the likely pollinating agent and explain your answer.

### SUGGESTED ANSWER

The likely pollinating agent is an animal such as an insect.

The bright colour and scent attract animals, while nectar provides a food reward that encourages visits to the flower and increases the chance of pollen transfer.

---

## QUESTION 12

Explain how seed dispersal benefits a plant population.

### SUGGESTED ANSWER

1. Seeds are moved away from the parent plant.
2. Competition for light, water, minerals and space is reduced.
3. Competition between seedlings is reduced.
4. Seeds can reach new habitats.
5. The species can spread over a larger area.

---

## QUESTION 13

Compare pollen grain development and embryo sac development.

### SUGGESTED ANSWER

1. Pollen grains develop in the anther, whereas the embryo sac develops in the ovule.
2. Pollen development begins with a microspore mother cell, whereas embryo sac development begins with a megaspore mother cell.
3. Both processes involve meiosis followed by further development of haploid structures.
4. Pollen grains form the male gametophyte, whereas the embryo sac forms the female gametophyte.

---

## QUESTION 14

Explain how sexual reproduction contributes to genetic variation in flowering plants.

### SUGGESTED ANSWER

1. Meiosis produces genetically different haploid cells.
2. Crossing over can exchange genetic material between homologous chromosomes.
3. Independent assortment produces different combinations of chromosomes.
4. Cross-pollination can bring pollen from genetically different plants.
5. Random fusion of gametes produces new combinations of alleles.

---

## QUESTION 15

A farmer wants to produce seedless fruits.

Suggest a reproductive process or agricultural approach that could be used and explain the principle involved.

### SUGGESTED ANSWER

Parthenocarpy can be used.

Parthenocarpy is the development of fruit without fertilisation. Plant hormones can be used in some crops to stimulate fruit development without normal seed formation, producing seedless or nearly seedless fruits.

# FINAL REVISION SUMMARY

## 6.1 STRUCTURE OF A FLOWER

- Sepals protect the developing flower.
- Petals attract pollinating agents.
- Stamens are male reproductive structures.
- Anther produces pollen grains.
- Filament supports the anther.
- Carpel is the female reproductive structure.
- Stigma receives pollen.
- Style provides a pathway for the pollen tube.
- Ovary contains ovules.

## 6.2 DEVELOPMENT OF POLLEN GRAINS AND EMBRYO SAC

- Microspore mother cells undergo meiosis to produce haploid microspores.
- Microspores develop into pollen grains.
- Megaspore mother cells undergo meiosis to produce haploid megaspores.
- Usually one functional megaspore develops into the embryo sac.
- The embryo sac contains the egg cell and other cells/nuclei.

## 6.3 POLLINATION AND FERTILISATION

- Pollination is pollen transfer to a stigma.
- Self-pollination occurs within the same plant.
- Cross-pollination occurs between different plants of the same species.
- Pollen germinates and forms a pollen tube.
- The pollen tube delivers two male gametes.
- Double fertilisation produces a diploid zygote and a triploid endosperm nucleus.

## 6.4 DEVELOPMENT OF SEEDS AND FRUITS

- Zygote develops into embryo.
- Ovule develops into seed.
- Integuments develop into seed coat.
- Primary endosperm nucleus develops into endosperm.
- Ovary develops into fruit.
- Ovary wall develops into pericarp.

## 6.5 IMPORTANCE OF SEEDS

- Protect the embryo.
- Store food.
- Allow dormancy.
- Survive unfavourable conditions.
- Allow dispersal.
- Reduce competition.
- Help plants colonise new habitats.
- Support sexual reproduction and genetic variation.
