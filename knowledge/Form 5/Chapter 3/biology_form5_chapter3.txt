# 3.1 MAIN INORGANIC NUTRIENTS

## 3.1.1 INTRODUCTION TO PLANT NUTRITION

Plant nutrition is the process by which plants obtain and use nutrients required for growth, development and normal cellular activities.

Green plants are generally autotrophs because they can produce organic substances through photosynthesis.

However, plants also require inorganic nutrients from their environment.

The main inorganic nutrients required by plants include:

1. Water.
2. Mineral salts.
3. Carbon dioxide.

Water and mineral ions are mainly absorbed from the soil through the roots, while carbon dioxide enters leaves through stomata.

Mineral nutrients are essential for:

- Formation of proteins and nucleic acids.
- Chlorophyll production.
- Enzyme activity.
- Cell division and growth.
- Development of roots, stems and leaves.
- Maintenance of normal cellular functions.

## 3.1.2 MACRONUTRIENTS AND MICRONUTRIENTS

Mineral nutrients can be classified into two groups according to the quantity required by plants.

**A. MACRONUTRIENTS**

Macronutrients are mineral elements required by plants in relatively large quantities.

Examples include:

- Nitrogen (N).
- Phosphorus (P).
- Potassium (K).
- Calcium (Ca).
- Magnesium (Mg).
- Sulphur (S).

**B. MICRONUTRIENTS**

Micronutrients are mineral elements required by plants in very small quantities.

Examples include:

- Iron (Fe).
- Manganese (Mn).
- Zinc (Zn).
- Copper (Cu).
- Boron (B).
- Molybdenum (Mo).
- Chlorine (Cl).
- Nickel (Ni).

IMPORTANT:

Micronutrients are required in small quantities, but they are still essential. A deficiency can affect normal plant growth and development.

## 3.1.3 FUNCTIONS OF IMPORTANT MACRONUTRIENTS

**A. NITROGEN (N)**

Plants mainly absorb nitrogen in the form of nitrate ions (NO₃⁻) and ammonium ions (NH₄⁺).

Functions:

1. Required for the synthesis of amino acids.
2. Needed for protein formation.
3. Required for the formation of nucleic acids such as DNA and RNA.
4. Important for chlorophyll production.
5. Supports vegetative growth, including the development of leaves and stems.

**Symptoms of nitrogen deficiency:**

- Older leaves become yellow.
- Plant growth becomes stunted.
- Leaves may become smaller.
- Overall plant development is reduced.

Explanation:

Nitrogen is needed to produce proteins and chlorophyll. When nitrogen is insufficient, chlorophyll production and normal growth are affected.

**B. PHOSPHORUS (P)**

Plants mainly absorb phosphorus in the form of phosphate ions.

Functions:

1. Required for the formation of ATP.
2. Forms part of nucleic acids such as DNA and RNA.
3. Forms part of phospholipids in cell membranes.
4. Supports root development.
5. Important for energy transfer and metabolic reactions.

**Symptoms of phosphorus deficiency:**

- Poor root development.
- Stunted growth.
- Older leaves may develop a dark green or purplish colour.
- Delayed flowering and development may occur.

Explanation:

Phosphorus is important for ATP formation and energy transfer. A shortage can affect growth and the development of roots and reproductive structures.

**C. POTASSIUM (K)**

Plants absorb potassium in the form of potassium ions (K⁺).

Functions:

1. Activates many enzymes.
2. Helps regulate osmotic balance in cells.
3. Important for stomatal opening and closing.
4. Supports photosynthesis and other metabolic activities.
5. Helps maintain normal plant growth.

**Symptoms of potassium deficiency:**

- Yellowing at the edges of older leaves.
- Brown or scorched-looking leaf margins.
- Weak stems.
- Poor growth.

Explanation:

Potassium is important for enzyme activation and water regulation. A deficiency affects normal metabolic activities and may cause damage to leaf margins.

**D. CALCIUM (Ca)**

Plants absorb calcium in the form of calcium ions (Ca²⁺).

Functions:

1. Important for cell wall formation.
2. Helps stabilise cell membranes.
3. Involved in cell signalling.
4. Supports the development of growing tissues.
5. Important for normal root and shoot development.

**Symptoms of calcium deficiency:**

- Damage to growing root tips.
- Deformed young leaves.
- Poor development of shoot tips.
- Growing regions may become damaged or die.

Explanation:

Calcium is important for the development and stability of cell structures. Young tissues are particularly affected when calcium is insufficient.

**E. MAGNESIUM (Mg)**

Plants absorb magnesium in the form of magnesium ions (Mg²⁺).

Functions:

1. Forms the central atom of the chlorophyll molecule.
2. Required for chlorophyll formation and function.
3. Activates several enzymes.
4. Supports photosynthesis.
5. Involved in phosphate metabolism.

**Symptoms of magnesium deficiency:**

- Yellowing between the veins of older leaves.
- Leaf veins may remain green while surrounding tissues become yellow.
- Severe deficiency may cause leaf damage.

Explanation:

Magnesium is part of chlorophyll. A shortage affects chlorophyll function and reduces the plant's ability to photosynthesise.

**F. SULPHUR (S)**

Plants mainly absorb sulphur in the form of sulphate ions (SO₄²⁻).

Functions:

1. Required for the synthesis of certain amino acids.
2. Needed for protein formation.
3. Forms part of some vitamins and coenzymes.
4. Supports normal metabolic activities.

**Symptoms of sulphur deficiency:**

- Yellowing of young leaves.
- Reduced growth.
- Smaller leaves.
- Poor protein synthesis.

Explanation:

Sulphur is required to produce certain amino acids. A shortage affects protein formation and normal growth.

## 3.1.4 FUNCTIONS OF IMPORTANT MICRONUTRIENTS

**A. IRON (Fe)**

Functions:

1. Required for chlorophyll synthesis.
2. Involved in electron transport during photosynthesis and respiration.
3. Supports enzyme activity.

Deficiency symptoms:

- Interveinal chlorosis, especially in young leaves.
- Leaves become yellow while veins may remain green.

**B. MANGANESE (Mn)**

Functions:

1. Involved in enzyme activation.
2. Required for normal photosynthetic activity.
3. Supports the light-dependent reactions of photosynthesis.

Deficiency symptoms:

- Interveinal chlorosis.
- Small brown spots may develop on leaves.

**C. ZINC (Zn)**

Functions:

1. Required for the activity of several enzymes.
2. Involved in the production and regulation of plant growth hormones.
3. Supports normal growth and development.

Deficiency symptoms:

- Shortened internodes.
- Small leaves.
- Stunted growth.

**D. COPPER (Cu)**

Functions:

1. Required for the activity of certain enzymes.
2. Involved in electron transport.
3. Supports normal plant metabolism.

Deficiency symptoms:

- Young leaves may become distorted.
- Shoot tips may become damaged.
- Growth may be reduced.

**E. BORON (B)**

Functions:

1. Important for cell wall development.
2. Supports cell division and growth.
3. Important for pollen development and reproductive processes.
4. Supports the development of growing tissues.

Deficiency symptoms:

- Death or damage of growing points.
- Deformed young leaves.
- Poor root development.
- Reproductive development may be affected.

**F. MOLYBDENUM (Mo)**

Functions:

1. Required by enzymes involved in nitrogen metabolism.
2. Important for nitrate reduction.
3. Supports nitrogen utilisation.

Deficiency symptoms:

- Reduced growth.
- Yellowing of leaves.
- Symptoms may resemble nitrogen deficiency.

## 3.1.5 COMPARISON OF IMPORTANT MINERAL NUTRIENTS

| Mineral nutrient | Main function | Common deficiency symptoms |
|---|---|---|
| Nitrogen | Amino acids, proteins and chlorophyll | Yellowing of older leaves, stunted growth |
| Phosphorus | ATP, nucleic acids and root development | Poor root growth, purplish older leaves |
| Potassium | Enzyme activation and stomatal regulation | Yellow or scorched leaf margins |
| Calcium | Cell walls, membranes and growing tissues | Damaged root tips and young leaves |
| Magnesium | Component of chlorophyll | Interveinal chlorosis in older leaves |
| Sulphur | Certain amino acids and proteins | Yellowing of young leaves |
| Iron | Chlorophyll synthesis and electron transport | Interveinal chlorosis in young leaves |
| Zinc | Enzyme activity and growth regulation | Small leaves and shortened internodes |
| Boron | Cell wall development and growing tissues | Damaged growing points |

IMPORTANT:

Deficiency symptoms can overlap. The location and pattern of yellowing may help identify the nutrient involved, but symptoms alone are not always enough for a definite diagnosis.

## 3.1.6 WHY ARE MINERAL NUTRIENTS IMPORTANT?

Mineral nutrients are required because plants cannot complete normal growth and development without them.

For example:

- Without nitrogen, protein synthesis is affected.
- Without magnesium, chlorophyll function is affected.
- Without phosphorus, ATP formation and energy transfer are affected.
- Without calcium, developing cell walls and growing tissues are affected.
- Without potassium, enzyme activity and stomatal regulation may be disrupted.

## 3.1.7 EXAM TIPS

**Question: Explain why nitrogen is important for plant growth.**

Suggested answer:

1. Nitrogen is required for amino acid synthesis.
2. Amino acids are used to form proteins.
3. Proteins are needed for the formation of enzymes and cellular structures.
4. Therefore, nitrogen supports normal growth and development.

**Question: Explain why magnesium deficiency affects photosynthesis.**

Suggested answer:

1. Magnesium forms the central atom of chlorophyll.
2. Chlorophyll absorbs light energy.
3. Insufficient magnesium affects chlorophyll production or function.
4. The rate of photosynthesis may decrease.

**Question: Differentiate between macronutrients and micronutrients.**

Suggested answer:

Macronutrients are mineral elements required by plants in relatively large quantities, whereas micronutrients are mineral elements required in very small quantities.

# 3.2 ORGAN FOR WATER AND MINERAL SALT UPTAKE

## 3.2.1 ROOTS AS THE MAIN ORGAN FOR WATER AND MINERAL SALT UPTAKE

Roots are the main plant organs responsible for absorbing water and mineral ions from the soil.

The outer surface of young roots contains many root hair cells.

Root hairs increase the surface area available for absorption.

Water and mineral ions are absorbed from the soil solution and transported towards the xylem.

The xylem then transports water and mineral salts to the stems and leaves.

## 3.2.2 STRUCTURE OF A ROOT HAIR CELL

A root hair cell is a specialised epidermal cell found near the growing region of a root.

Characteristics:

1. Has a long, narrow projection called a root hair.
2. Has a thin cell wall.
3. Has a large surface area relative to its volume.
4. Contains a cell membrane.
5. Contains cytoplasm and a nucleus.
6. Has a large vacuole containing cell sap.
7. Contains many mitochondria to provide energy for active transport.

## 3.2.3 ADAPTATIONS OF ROOT HAIR CELLS

| Adaptation | Importance |
|---|---|
| Long root hair | Increases surface area for absorption |
| Thin cell wall | Shortens the diffusion pathway |
| Large vacuole | Helps maintain a low water potential in the cell |
| Many mitochondria | Supply ATP for active transport of mineral ions |
| Numerous root hairs | Increase total contact with soil particles |

These adaptations make root hair cells effective at absorbing water and mineral ions.

## 3.2.4 ABSORPTION OF WATER BY ROOT HAIR CELLS

Water enters root hair cells mainly by osmosis.

Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential through a selectively permeable membrane.

**MECHANISM OF WATER ABSORPTION:**

1. Soil water usually has a higher water potential than the cell sap inside the root hair cell.
2. Water moves from the soil solution towards the root hair cell.
3. Water passes through the selectively permeable cell membrane by osmosis.
4. Water enters the cytoplasm and vacuole.
5. The root hair cell becomes more turgid.

The movement of water depends on the water potential gradient between the soil and the root cells.

IMPORTANT:

If the soil becomes very dry or contains a high concentration of dissolved salts, its water potential may become too low for efficient water absorption.

## 3.2.5 ABSORPTION OF MINERAL IONS BY ROOT HAIR CELLS

Mineral ions are absorbed through the cell membrane by different transport mechanisms.

These include:

1. Diffusion.
2. Active transport.

**A. DIFFUSION**

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration.

When mineral ion concentration is higher in the soil solution than inside the root hair cell, some ions can enter by diffusion through suitable membrane transport proteins.

Diffusion does not require ATP.

**B. ACTIVE TRANSPORT**

Active transport is the movement of substances across a cell membrane against their concentration gradient using energy.

Mechanism:

1. Mineral ion concentration in the soil may be lower than inside the root hair cell.
2. Mineral ions need to move against their concentration gradient.
3. Specific transport proteins in the cell membrane help move the ions.
4. Energy from ATP is required.
5. Mineral ions accumulate inside the root hair cell.

Mitochondria provide ATP needed for active transport.

## 3.2.6 DIFFERENCES BETWEEN OSMOSIS, DIFFUSION AND ACTIVE TRANSPORT

| Process | Direction of movement | Energy required? |
|---|---|---|
| Osmosis | Water moves from higher water potential to lower water potential | No direct ATP required |
| Diffusion | Particles move down their concentration gradient | No |
| Active transport | Substances move against their concentration gradient | Yes, ATP required |

IMPORTANT:

Osmosis specifically refers to water movement through a selectively permeable membrane. It should not be used to describe the absorption of mineral ions.

## 3.2.7 MOVEMENT OF WATER THROUGH ROOT TISSUES

After entering root hair cells, water moves across the root towards the xylem.

There are two main pathways:

**A. APOPLAST PATHWAY**

The apoplast pathway is the movement of water through cell walls and intercellular spaces.

Characteristics:

- Water moves outside the plasma membranes.
- Water travels through cell walls and spaces between cells.
- It provides a pathway through root tissues.

**B. SYMPLAST PATHWAY**

The symplast pathway is the movement of water through the cytoplasm of cells connected by plasmodesmata.

Characteristics:

- Water enters the cytoplasm through the plasma membrane.
- It moves from one cell to another through plasmodesmata.
- It travels through the connected cytoplasm of root cells.

**THE ROLE OF THE ENDODERMIS**

The endodermis is a layer of cells surrounding the vascular cylinder of the root.

It contains a waterproof band called the Casparian strip.

Functions:

1. Blocks uncontrolled movement of water through the cell walls.
2. Forces water and dissolved mineral ions to pass through cell membranes at the endodermis.
3. Helps regulate which substances enter the vascular tissues.

After crossing the endodermis, water enters the vascular tissues and eventually reaches the xylem.

## 3.2.8 TRANSPORT OF WATER AND MINERAL SALTS TO THE LEAVES

Water and dissolved mineral ions enter the xylem in the roots.

They are transported upwards through the xylem vessels.

The upward movement of water is supported by:

1. Transpiration pull.
2. Cohesion between water molecules.
3. Adhesion between water molecules and xylem walls.
4. Root pressure, which may contribute under certain conditions.

Transpiration pull is generally the major force responsible for upward water transport in many terrestrial plants.

## 3.2.9 IMPORTANCE OF WATER ABSORPTION

Water is important because it:

1. Acts as a solvent for metabolic reactions.
2. Is a raw material for photosynthesis.
3. Maintains cell turgidity.
4. Supports plant growth.
5. Helps transport dissolved mineral ions.
6. Helps regulate plant temperature through transpiration.

## 3.2.10 IMPORTANCE OF MINERAL SALT ABSORPTION

Mineral ions are needed for:

1. Formation of proteins and nucleic acids.
2. Chlorophyll production.
3. Enzyme activation.
4. Cell division and growth.
5. Development of roots, stems and leaves.
6. Normal metabolic activities.

## 3.2.11 EXAM TIPS

**Question: Explain how root hair cells are adapted for water absorption.**

Suggested answer:

1. Root hair cells have long projections that increase surface area.
2. Their cell walls are thin, allowing a short pathway for water movement.
3. Their large vacuoles help maintain a water potential gradient.
4. Water enters through the selectively permeable membrane by osmosis.

**Question: Explain why root hair cells contain many mitochondria.**

Suggested answer:

1. Mineral ions may need to be absorbed against their concentration gradient.
2. Active transport is required.
3. Active transport requires ATP.
4. Mitochondria produce ATP through cellular respiration.

**Question: Explain how mineral ions can enter root hair cells when their concentration is lower in the soil than inside the cells.**

Suggested answer:

1. Mineral ions move against their concentration gradient.
2. Specific transport proteins in the membrane transport the ions.
3. Energy from ATP is required.
4. This process is called active transport.

# 3.3 DIVERSITY IN PLANT NUTRITION

## 3.3.1 INTRODUCTION TO DIVERSITY IN PLANT NUTRITION

Most green plants are autotrophs because they produce organic substances through photosynthesis.

However, not all plants obtain nutrients in exactly the same way.

Some plants have special adaptations that allow them to obtain nutrients from other organisms or from unusual environments.

Examples include:

1. Insectivorous plants.
2. Parasitic plants.
3. Mycoheterotrophic plants.
4. Epiphytic plants.

These adaptations help plants survive in environments where particular nutrients or resources may be limited.

## 3.3.2 AUTOTROPHIC PLANTS

Autotrophic plants produce organic substances from inorganic substances through photosynthesis.

They use:

- Carbon dioxide from the atmosphere.
- Water from the soil.
- Mineral ions from the soil.
- Light energy from the Sun.

Examples:

- Grass.
- Hibiscus.
- Mango trees.
- Most green flowering plants.

Autotrophic plants form the base of many food chains because they produce organic substances that support other organisms.

## 3.3.3 INSECTIVOROUS PLANTS

Insectivorous plants are plants that trap and digest insects or other small animals to obtain nutrients, especially nitrogen.

They usually grow in habitats where the soil contains insufficient available nitrogen.

IMPORTANT:

Insectivorous plants still carry out photosynthesis. They do not obtain all their food energy from insects.

The trapped animals mainly provide mineral nutrients.

**A. PITCHER PLANT**

The pitcher plant has modified leaves that form pitcher-shaped structures.

Adaptations:

1. Leaves are modified into pitcher-shaped traps.
2. The rim may produce nectar that attracts insects.
3. The inner surface may be slippery.
4. Insects fall into the pitcher.
5. Digestive fluids break down the trapped organisms.
6. Nutrients are absorbed by the pitcher tissues.

Functions:

- Obtains nitrogen and other mineral nutrients.
- Helps the plant survive in nutrient-poor soil.

**B. VENUS FLYTRAP**

The Venus flytrap has modified leaves with two lobes that can close rapidly.

Adaptations:

1. The leaf lobes form a trap.
2. Trigger hairs detect the presence of prey.
3. When suitable stimulation occurs, the lobes close.
4. The trapped organism is digested by enzymes.
5. Nutrients are absorbed by the leaf.

**C. SUNDEW**

Sundew plants have leaves covered with glandular hairs.

Adaptations:

1. Glandular hairs produce sticky secretions.
2. Small insects become trapped.
3. The leaf may bend around the prey.
4. Digestive enzymes break down the trapped organism.
5. Nutrients are absorbed.

## 3.3.4 PARASITIC PLANTS

Parasitic plants obtain some or all of their nutrients from another living plant called the host.

They attach to the host using specialised structures called haustoria.

Haustoria penetrate host tissues and establish connections with the host's transport tissues.

Parasitic plants can be divided into two main groups.

**A. HOLOPARASITES**

Holoparasites depend almost completely on their host for organic nutrients.

They generally contain little or no chlorophyll and cannot carry out sufficient photosynthesis to meet their nutritional requirements.

Example: *Rafflesia*.

Characteristics:

- Obtains organic nutrients from a host.
- Has greatly reduced vegetative structures.
- Depends on the host for nutrition.

**B. HEMIPARASITES**

Hemiparasites contain chlorophyll and can carry out photosynthesis.

However, they obtain water and mineral nutrients from their host through specialised connections.

Example: mistletoe.

Characteristics:

- Contains chlorophyll.
- Can photosynthesise.
- Obtains water and mineral salts from a host.
- Depends on another plant for some resources.

## 3.3.5 MYCOHETEROTROPHIC PLANTS

Mycoheterotrophic plants obtain organic nutrients through associations with fungi.

The fungi are connected to the roots of other plants, and nutrients can be transferred through the fungal network.

Some mycoheterotrophic plants have little or no chlorophyll and do not depend on photosynthesis for their organic carbon supply.

Characteristics:

1. Depend on fungal partners for organic nutrients.
2. Often grow in shaded forest environments.
3. May have reduced leaves and chlorophyll.
4. Obtain nutrients through fungal associations.

IMPORTANT:

Mycoheterotrophic plants should not be confused with ordinary parasitic plants that directly attach to the vascular tissues of another plant.

## 3.3.6 EPIPHYTIC PLANTS

Epiphytes are plants that grow on the surfaces of other plants, usually trees, for physical support.

They are not necessarily parasites.

They generally obtain water and mineral nutrients from rain, dust, decaying organic matter and the surrounding environment.

Example: many orchids and epiphytic ferns.

Adaptations:

1. Aerial roots may absorb water from the atmosphere.
2. Some roots have specialised outer tissues that absorb and retain water.
3. Leaves may have adaptations that reduce water loss.
4. Their position on tree branches allows access to sunlight.

IMPORTANT:

An epiphyte uses another plant as physical support but does not necessarily obtain nutrients directly from that host.

## 3.3.7 COMPARISON OF PLANT NUTRITION TYPES

| Plant type | Main source of organic nutrients | Relationship with other organisms |
|---|---|---|
| Autotrophic plant | Produces organic substances through photosynthesis | Generally independent for organic food production |
| Insectivorous plant | Photosynthesis; obtains extra mineral nutrients from trapped animals | Captures and digests small animals |
| Holoparasite | Obtains organic nutrients from a host plant | Strong dependence on host |
| Hemiparasite | Photosynthesis, with water and mineral nutrients from a host | Partially dependent on host |
| Mycoheterotroph | Obtains organic nutrients through fungi | Depends on fungal association |
| Epiphyte | Photosynthesis; water and minerals from surroundings | Uses another plant mainly for support |

## 3.3.8 WHY DO SOME PLANTS HAVE SPECIALISED NUTRITIONAL ADAPTATIONS?

Different environments provide different levels of nutrients and resources.

For example:

- Insectivorous plants may grow in nitrogen-poor soils.
- Parasitic plants obtain nutrients from host plants.
- Mycoheterotrophic plants obtain organic nutrients through fungi.
- Epiphytes grow above the forest floor to obtain better access to light.

These adaptations increase the ability of plants to survive in particular habitats.

## 3.3.9 EXAM TIPS

**Question: Explain why insectivorous plants trap insects even though they can photosynthesise.**

Suggested answer:

1. Insectivorous plants often grow in soil with low nitrogen availability.
2. They trap and digest insects.
3. Digestion releases nitrogen-containing mineral nutrients.
4. These nutrients are absorbed and used for growth and protein synthesis.

**Question: Differentiate between holoparasites and hemiparasites.**

Suggested answer:

1. Holoparasites depend on their hosts for organic nutrients and generally cannot photosynthesise sufficiently.
2. Hemiparasites contain chlorophyll and can photosynthesise.
3. Hemiparasites obtain water and mineral nutrients from their hosts.

**Question: Explain why an epiphyte is not necessarily a parasite.**

Suggested answer:

1. An epiphyte grows on another plant mainly for physical support.
2. It obtains water and mineral nutrients from its surrounding environment.
3. It does not necessarily absorb nutrients directly from the host plant.

# CHAPTER 3 — IMPORTANT COMPARISONS

## 1. MACRONUTRIENTS AND MICRONUTRIENTS

**Macronutrients:**

- Required in relatively large quantities.
- Examples: nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium and sulphur.

**Micronutrients:**

- Required in very small quantities.
- Examples: iron, manganese, zinc, copper, boron and molybdenum.

## 2. OSMOSIS, DIFFUSION AND ACTIVE TRANSPORT

**Osmosis:**

- Movement of water.
- From higher water potential to lower water potential.
- Through a selectively permeable membrane.

**Diffusion:**

- Movement of particles down their concentration gradient.
- Does not require ATP.

**Active transport:**

- Movement of substances against their concentration gradient.
- Requires energy from ATP.
- Uses membrane transport proteins.

## 3. HOLOPARASITES AND HEMIPARASITES

**Holoparasites:**

- Depend on a host for organic nutrients.
- Generally lack sufficient chlorophyll for photosynthesis.
- Example: Rafflesia.

**Hemiparasites:**

- Contain chlorophyll.
- Can carry out photosynthesis.
- Obtain water and mineral nutrients from a host.
- Example: mistletoe.

## 4. INSECTIVOROUS PLANTS AND PARASITIC PLANTS

**Insectivorous plants:**

- Trap and digest small animals.
- Obtain additional mineral nutrients, especially nitrogen.
- Usually still photosynthesise.

**Parasitic plants:**

- Obtain some or all nutrients from another living plant.
- Use specialised structures called haustoria.

# COMMON EXAM MISTAKES

1. Macronutrients are required in large quantities, not necessarily because they are more important than micronutrients.
2. Micronutrients are essential even though plants require only small amounts.
3. Nitrogen is required for amino acids and proteins.
4. Magnesium forms the central atom of chlorophyll.
5. Phosphorus is important in ATP, DNA, RNA and phospholipids.
6. Water enters root hair cells mainly through osmosis.
7. Mineral ions can enter by diffusion or active transport.
8. Active transport requires energy from ATP.
9. Root hairs increase the surface area for absorption.
10. Xylem transports water and mineral salts towards the leaves.
11. Insectivorous plants trap animals mainly to obtain mineral nutrients, not because they cannot photosynthesise.
12. Holoparasites depend heavily on their hosts for organic nutrients.
13. Hemiparasites can photosynthesise.
14. Epiphytes use other plants mainly for support and are not necessarily parasites.
15. Mycoheterotrophic plants obtain organic nutrients through fungal associations.

2. They absorb water and mineral nutrients from their surroundings.
3. They do not necessarily obtain nutrients directly from the host plant.
