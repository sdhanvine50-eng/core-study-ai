# 7.1 ADAPTATIONS OF PLANTS

## 7.1.1 INTRODUCTION TO PLANT ADAPTATIONS

Plants live in different habitats, including:

- Deserts.
- Tropical rainforests.
- Mangrove swamps.
- Freshwater environments.
- Coastal areas.
- Mountain environments.
- Grasslands.

Each habitat has different environmental conditions.

Examples of environmental factors include:

1. Availability of water.
2. Light intensity.
3. Temperature.
4. Salinity.
5. Soil conditions.
6. Availability of mineral nutrients.
7. Wind exposure.
8. Oxygen availability around the roots.

Plants possess adaptations that allow them to survive, grow and reproduce under the conditions of their habitats.

**Adaptation** is an inherited structural, physiological or behavioural characteristic that increases an organism's ability to survive and reproduce in a particular environment.

In plants, adaptations can be divided into three major categories.

### A. STRUCTURAL ADAPTATIONS

Structural adaptations are physical characteristics of a plant that help it survive in its habitat.

Examples:

- Thick waxy cuticle.
- Reduced leaves.
- Extensive root systems.
- Pneumatophores.
- Sunken stomata.
- Succulent stems.

### B. PHYSIOLOGICAL ADAPTATIONS

Physiological adaptations involve internal processes and functions that help a plant survive.

Examples:

- Regulation of stomatal opening.
- Salt exclusion or salt secretion.
- Osmotic adjustment.
- CAM photosynthesis.
- Production of substances that protect cells against water loss.

### C. REPRODUCTIVE ADAPTATIONS

Reproductive adaptations help plants reproduce successfully in their habitats.

Examples:

- Floating fruits or seeds.
- Vivipary in certain mangrove species.
- Seed dormancy.
- Seed dispersal by wind or water.

IMPORTANT:

A plant may possess several adaptations at the same time. These adaptations work together to improve survival under particular environmental conditions.

---

# 7.1.2 TERRESTRIAL PLANTS

Terrestrial plants are plants that grow on land.

However, land habitats differ greatly in their environmental conditions.

For example:

- Desert plants experience limited water availability.
- Rainforest plants experience competition for light.
- Plants in cold environments experience low temperatures.
- Plants in windy habitats may experience mechanical stress.

Different terrestrial plants therefore have different adaptations.

---

# A. XEROPHYTES

Xerophytes are plants adapted to survive in habitats where water is limited.

Examples of habitats:

- Deserts.
- Dry grasslands.
- Rocky areas.
- Regions with long dry seasons.

Examples of xerophytic plants include:

- Cacti.
- Aloe.
- Certain desert shrubs.

## 7.1.3 ENVIRONMENTAL CHALLENGES FACED BY XEROPHYTES

Xerophytes face several challenges:

1. Limited water availability.
2. High temperatures.
3. High rates of evaporation.
4. Strong sunlight.
5. Low soil moisture.
6. Irregular rainfall.

The major challenge is preventing excessive water loss while obtaining enough water for photosynthesis and metabolism.

---

## 7.1.4 STRUCTURAL ADAPTATIONS OF XEROPHYTES

### A. REDUCED LEAF SURFACE AREA

Some xerophytes have very small leaves or leaves modified into spines.

Explanation:

1. Smaller leaves have less exposed surface area.
2. Less leaf surface is exposed to the surrounding air.
3. This reduces the potential area for water loss through transpiration.
4. The plant conserves water.

Example:

Cacti have leaves modified into spines.

IMPORTANT:

Spines also provide protection against herbivores.

The green stem carries out most photosynthesis in many cacti.

### B. THICK WAXY CUTICLE

The leaf or stem may have a thick waxy cuticle.

Explanation:

1. The cuticle forms a protective outer covering.
2. Its waxy nature reduces evaporation from the epidermal surface.
3. Less water is lost directly through the plant surface.
4. This helps conserve water.

### C. SUNKEN STOMATA

Some xerophytes have stomata located in depressions below the leaf surface.

Explanation:

1. The depressions trap relatively humid air.
2. Air movement around the stomata may be reduced.
3. The water vapour concentration gradient between the leaf and surrounding air becomes smaller.
4. The rate of water vapour diffusion can decrease.
5. Transpiration is reduced.

### D. LEAF ROLLING

Some grasses roll their leaves during dry conditions.

Explanation:

1. Rolling reduces the exposed leaf surface.
2. Stomata may become enclosed within the rolled leaf.
3. Humid air can become trapped inside.
4. Air movement around the stomata decreases.
5. Water loss through transpiration is reduced.

### E. HAIRY LEAF SURFACE

Some xerophytes have hairs on their leaf surfaces.

Explanation:

1. Hairs trap a layer of still air near the leaf surface.
2. Air movement is reduced.
3. The air layer may become more humid.
4. The water vapour gradient between the leaf and surrounding air decreases.
5. Water loss is reduced.

### F. SUCCULENT STEMS OR LEAVES

Succulent plants have thick, fleshy stems or leaves that store water.

Examples:

- Cactus.
- Aloe.

Explanation:

1. Water is stored in specialised water-storage tissues.
2. The stored water can be used during dry periods.
3. This allows the plant to continue essential metabolic activities when water is unavailable from the soil.

### G. EXTENSIVE ROOT SYSTEM

Some xerophytes possess extensive root systems.

Two common patterns are:

- Deep roots.
- Wide-spreading shallow roots.

Deep roots:

1. Extend into deeper soil layers.
2. Reach water that is unavailable near the surface.
3. Allow water absorption during dry periods.

Wide-spreading roots:

1. Cover a large area of soil.
2. Absorb water quickly after rainfall.
3. Increase the volume of soil explored by the plant.

---

## 7.1.5 PHYSIOLOGICAL ADAPTATIONS OF XEROPHYTES

### A. CAM PHOTOSYNTHESIS

Some succulent plants use a photosynthetic pathway called Crassulacean Acid Metabolism (CAM).

CAM is an adaptation that helps reduce water loss in hot and dry environments.

In many CAM plants:

**At night:**

1. Stomata open.
2. Carbon dioxide enters the leaf.
3. Carbon dioxide is fixed into organic acids, mainly malic acid.
4. These acids are stored in vacuoles.

**During the day:**

1. Stomata remain mostly closed.
2. Stored organic acids release carbon dioxide.
3. Carbon dioxide is used in the Calvin cycle.
4. Photosynthesis continues while daytime water loss is reduced.

WHY IS CAM IMPORTANT?

Opening stomata at night reduces water loss because:

- Temperatures are generally lower.
- Relative humidity is often higher.
- The water vapour gradient may be smaller.

IMPORTANT:

CAM plants do not necessarily photosynthesise only at night.

They usually capture carbon dioxide at night and use the stored carbon dioxide during the day for photosynthesis.

### B. STOMATAL REGULATION

Xerophytes can regulate stomatal opening in response to water availability.

During water stress:

1. Chemical signals such as abscisic acid (ABA) increase.
2. Guard cells lose turgor.
3. Stomata close or become less open.
4. Water vapour loss decreases.
5. Water is conserved.

However, closing stomata also reduces carbon dioxide entry, which may limit photosynthesis.

# B. MESOPHYTES

Mesophytes are plants adapted to habitats with a moderate supply of water.

They do not normally experience extremely dry or permanently waterlogged conditions.

Examples:

- Hibiscus.
- Mango.
- Sunflower.
- Many garden plants.

## 7.1.7 CHARACTERISTICS OF MESOPHYTES

Mesophytes generally have:

1. Well-developed leaves.
2. A moderately developed cuticle.
3. Stomata mainly on the lower epidermis in many broad-leaved plants.
4. A root system that absorbs water from ordinary soil.
5. Normal transpiration and photosynthesis under suitable conditions.

Their structures are adapted to balance:

- Water absorption.
- Water loss.
- Gas exchange.
- Photosynthesis.

### A. NORMAL LEAF STRUCTURE

Mesophytes commonly possess broad leaves.

Advantages:

1. Large surface area receives sunlight.
2. More light can be absorbed for photosynthesis.
3. Leaves can exchange gases through stomata.

### B. STOMATA

Many mesophytic dicot leaves have more stomata on the lower epidermis.

Advantages:

1. The lower surface is often less exposed to direct sunlight.
2. It may experience lower temperatures.
3. This can reduce excessive transpiration while allowing gaseous exchange.

This is a common pattern, not a universal feature of every mesophyte.

### C. CUTICLE

Mesophytes usually have a cuticle that helps reduce uncontrolled water loss.

However, it is generally not as extremely thick as that of many xerophytes.

### D. ROOT SYSTEM

Mesophytes possess roots that absorb water and mineral ions from the soil.

Root hairs increase the surface area available for absorption.

---

# C. HYDROPHYTES

Hydrophytes are plants adapted to live in water or environments where water is abundant.

Examples:

- Water lily.
- Hydrilla.
- Water hyacinth.
- Duckweed.

Hydrophytes may be:

1. Completely submerged.
2. Floating on the water surface.
3. Partially submerged with leaves or stems extending above water.

---

## 7.1.8 ENVIRONMENTAL CHALLENGES FACED BY HYDROPHYTES

Although water is abundant, aquatic plants face other challenges:

1. Limited carbon dioxide availability underwater.
2. Reduced light intensity at greater water depths.
3. Low oxygen availability in waterlogged sediments.
4. Mechanical support is reduced because water provides buoyancy.
5. Water movement may damage delicate structures.

---

## 7.1.9 STRUCTURAL ADAPTATIONS OF HYDROPHYTES

### A. LARGE AIR SPACES

Many aquatic plants contain large air spaces in their tissues.

This tissue is called aerenchyma.

Functions:

1. Stores air.
2. Helps transport gases within the plant.
3. Provides buoyancy.
4. Helps submerged tissues obtain oxygen.

Example:

Water hyacinth has swollen structures containing air spaces that assist flotation.

### B. REDUCED ROOT SYSTEM

Some floating hydrophytes have reduced roots.

Explanation:

1. Water is directly available around the plant.
2. The plant may not require an extensive root system for water absorption.
3. Roots may mainly provide anchorage or absorb some nutrients.

IMPORTANT:

Not all hydrophytes have reduced roots. Root development depends on the species and habitat.

### C. REDUCED CUTICLE

Submerged leaves often have a very thin cuticle or little cuticle.

Explanation:

1. Water is continuously available around the leaf.
2. Preventing water loss is less important.
3. A thin cuticle can allow substances to move across the leaf surface more easily.

Floating leaves may still have a cuticle on their upper surface.

### D. STOMATA POSITION

In floating leaves:

- Stomata are commonly found on the upper epidermis.
- The lower surface is in contact with water.

This arrangement allows gaseous exchange with the air above the water.

Submerged leaves may lack functional stomata and exchange gases directly through their surfaces.

### E. FLEXIBLE STEMS

Some aquatic plants have flexible stems.

Functions:

1. Allow the plant to bend with water currents.
2. Reduce the risk of mechanical damage.
3. Help the plant remain attached while moving with water.

### F. REDUCED SUPPORTING TISSUES

Aquatic plants often have less developed supporting tissues.

Reason:

1. Water provides buoyancy.
2. The plant does not need as much mechanical support against gravity as a terrestrial plant.
3. Reduced supporting tissue can make stems and leaves more flexible.

### G. FINELY DIVIDED SUBMERGED LEAVES

Some submerged plants have narrow or finely divided leaves.

Advantages:

1. Increase the surface area exposed to water relative to tissue volume.
2. Allow efficient exchange of dissolved gases and substances.
3. Reduce resistance to water movement.

---

## 7.1.10 PHYSIOLOGICAL ADAPTATIONS OF HYDROPHYTES

### A. GASEOUS EXCHANGE

Submerged hydrophytes exchange gases directly with surrounding water.

Carbon dioxide dissolved in water can enter the plant for photosynthesis.

Oxygen produced during photosynthesis can diffuse into the surrounding water or be used in respiration.

### B. INTERNAL GAS TRANSPORT

Aerenchyma allows gases to move between different parts of aquatic plants.

This is especially important when roots are surrounded by waterlogged sediments with low oxygen availability.

### C. NUTRIENT ABSORPTION

Some aquatic plants absorb mineral ions through:

- Roots.
- Submerged stems.
- Leaves.

The exact method depends on the species.

---

# D. HALOPHYTES

Halophytes are plants adapted to grow in habitats with high salt concentrations.

Examples of habitats:

- Mangrove swamps.
- Salt marshes.
- Coastal salt flats.
- Saline soils.

Examples:

- Rhizophora.
- Avicennia.
- Sonneratia.

Mangrove plants are important examples of halophytes.

---

# 7.1.13 ENVIRONMENTAL CHALLENGES FACED BY HALOPHYTES

Halophytes face several challenges:

1. High salt concentration in the surrounding water or soil.
2. Difficulty absorbing water because of low water potential in saline soil.
3. Excess accumulation of toxic ions.
4. Low oxygen availability in waterlogged sediments.
5. Unstable muddy soil.
6. Tidal flooding and strong coastal winds.

IMPORTANT:

Even though mangroves grow in water, the high salt concentration makes water uptake difficult.

This is sometimes described as physiological drought.

The plant may be surrounded by water but still experience difficulty obtaining water because the surrounding solution has a very low water potential.

---

# 7.1.14 STRUCTURAL ADAPTATIONS OF HALOPHYTES

### A. PNEUMATOPHORES

Pneumatophores are specialised aerial roots that grow upwards from the waterlogged soil.

They are commonly found in certain mangrove species, such as Avicennia.

Functions:

1. Extend above the waterlogged sediment.
2. Contain lenticels or pores for gaseous exchange.
3. Allow oxygen to enter the root tissues.
4. Help roots obtain oxygen in poorly aerated soil.

This is important because waterlogged sediments contain little oxygen.

### B. STILT ROOTS

Stilt roots are supporting roots that grow from the stem or lower branches towards the soil.

They are common in some Rhizophora mangroves.

Functions:

1. Provide mechanical support.
2. Anchor the plant in soft muddy soil.
3. Help the plant resist waves and tidal movement.

### C. BUTTRESS ROOTS

Some trees develop broad, flattened roots near the base of the trunk.

Functions:

1. Increase stability.
2. Spread mechanical support over a larger area.
3. Help the plant remain upright in unstable soil.

### D. THICK AND FLESHY LEAVES

Some halophytes have succulent leaves.

Functions:

1. Store water.
2. Dilute accumulated salts.
3. Help maintain water balance.

### E. THICK CUTICLE

Some halophytes possess a thick cuticle.

Functions:

1. Reduces excessive water loss.
2. Helps protect leaves against dry coastal winds.
3. Supports water conservation.

---

# 7.1.15 PHYSIOLOGICAL ADAPTATIONS OF HALOPHYTES

### A. SALT EXCLUSION

Some mangrove plants reduce the amount of salt entering their roots.

Explanation:

1. Specialised root tissues restrict the entry of many salt ions.
2. Water can still be absorbed.
3. Excessive salt accumulation in plant tissues is reduced.

### B. SALT SECRETION

Some halophytes remove excess salt through specialised salt glands.

Explanation:

1. Salt ions enter the plant.
2. Specialised cells transport excess salts towards the leaf surface.
3. Salt is released onto the leaf surface.
4. Rain or other water movement may wash the salt away.

This is found in certain mangrove species, including some Avicennia species.

### C. ION COMPARTMENTALISATION

Some halophytes store excess salts inside vacuoles.

Explanation:

1. Excess salt ions are transported into vacuoles.
2. The cytoplasm is protected from excessive ion concentrations.
3. Water balance can be regulated through osmotic adjustment.

### D. OSMOTIC ADJUSTMENT

Halophytes may accumulate compatible solutes such as certain sugars and amino-acid derivatives.

These substances help lower the water potential of cells.

As a result:

1. Water absorption becomes more favourable.
2. Cells maintain turgor.
3. Normal cellular functions can continue under saline conditions.

---

# 7.1.16 REPRODUCTIVE ADAPTATIONS OF MANGROVES

Some mangrove plants have a special reproductive adaptation called vivipary.

### VIVIPARY

Vivipary is a condition in which the seed germinates while still attached to the parent plant.

In many viviparous mangroves:

1. The embryo develops while the fruit remains attached to the parent plant.
2. The elongated propagule develops.
3. It eventually detaches from the parent plant.
4. It may fall into the mud or water.
5. It can become established in a suitable location.

Advantages:

- The young plant has already developed before detachment.
- It may establish more quickly after reaching suitable sediment.
- It increases the chance of successful establishment in unstable coastal habitats.

IMPORTANT:

Vivipary is found in some mangrove species, not all mangroves.

# 7.1.18 COMPARISON: XEROPHYTES AND HALOPHYTES

Both xerophytes and halophytes may experience difficulty obtaining water.

However, the main environmental causes differ.

| Xerophytes | Halophytes |
|---|---|
| Adapted to dry habitats | Adapted to saline habitats |
| Water is physically limited | Water may be present but difficult to absorb |
| High evaporation is a major challenge | High salt concentration is a major challenge |
| Often have reduced leaves and thick cuticles | May have salt exclusion or secretion mechanisms |
| Some use CAM photosynthesis | Some use ion compartmentalisation and osmotic adjustment |

# 7.1.19 PLANTS IN DIFFERENT HABITATS: OVERALL COMPARISON

| Habitat | Plant group | Main challenge | Important adaptations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Desert | Xerophytes | Water shortage | Spines, thick cuticle, succulent stems, deep roots |
| Moderate terrestrial habitat | Mesophytes | Balancing water and gas exchange | Broad leaves, normal stomata and roots |
| Aquatic habitat | Hydrophytes | Gas exchange and support | Aerenchyma, flexible stems, reduced cuticle |
| Saline coastal habitat | Halophytes | Salt stress and low soil oxygen | Pneumatophores, salt glands, stilt roots |

# 7.1.20 WHY DO PLANTS NEED DIFFERENT ADAPTATIONS?

Plants cannot survive equally well in every habitat because environmental conditions differ.

For example:

A cactus is adapted to conserve water in dry habitats.

A water lily is adapted to float and exchange gases above the water surface.

A mangrove is adapted to tolerate saline conditions and waterlogged soil.

A typical garden hibiscus is adapted to conditions where water availability is more moderate.

These examples show that an adaptation that is useful in one habitat may not be necessary or beneficial in another.

---

# 7.1.21 ADAPTATION DOES NOT MEAN THAT A PLANT NEVER EXPERIENCES STRESS

Adaptations improve survival, but they do not make plants completely unaffected by environmental conditions.

For example:

- A cactus can still suffer if water shortage becomes too severe.
- A hydrophyte can still be affected by pollution or insufficient light.
- A mangrove can still be damaged by extreme changes in salinity.
- A mesophyte can wilt when soil water becomes insufficient.

Adaptations increase the likelihood of survival and reproduction under particular environmental conditions.

# COMMON EXAM MISTAKES

## 1. Xerophytes and hydrophytes are not the same

Xerophytes are adapted to dry habitats.

Hydrophytes are adapted to aquatic or water-abundant habitats.

## 2. Mangroves are halophytes

Mangroves are adapted to saline coastal habitats.

They may also possess adaptations for waterlogged soil.

## 3. Pneumatophores are not ordinary absorption roots

Pneumatophores are specialised aerial roots involved in gaseous exchange.

## 4. Aerenchyma is important in aquatic plants

Aerenchyma contains large air spaces.

It assists internal gas movement and buoyancy.

## 5. Spines reduce leaf surface area

In cacti, leaves are modified into spines.

The green stem performs much of the photosynthesis.

## 6. Sunken stomata help conserve water

They trap humid air and reduce air movement around the stomata.

## 7. Not all aquatic plants have reduced roots

Some hydrophytes have well-developed roots for anchorage and nutrient absorption.

## 8. Not all mangroves have vivipary

Vivipary occurs in certain mangrove species.

## 9. Water abundance does not mean easy water absorption

Saline soil can have low water potential, making water uptake difficult.

## 10. CAM and ordinary photosynthesis are not separate alternatives

CAM changes the timing of carbon dioxide uptake and helps conserve water.

The plant still uses the Calvin cycle to produce carbohydrates.

Define xerophytes.

Xerophytes are plants adapted to survive in habitats where water availability is limited.

## QUESTION 2

Explain how a thick waxy cuticle helps xerophytes survive in dry habitats.

Explain how sunken stomata reduce water loss.

SUGGESTED ANSWER:

1. Sunken stomata are located in depressions below the leaf surface.

A cactus has leaves modified into spines and a thick green stem.

1. Spines reduce the leaf surface area exposed to the air.
2. This reduces water loss through transpiration.
3. The spines also provide protection against herbivores.
4. The thick stem stores water.
5. The green stem carries out photosynthesis.

## QUESTION 5

Describe CAM photosynthesis in xerophytes.

SUGGESTED ANSWER:

1. Stomata open mainly at night.
2. Carbon dioxide enters the plant.
3. Carbon dioxide is fixed into organic acids.
4. Organic acids are stored in vacuoles.
5. During the day, stomata remain mostly closed.
6. Stored organic acids release carbon dioxide.
7. Carbon dioxide is used in the Calvin cycle for photosynthesis.

## QUESTION 6

Define hydrophytes.

SUGGESTED ANSWER:

Hydrophytes are plants adapted to live in aquatic habitats or environments where water is abundant.

## QUESTION 7

Explain how aerenchyma helps aquatic plants survive.

SUGGESTED ANSWER:

1. Aerenchyma contains large air spaces.
2. The air spaces store gases.
3. They allow gases to move between different plant tissues.
4. They provide buoyancy.
5. This helps submerged tissues obtain oxygen and allows some plants to float.

## QUESTION 8

A floating aquatic plant has stomata mainly on its upper leaf surface.

Explain why this arrangement is suitable.

SUGGESTED ANSWER:

1. The upper leaf surface is exposed to the air.
2. The lower surface is in contact with water.
3. Stomata on the upper surface allow carbon dioxide to enter from the atmosphere.
4. Oxygen and water vapour can also move between the leaf and surrounding air.

## QUESTION 9

Define halophytes.

SUGGESTED ANSWER:

Halophytes are plants adapted to grow in habitats with high salt concentrations.

## QUESTION 10

Explain why mangrove plants may experience difficulty absorbing water even though they grow in water.

SUGGESTED ANSWER:

1. The surrounding water or soil contains a high concentration of dissolved salts.
2. The water potential of the saline environment is low.
3. Water absorption by the roots becomes difficult.
4. The plant may experience physiological drought.

## QUESTION 11

Explain how pneumatophores help mangrove plants survive in waterlogged soil.

SUGGESTED ANSWER:

1. Pneumatophores grow upwards above the waterlogged sediment.
2. They contain lenticels or pores.
3. Oxygen enters through these structures.
4. Oxygen is transported to underground root tissues.
5. This allows root respiration in poorly aerated soil.

## QUESTION 12

Differentiate between pneumatophores and stilt roots.

SUGGESTED ANSWER:

Pneumatophores are specialised aerial roots that assist gaseous exchange, whereas stilt roots provide anchorage and mechanical support in soft muddy soil.

## QUESTION 13

Explain how salt secretion helps certain mangrove plants survive.

SUGGESTED ANSWER:

1. Excess salt ions are transported towards specialised salt glands.
2. Salt is released onto the leaf surface.
3. Rain or water movement washes the salt away.
4. Excessive salt accumulation inside the plant is reduced.

## QUESTION 14

Compare xerophytes and hydrophytes.

SUGGESTED ANSWER:

1. Xerophytes are adapted to dry habitats, whereas hydrophytes are adapted to aquatic habitats.
2. Xerophytes often have thick cuticles, whereas submerged hydrophytes often have thin cuticles.
3. Xerophytes may have reduced leaves to conserve water, whereas hydrophytes may have finely divided submerged leaves for exchange.
4. Xerophytes may have extensive root systems, whereas some floating hydrophytes have reduced roots.

## QUESTION 15

A plant grows in coastal mud that is frequently flooded by seawater. Its roots extend above the soil surface.

Identify the likely plant group and explain the function of the exposed roots.

SUGGESTED ANSWER:

The plant is likely a halophyte, such as a mangrove.

The exposed roots are likely pneumatophores. They allow oxygen to enter the underground root system because waterlogged soil contains limited oxygen.

- Stomatal closure during water stress.

## MESOPHYTES

Habitat: Environments with moderate water availability.

Main challenge: Balancing water conservation with photosynthesis and gaseous exchange.

Characteristics:

- Broad, well-developed leaves.
- Moderately developed cuticle.
- Functional stomata.
- Well-developed roots.
- Normal transpiration under suitable conditions.

## HYDROPHYTES

Habitat: Aquatic or water-abundant environments.

Main challenges: Gaseous exchange, light availability and mechanical support.

Adaptations:

- Large air spaces.
- Aerenchyma.
- Reduced cuticle in submerged leaves.
- Stomata on the upper surface of floating leaves.
- Flexible stems.
- Reduced supporting tissues.
- Finely divided submerged leaves.

## HALOPHYTES

Habitat: Saline environments, including mangrove swamps.

Main challenges: High salt concentration, physiological drought and low oxygen in waterlogged soil.

Adaptations:

- Pneumatophores.
- Stilt roots.
- Buttress roots.
- Thick cuticle.
- Succulent leaves.
- Salt exclusion.
- Salt secretion.
- Ion compartmentalisation.
- Osmotic adjustment.
- Vivipary in certain mangrove species.

# ULTRA-HIGH-YIELD MEMORY MAP

**XEROPHYTES = DRY**

- Thick cuticle.
- Spines.
- Sunken stomata.
- Succulent stems.
- Deep roots.
- CAM.

**MESOPHYTES = MODERATE WATER**

- Broad leaves.
- Normal stomata.
- Moderate cuticle.
- Well-developed roots.

**HYDROPHYTES = WATER**

- Aerenchyma.
- Air spaces.
- Buoyancy.
- Flexible stems.
- Reduced cuticle in submerged leaves.

**HALOPHYTES = SALT**

- Pneumatophores.
- Stilt roots.
- Salt glands.
- Salt exclusion.
- Osmotic adjustment.
- Vivipary in some species.
