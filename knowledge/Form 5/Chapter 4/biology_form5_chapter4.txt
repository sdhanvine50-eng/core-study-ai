# CHAPTER INTRODUCTION

Plants are living organisms that require water, mineral salts and organic nutrients to survive, grow and reproduce.

Unlike animals, most plants do not have a heart or a specialised pumping organ to transport substances around their bodies. Instead, plants use specialised vascular tissues to move materials over long distances.

The two main vascular tissues are:

1. Xylem
2. Phloem

**Xylem** transports water and mineral salts, mainly from the roots towards the stems and leaves.

**Phloem** transports organic substances, especially sucrose, from sources where they are produced or released to sinks where they are used or stored.

Transport in plants is important for:

- Photosynthesis.
- Respiration.
- Growth and development.
- Storage of nutrients.
- Maintenance of cell functions.
- Removal or management of certain harmful substances.

This chapter explains the structures and functions of vascular tissues, the movement of water and mineral salts, translocation and the use of plants to remove or manage pollutants.

# 4.1 VASCULAR TISSUES

## 4.1.1 Introduction to Vascular Tissues

Vascular tissues are specialised plant tissues responsible for transporting substances throughout a plant.

They form a continuous transport system connecting the roots, stems and leaves.

There are two main types:

**1. Xylem**

- Transports water and mineral salts.
- Transport is mainly upwards, from roots to leaves.
- Also provides mechanical support to the plant.

**2. Phloem**

- Transports organic substances such as sucrose and amino acids.
- Moves substances from sources to sinks.
- Transport can occur in different directions in different sieve tubes.

Both tissues are found in vascular bundles.

A vascular bundle is a strand of vascular tissue containing xylem and phloem.

## 4.1.2 Structure and Functions of Xylem

Xylem is a complex permanent tissue responsible for transporting water and dissolved mineral salts from the roots to other parts of the plant.

It also provides mechanical strength.

Xylem consists of several types of cells:

1. Vessel elements.
2. Tracheids.
3. Xylem fibres.
4. Xylem parenchyma.

### A. Vessel Elements

Vessel elements are elongated cells arranged end to end to form continuous vessels.

Characteristics:

- Mature vessel elements are dead.
- They do not contain a nucleus or cytoplasm.
- Their end walls are perforated or absent, allowing water to flow between neighbouring elements.
- Their walls are thickened with lignin.
- They form hollow tubes with a relatively wide lumen.

Functions:

1. Transport water and mineral salts.
2. Provide a continuous pathway for water movement.
3. Support the plant through lignified walls.

**Why are vessel elements dead at maturity?**

The absence of cytoplasm and other internal contents creates a hollow space. This reduces resistance to water movement through the vessel.

### B. Tracheids

Tracheids are long, narrow cells with tapering ends.

Characteristics:

- Mature tracheids are dead.
- Their walls are thickened with lignin.
- They have pits in their walls.
- They do not have perforated end plates like typical vessel elements.

Functions:

1. Transport water between neighbouring cells through pits.
2. Provide mechanical support.
3. Help maintain the structure of the xylem.

Water can move from one tracheid to another through pits in their walls.

### C. Xylem Fibres

Xylem fibres are elongated cells with thick, lignified walls.

Characteristics:

- They are usually dead at maturity.
- Their walls are thick and strong.
- They have narrow internal spaces.

Function:

- Provide mechanical strength and support to the plant.

### D. Xylem Parenchyma

Xylem parenchyma consists of living parenchyma cells associated with the xylem.

Functions:

1. Store food substances such as starch.
2. Participate in the movement of substances within the xylem.
3. Help maintain and support xylem tissue.

### Summary of Xylem Cell Types

| Cell type | Living or dead at maturity | Main function |
|---|---|---|
| Vessel elements | Dead | Water transport |
| Tracheids | Dead | Water transport and support |
| Xylem fibres | Usually dead | Mechanical support |
| Xylem parenchyma | Living | Storage and transport support |

## 4.1.3 Lignification of Xylem

Lignin is a complex substance deposited in the walls of certain plant cells.

The deposition of lignin is called lignification.

Lignification makes xylem walls:

- Strong.
- Rigid.
- Less likely to collapse.
- More resistant to pressure changes.

Different patterns of lignin deposition can occur in xylem vessels, including:

- Annular thickening.
- Spiral thickening.
- Scalariform thickening.
- Reticulate thickening.
- Pitted thickening.

These patterns allow vessels to provide support while accommodating different growth and transport requirements.

**Why is lignin important in xylem?**

Water transported through xylem is under tension during transpiration. Lignified walls help prevent the vessels from collapsing under this tension.

Lignin also strengthens the plant and helps stems remain upright.

## 4.1.4 Structure and Functions of Phloem

Phloem is a complex permanent tissue responsible for transporting organic substances throughout a plant.

The main substances transported include:

- Sucrose.
- Amino acids.
- Other organic nutrients.

Phloem consists of:

1. Sieve tube elements.
2. Companion cells.
3. Phloem parenchyma.
4. Phloem fibres.

### A. Sieve Tube Elements

Sieve tube elements are elongated living cells arranged end to end.

Characteristics:

- They form long sieve tubes.
- Their end walls form sieve plates.
- Sieve plates contain pores that allow substances to pass between neighbouring sieve tube elements.
- Mature sieve tube elements have very little cytoplasm.
- They lack a nucleus at maturity.
- They depend on companion cells to support their metabolic activities.

Functions:

- Transport dissolved organic substances through the plant.
- Allow movement of substances between neighbouring sieve tube elements.

### B. Sieve Plates

Sieve plates are specialised end walls between neighbouring sieve tube elements.

They contain pores that allow phloem sap to pass from one sieve tube element to another.

The pores reduce resistance to the movement of substances along the sieve tube.

### C. Companion Cells

Companion cells are living cells closely associated with sieve tube elements.

Characteristics:

- They contain a nucleus.
- They contain dense cytoplasm.
- They have many mitochondria.
- They are connected to sieve tube elements through plasmodesmata.

Functions:

1. Provide metabolic support to sieve tube elements.
2. Supply energy for active transport processes involved in phloem loading and unloading.
3. Help regulate the movement of substances into and out of sieve tubes.

**Why do sieve tube elements need companion cells?**

Mature sieve tube elements lack a nucleus and have limited cytoplasm. Companion cells help maintain their functions and provide metabolic support.

### D. Phloem Parenchyma

Phloem parenchyma consists of living cells.

Functions:

- Store organic substances.
- Help with the lateral movement of substances.
- Support the functioning of phloem tissue.

### E. Phloem Fibres

Phloem fibres are elongated cells with thick walls.

They mainly provide mechanical support to the plant.

Some phloem fibres are dead at maturity.

## 4.1.5 Comparison Between Xylem and Phloem

| Feature | Xylem | Phloem |
|---|---|---|
| Main function | Transports water and mineral salts | Transports organic substances |
| Main transported material | Water and mineral ions | Mainly sucrose and amino acids |
| Direction | Mainly upwards | Source to sink |
| Main conducting cells | Vessel elements and tracheids | Sieve tube elements |
| Conducting cells at maturity | Dead | Living, but sieve tube elements lack a nucleus |
| Supporting cells | Xylem fibres | Phloem fibres |
| Special structures | Lignified walls and pits | Sieve plates and companion cells |
| Energy requirement | Water transport is mainly passive | Loading and unloading may require energy |
| Additional role | Mechanical support | Distribution of organic nutrients |

**Important exam point:**

Do not write that phloem always transports substances upwards or always downwards. Phloem transport depends on the locations of sources and sinks.

## 4.1.6 Arrangement of Vascular Tissues

Xylem and phloem are arranged in vascular bundles.

Their arrangement differs between plant organs.

### A. In Roots

In many dicot roots:

- Xylem is found near the centre.
- Xylem often forms a star-shaped arrangement.
- Phloem is located between the arms of the xylem.

### B. In Stems

In many dicot stems:

- Vascular bundles are arranged in a ring.
- Xylem is located towards the inside.
- Phloem is located towards the outside.
- Vascular cambium may be present between xylem and phloem.

### C. In Leaves

Vascular tissues form the veins and midrib of leaves.

- Xylem is generally towards the upper side of the vascular bundle.
- Phloem is generally towards the lower side.

The veins transport water and mineral salts to leaf tissues and carry organic substances away from photosynthetic tissues.

**Note:** These are common arrangements in typical flowering plants. Vascular bundle arrangements can vary between plant groups.

## 4.1.7 Importance of Vascular Tissues

Vascular tissues allow plants to grow larger and develop more complex structures.

Without an efficient transport system:

- Water absorbed by roots would not reach distant leaves effectively.
- Mineral salts would not be distributed efficiently.
- Sugars produced during photosynthesis would not be distributed to non-photosynthetic tissues.
- Growth and metabolism would be limited.

Xylem and phloem therefore work together to maintain the life processes of plants.

# 4.2 TRANSPORT OF WATER AND MINERAL SALTS

## 4.2.1 Introduction

Plants absorb water and mineral salts mainly through their roots.

Water is essential for:

1. Photosynthesis.
2. Maintaining cell turgidity.
3. Supporting plant tissues.
4. Transporting dissolved substances.
5. Maintaining the temperature of plant tissues.
6. Participating in metabolic reactions.

Mineral salts provide essential elements required for plant growth and metabolism.

Examples include:

- Nitrate ions.
- Magnesium ions.
- Phosphate ions.
- Potassium ions.
- Calcium ions.

Water and mineral salts are absorbed from the soil and transported through the xylem.

The movement of water through a plant can be understood in three main stages:

1. Absorption by root hairs.
2. Movement through the root cortex and into the xylem.
3. Transport through the xylem towards the leaves.

## 4.2.2 Structure and Adaptations of Root Hair Cells

Root hair cells are specialised epidermal cells found near the tips of young roots.

They increase the surface area available for absorbing water and mineral ions.

### Adaptations of Root Hair Cells

**1. Long hair-like projection**

- Provides a large surface area.
- Increases contact with soil particles.
- Improves the absorption of water and mineral ions.

**2. Thin cell wall**

- Provides a short distance for water to move into the cell.
- Allows efficient absorption.

**3. Large vacuole**

- Contains cell sap.
- Helps maintain a low water potential inside the cell.
- Supports the entry of water by osmosis.

**4. Many mitochondria**

- Provide ATP through cellular respiration.
- Supply energy for active transport of mineral ions.

**5. Close contact with soil particles**

- Allows the root hair to absorb water and dissolved mineral ions from the soil solution.

**Exam tip:**

When explaining why root hair cells are efficient at absorbing water, connect their large surface area and water potential difference to osmosis.

When explaining mineral ion absorption, mention active transport where ions move against their concentration gradient.

## 4.2.3 Absorption of Water by Osmosis

Water enters root hair cells mainly by osmosis.

Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential through a selectively permeable membrane.

The soil solution usually has a higher water potential than the cell sap of a root hair cell.

Therefore, water moves:

Soil solution  
↓  
Root hair cell

The selectively permeable plasma membrane controls the movement of water and other substances into the cell.

After entering the root hair cell, water moves through neighbouring cells towards the centre of the root.

**Important:**

Water does not move because the root hair cell simply "wants" water. The movement occurs because of a water potential gradient.

## 4.2.4 Absorption of Mineral Salts

Mineral ions are absorbed from the soil solution by root cells.

Some mineral ions enter by diffusion when their concentration is higher in the soil solution than inside the root cells.

However, many mineral ions are absorbed by active transport.

Active transport is the movement of substances across a membrane against their concentration gradient using energy from ATP and transport proteins.

### Why is Active Transport Needed?

Sometimes the concentration of a particular mineral ion is lower in the soil solution than inside the root cell.

The plant may still need to absorb the ion.

Therefore, the ion must move against its concentration gradient.

Active transport allows this movement.

### Role of Mitochondria

Mitochondria carry out aerobic respiration and produce ATP.

ATP provides energy for active transport.

This is why root hair cells contain many mitochondria.

**Comparison: Diffusion and Active Transport**

| Feature | Diffusion | Active transport |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | Down a concentration gradient | Against a concentration gradient |
| Energy | Does not require ATP directly | Requires energy, commonly from ATP |
| Membrane proteins | May or may not be involved | Requires specific transport proteins |
| Example | Movement of some mineral ions into roots | Uptake of mineral ions against their concentration gradient |

## 4.2.5 Pathways of Water Movement Across Roots

After entering the root hair cells, water moves across the root cortex towards the xylem.

There are two main pathways:

1. Apoplast pathway.
2. Symplast pathway.

### A. Apoplast Pathway

The apoplast pathway involves the movement of water through:

- Cell walls.
- Intercellular spaces.

Water moves outside the plasma membranes of living cells.

Characteristics:

- Water moves through the cell walls and spaces between cells.
- It does not need to cross the plasma membrane at every cell boundary.
- Movement can be relatively rapid.

However, the apoplast pathway is interrupted at the endodermis by the Casparian strip.

### B. Symplast Pathway

The symplast pathway involves the movement of water through the cytoplasm of living cells.

Neighbouring cells are connected by plasmodesmata.

Plasmodesmata are small cytoplasmic channels that pass through cell walls and connect adjacent plant cells.

Characteristics:

- Water enters the cytoplasm through a plasma membrane.
- It moves from cell to cell through plasmodesmata.
- It passes through the living contents of the cells.

### Comparison Between Apoplast and Symplast Pathways

| Feature | Apoplast | Symplast |
|---|---|---|
| Route | Cell walls and intercellular spaces | Cytoplasm of living cells |
| Plasma membrane crossing | Not required at every cell boundary | Water enters the symplast through a membrane |
| Main connections | Cell walls and spaces | Plasmodesmata |
| Casparian strip | Blocked at endodermis | Can continue through the endodermal cytoplasm |

## 4.2.6 Role of the Endodermis and Casparian Strip

The endodermis is a specialised layer of cells surrounding the vascular cylinder in roots.

The walls of endodermal cells contain a waterproof band called the Casparian strip.

The Casparian strip contains suberin, a water-resistant substance.

### Functions of the Casparian Strip

1. Blocks uncontrolled movement of water through cell walls.
2. Forces water and dissolved substances to cross a plasma membrane before entering the vascular cylinder.
3. Helps regulate which ions enter the xylem.
4. Reduces the passive entry of some unwanted substances.

**Why is the Casparian strip important?**

It ensures that water and mineral ions entering the vascular cylinder are subject to selective control by cell membranes.

This helps the plant regulate the composition of substances entering the xylem.

## 4.2.7 Movement of Water into the Xylem

Water moves from the root cortex towards the vascular cylinder.

It crosses the endodermis and enters the xylem.

Mineral ions are also transported into the xylem.

The accumulation of dissolved ions in the xylem can lower its water potential.

Water then moves into the xylem by osmosis.

The water in the xylem forms a continuous column extending from the roots towards the stems and leaves.

## 4.2.8 Transport of Water Through Xylem

Water is transported upwards through xylem mainly through the transpiration pull mechanism.

Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from the aerial parts of a plant, mainly through the stomata of leaves.

The loss of water from leaves creates tension in the xylem.

This tension helps pull water upwards from the roots.

The movement of water through xylem is explained by the cohesion-tension mechanism.

## 4.2.9 Cohesion-Tension Mechanism

The cohesion-tension mechanism explains how water moves upwards through xylem.

It depends on:

1. Transpiration.
2. Cohesion.
3. Adhesion.
4. Tension in the xylem.

### Step-by-Step Mechanism

**Step 1: Water evaporates from mesophyll cells**

Water evaporates from the moist surfaces of mesophyll cells into the air spaces inside the leaf.

**Step 2: Water vapour diffuses out**

Water vapour diffuses from the leaf air spaces through the stomata into the surrounding atmosphere.

This process is part of transpiration.

**Step 3: Water potential in the leaf decreases**

As water is lost from mesophyll cell surfaces, the water potential of the leaf tissues decreases.

**Step 4: Water moves from the xylem into leaf tissues**

Water moves from the xylem towards the mesophyll cells by osmosis and other water movement pathways.

**Step 5: Tension develops in the xylem**

The removal of water from the xylem creates tension, or negative pressure, in the water column.

**Step 6: Water is pulled upwards**

The tension is transmitted down the continuous column of water.

Water is pulled upwards from the roots through the xylem.

**Step 7: Roots absorb replacement water**

Water enters the root hair cells from the soil and moves towards the xylem, replacing water lost through transpiration.

### Role of Cohesion

Cohesion is the attraction between molecules of the same substance.

Water molecules attract one another through hydrogen bonding.

Cohesion helps maintain a continuous column of water inside xylem vessels.

### Role of Adhesion

Adhesion is the attraction between molecules of different substances.

Water molecules are attracted to the walls of xylem vessels.

Adhesion helps water remain in contact with the vessel walls and supports water movement through narrow tubes.

### Role of Transpiration Pull

Transpiration pull is the pulling force generated by water loss from the leaves.

It creates tension in the xylem and pulls the continuous water column upwards.

**Important exam distinction:**

- Cohesion: attraction between water molecules.
- Adhesion: attraction between water molecules and xylem walls.
- Transpiration pull: pulling force created by water loss from leaves.
- Tension: negative pressure generated in the xylem.

## 4.2.10 Other Factors Contributing to Water Movement

### A. Root Pressure

Root pressure is a positive pressure that can develop in the xylem when mineral ions accumulate in it and water enters by osmosis.

This can push water upwards over short distances.

Root pressure may contribute to guttation in some plants.

However, root pressure is generally not sufficient to explain water transport in tall plants.

### B. Capillary Action

Capillary action is the movement of liquid through narrow tubes due to cohesion and adhesion.

The narrow diameter of xylem vessels helps capillary effects occur.

However, capillary action alone cannot explain the transport of water to the tops of tall trees.

### C. Transpiration Pull

Transpiration pull is the main driving force for the upward movement of water in most tall plants.

## 4.2.11 Factors Affecting the Rate of Transpiration

The rate of transpiration is affected by environmental conditions and leaf characteristics.

### A. Light Intensity

When light intensity increases:

- Stomata generally open.
- More water vapour can diffuse out.
- Transpiration rate generally increases.

In darkness, stomata often close, reducing water loss.

### B. Temperature

When temperature increases:

- Water evaporates more rapidly from mesophyll surfaces.
- Water vapour diffuses more rapidly.
- Transpiration rate generally increases.

Extremely high temperatures may cause stomata to close, reducing transpiration.

### C. Humidity

Humidity refers to the amount of water vapour in the air.

When humidity is high:

- The water vapour concentration gradient between the leaf and atmosphere becomes smaller.
- Diffusion of water vapour out of the leaf becomes slower.
- Transpiration rate decreases.

When humidity is low, transpiration generally increases.

### D. Wind Speed

When wind speed increases:

- Water vapour surrounding the leaf is removed.
- The boundary layer becomes thinner.
- The water vapour concentration gradient is maintained.
- Transpiration generally increases.

Very strong wind may cause stomata to close or trigger water stress responses.

### E. Water Availability in Soil

When water is readily available:

- Root cells can absorb water.
- Stomata may remain open under suitable conditions.
- Transpiration can continue.

When soil water is limited:

- Plants may close their stomata.
- Water loss decreases.
- Photosynthesis may also decrease because less carbon dioxide can enter.

### F. Leaf Surface Area

A larger leaf surface area may increase the area available for water evaporation.

However, the actual transpiration rate also depends on stomatal number, cuticle thickness and environmental conditions.

### Summary Table

| Factor | Increase in factor | General effect on transpiration |
|---|---|---|
| Light intensity | More light | Increases |
| Temperature | Higher temperature | Increases |
| Humidity | More humid air | Decreases |
| Wind speed | Faster air movement | Increases |
| Soil water availability | More available water | May support higher transpiration |
| Leaf surface area | Larger surface area | May increase water loss |

## 4.2.12 Transpiration and Stomata

Stomata are small pores found mainly on the epidermis of leaves.

Each stoma is surrounded by two guard cells.

Guard cells regulate the opening and closing of the stomatal pore.

### When Guard Cells Become Turgid

1. Guard cells absorb water.
2. Their turgor pressure increases.
3. The cells change shape.
4. The stomatal pore opens.

### When Guard Cells Become Flaccid

1. Guard cells lose water.
2. Their turgor pressure decreases.
3. The cells become less swollen.
4. The stomatal pore closes or becomes narrower.

Opening and closing of stomata regulate the exchange of gases and the loss of water vapour.

## 4.2.13 Potometer

A potometer is an apparatus used to estimate the rate of water uptake by a cut shoot.

It is commonly used to investigate how environmental factors affect water uptake.

### Principle

When a shoot loses water through transpiration, it takes up more water through its cut stem.

The movement of an air bubble in a potometer can be used to estimate the rate of water uptake.

### Limitations

A potometer does not directly measure the rate of transpiration.

This is because:

- Some absorbed water may be used in photosynthesis and other metabolic processes.
- Some water may be stored in plant tissues.
- Not all absorbed water is necessarily lost through transpiration.

Therefore, water uptake is used as an estimate of transpiration.

### Factors That Must Be Controlled

When investigating the effect of one environmental factor, other variables should be kept constant.

Examples:

- Leaf area.
- Temperature.
- Light intensity.
- Wind speed.
- Humidity.
- Type and size of shoot.

## 4.2.14 Guttation

Guttation is the loss of liquid water droplets from the edges or tips of leaves through specialised structures called hydathodes.

It commonly occurs when:

- Soil water availability is high.
- Transpiration is low.
- Root pressure develops.

### Guttation vs Transpiration

| Feature | Guttation | Transpiration |
|---|---|---|
| Form of water lost | Liquid droplets | Water vapour |
| Main structures | Hydathodes | Mainly stomata |
| Common conditions | High soil water availability and low transpiration | Occurs under conditions that allow evaporation and diffusion |
| Main driving factor | Root pressure often contributes | Transpiration pull is associated with water loss |

Do not confuse guttation with dew. Dew forms when water vapour in the surrounding air condenses on a cool surface.

# 4.3 TRANSLOCATION

## 4.3.1 Introduction to Translocation

Translocation is the transport of organic substances through the phloem from sources to sinks.

The main transported substance is sucrose.

Other transported substances include:

- Amino acids.
- Organic acids.
- Hormones.
- Other dissolved organic compounds.

Translocation distributes the products of photosynthesis and stored organic materials to different parts of the plant.

Examples:

- Sucrose produced in leaves can be transported to growing roots.
- Organic substances can be transported to developing fruits.
- Stored substances can be moved from storage organs to growing shoots.

## 4.3.2 Sources and Sinks

Understanding translocation requires understanding two important terms:

1. Source.
2. Sink.

### A. Source

A source is a region of a plant where organic substances are produced or released into the phloem.

Examples:

- Mature photosynthetic leaves producing sucrose.
- Storage organs releasing stored sugars during growth.
- Cotyledons supplying nutrients to a developing seedling.

### B. Sink

A sink is a region where transported organic substances are used or stored.

Examples:

- Growing roots.
- Developing leaves.
- Flowers.
- Fruits.
- Seeds.
- Storage organs such as tubers.

A plant organ can act as a source at one stage of development and a sink at another.

For example:

- A young leaf is usually a sink because it receives sugars.
- A mature leaf can become a source when it produces more sugars than it uses.

## 4.3.3 Examples of Translocation

**Example 1: Mature leaf to root**

A mature leaf produces sucrose during photosynthesis.

Sucrose enters the phloem and is transported to the root.

The root uses the sucrose for respiration and growth or stores it.

**Example 2: Leaf to fruit**

A photosynthetic leaf produces organic substances.

These substances are transported through the phloem to a developing fruit.

The fruit uses them for growth and may store them.

**Example 3: Storage organ to growing shoot**

A storage organ may contain stored carbohydrates.

When a plant begins to grow, these carbohydrates can be converted into soluble sugars and transported to developing shoots.

## 4.3.4 Phloem Sap

Phloem sap is the fluid transported through the sieve tubes.

It contains dissolved organic substances, especially sucrose.

Its composition can vary depending on:

- Plant species.
- Developmental stage.
- Source and sink locations.
- Environmental conditions.

The movement of phloem sap is associated with pressure differences between source and sink regions.

## 4.3.5 Pressure Flow Hypothesis

The pressure flow hypothesis, also known as the mass flow hypothesis, explains the movement of phloem sap from sources to sinks.

It proposes that differences in hydrostatic pressure between source and sink regions drive the bulk flow of phloem sap.

### Step-by-Step Mechanism

**Step 1: Sucrose is produced at the source**

Mature leaves produce glucose during photosynthesis.

Some glucose is converted into sucrose.

Sucrose is the main sugar transported in phloem.

**Step 2: Sucrose is loaded into the phloem**

Sucrose moves from source cells into the sieve tube elements.

Phloem loading may involve active transport and transport proteins.

Companion cells provide metabolic support and energy for this process.

**Step 3: Water enters the sieve tubes**

As sucrose concentration increases in the sieve tube:

- Its solute concentration increases.
- Its water potential decreases.
- Water moves from nearby xylem into the phloem by osmosis.

**Step 4: Hydrostatic pressure increases**

The entry of water increases the hydrostatic pressure inside the sieve tube at the source.

**Step 5: Phloem sap moves towards the sink**

The pressure at the source is higher than the pressure at the sink.

This pressure difference causes phloem sap to move by mass flow towards the sink.

**Step 6: Sucrose is unloaded at the sink**

Sucrose leaves the sieve tube and enters sink cells.

It may be:

- Used in respiration.
- Used for growth.
- Converted into other organic substances.
- Stored as starch.

**Step 7: Water leaves the phloem**

When sucrose is removed from the phloem:

- The solute concentration decreases.
- The water potential increases.
- Water may move out of the phloem by osmosis.
- Water can return to the xylem.

This helps maintain the pressure difference needed for continued mass flow.

### Simplified Flow

Source  
↓  
Sucrose enters phloem  
↓  
Water enters by osmosis  
↓  
Hydrostatic pressure increases  
↓  
Phloem sap moves towards sink  
↓  
Sucrose is unloaded  
↓  
Water may leave the phloem

## 4.3.6 Role of Companion Cells in Translocation

Companion cells are important for the functioning of sieve tube elements.

They:

1. Provide metabolic support.
2. Help load sucrose into sieve tubes.
3. Help unload sucrose at sinks in some tissues.
4. Supply energy for active transport.
5. Maintain the functioning of sieve tube elements.

The close association between companion cells and sieve tube elements allows efficient transport of organic substances.

## 4.3.7 Direction of Translocation

Translocation occurs from sources to sinks.

It is not restricted to one fixed direction throughout the entire plant.

For example:

- Sucrose may move downwards from leaves to roots.
- Sucrose may move upwards from storage organs to growing shoots.
- Sucrose may move towards developing fruits.

Different sieve tubes can transport substances in different directions at the same time.

**Important exam point:**

Xylem transport is mainly upward, while phloem transport follows source-to-sink relationships.

Do not state that all phloem transport is always downward.

## 4.3.8 Comparison Between Translocation and Transpiration

| Feature | Translocation | Transpiration |
|---|---|---|
| Main tissue involved | Phloem | Water movement through xylem is linked to water loss from leaves |
| Main substance involved | Organic substances, especially sucrose | Water vapour is lost from aerial plant surfaces |
| Main function | Distributes organic nutrients | Helps drive water transport and contributes to cooling |
| Direction | Source to sink | Water movement through xylem is mainly upwards |
| Main mechanism | Pressure-driven mass flow in phloem | Evaporation and diffusion create tension in xylem |

## 4.3.9 Importance of Translocation

Translocation is essential because not every plant cell can carry out photosynthesis.

Roots, developing fruits, flowers and many storage organs depend on organic substances transported from other parts of the plant.

Translocation allows plants to:

1. Supply sucrose for cellular respiration.
2. Support growth and development.
3. Supply nutrients to developing fruits and seeds.
4. Store carbohydrates in roots, stems and other organs.
5. Distribute organic substances between different regions.

Without translocation, non-photosynthetic tissues would not receive sufficient organic nutrients.

# 4.4 PHYTOREMEDIATION

## 4.4.1 Introduction to Phytoremediation

Phytoremediation is the use of plants and their associated microorganisms to remove, reduce, contain or transform pollutants in the environment.

The word can be understood as:

- Phyto: plant.
- Remediation: the process of making something less harmful or improving a polluted environment.

Phytoremediation is an environmentally friendly approach that can be used to manage certain types of pollution.

It can be applied to:

- Contaminated soil.
- Polluted water.
- Some contaminated sediments.
- Certain polluted industrial or agricultural sites.

Plants may absorb pollutants, accumulate them in tissues, stabilise them in the soil or support their breakdown.

## 4.4.2 Types of Pollutants That Can Be Managed

Examples include:

### A. Heavy Metals

Heavy metals are metallic elements that can become harmful when present at excessive concentrations.

Examples include:

- Lead.
- Cadmium.
- Mercury.
- Arsenic is a metalloid but is also an important environmental contaminant.

Some plants can absorb and accumulate certain metals in their tissues.

### B. Excess Nutrients

Excess nitrate and phosphate in water can contribute to eutrophication.

Certain plants can absorb these nutrients and help reduce their concentration in polluted water.

### C. Organic Pollutants

Some organic pollutants may be broken down or transformed by plant enzymes or microorganisms associated with plant roots.

Examples include certain petroleum-related compounds and some pesticides.

The effectiveness depends on the pollutant, plant species and environmental conditions.

## 4.4.3 Main Mechanisms of Phytoremediation

Different phytoremediation methods work in different ways.

### A. Phytoextraction

Phytoextraction occurs when plants absorb contaminants from the soil through their roots and accumulate them in their above-ground tissues.

The contaminated plant material can then be harvested and removed.

**Process:**

1. Plants are grown in contaminated soil.
2. Their roots absorb certain pollutants.
3. The pollutants are transported into plant tissues.
4. Some pollutants accumulate in stems and leaves.
5. The plants are harvested.
6. The contaminated biomass is managed safely.

**Importance:**

Phytoextraction can reduce the concentration of certain contaminants in soil.

**Limitation:**

The process may be slow, especially when contamination is deep or highly concentrated.

### B. Phytostabilisation

Phytostabilisation involves the use of plants to reduce the movement of pollutants in soil.

Plants may:

- Hold soil together with their roots.
- Reduce soil erosion.
- Reduce the movement of contaminated particles.
- Reduce the movement of some contaminants into surrounding water.

**Importance:**

It helps prevent pollutants from spreading to other areas.

**Limitation:**

It does not necessarily remove the pollutants from the site. The contaminants may remain in the soil.

### C. Rhizofiltration

Rhizofiltration uses plant roots to absorb or adsorb contaminants from polluted water.

Absorption means a substance enters the plant or root tissue.

Adsorption means a substance attaches to a surface.

**Process:**

1. Plant roots are placed in contact with polluted water.
2. Contaminants attach to root surfaces or enter root tissues.
3. Contaminants are removed from the water.
4. The plant material is collected and managed safely.

**Importance:**

It can be used to reduce certain contaminants in water.

**Limitation:**

The roots and plant material may become contaminated and require proper disposal or treatment.

### D. Phytodegradation

Phytodegradation occurs when plants take up certain organic pollutants and break them down through metabolic processes.

Plant enzymes may transform complex pollutants into simpler substances.

**Importance:**

It can help reduce certain organic contaminants.

**Limitation:**

Not all pollutants can be broken down by plants. Some breakdown products may still require further treatment.

### E. Rhizodegradation

Rhizodegradation involves microorganisms living around plant roots breaking down pollutants.

The roots release substances into the surrounding soil that can support microbial activity.

These microorganisms may use certain organic pollutants as sources of carbon or energy.

**Importance:**

It can help break down some organic contaminants in soil.

**Limitation:**

It depends on suitable microorganisms and environmental conditions such as oxygen, moisture and temperature.

## 4.4.4 Comparison of Phytoremediation Methods

| Method | Main mechanism | Main target or effect |
|---|---|---|
| Phytoextraction | Plants absorb and accumulate pollutants in harvestable tissues | Removes certain contaminants from soil |
| Phytostabilisation | Roots and plant activity reduce pollutant movement | Contains or stabilises contaminants |
| Rhizofiltration | Roots absorb or adsorb contaminants from water | Reduces contaminants in polluted water |
| Phytodegradation | Plant enzymes transform or break down pollutants | Reduces certain organic pollutants |
| Rhizodegradation | Root-associated microorganisms break down pollutants | Reduces certain organic pollutants in soil |

## 4.4.5 Advantages of Phytoremediation

**1. Environmentally friendly**

It uses plants and natural biological processes to manage pollution.

**2. Potentially lower cost**

For suitable sites, phytoremediation may cost less than some conventional physical or chemical treatment methods.

**3. Can reduce soil erosion**

Plant roots help bind soil particles together.

**4. Can improve the appearance of polluted areas**

Vegetation can restore plant cover to degraded sites.

**5. Can treat large areas**

Some phytoremediation approaches can be applied over relatively large areas of contaminated land.

**6. May require less disturbance**

Some methods can be carried out without removing large amounts of soil.

## 4.4.6 Limitations of Phytoremediation

**1. Slow process**

Plants may require months or years to reduce pollutant concentrations.

**2. Limited root depth**

Plants can only treat contaminants within the areas reached by their roots.

**3. Depends on plant tolerance**

Some pollutants are toxic to plants and may prevent their growth.

**4. Seasonal limitations**

Plant growth and pollutant uptake may change with seasons and environmental conditions.

**5. Contaminated plant material**

Plants that accumulate pollutants may need to be harvested and disposed of safely.

**6. Not suitable for every pollutant**

Some pollutants cannot be effectively absorbed, stabilised or broken down by plants.

**7. Risk of pollutants entering food chains**

If contaminated plants are eaten by animals or humans, pollutants may enter food chains.

Therefore, contaminated plants must be managed carefully.

## 4.4.7 Applications of Phytoremediation

Phytoremediation can be used in several situations.

### A. Contaminated Agricultural Soil

Plants may be used to remove or stabilise certain heavy metals in contaminated soil.

### B. Industrial Sites

Certain plants may help manage contamination at abandoned or polluted industrial locations.

### C. Polluted Water

Aquatic plants or plants grown in treatment systems may absorb excess nutrients or certain contaminants.

### D. Areas Affected by Mining

Plants may help stabilise contaminated soil and reduce erosion around mining sites.

## 4.4.8 Important Considerations in Phytoremediation

A successful phytoremediation project must consider:

1. The type of pollutant.
2. The concentration of the pollutant.
3. The depth of contamination.
4. The plant species selected.
5. The ability of the plant to tolerate the pollutant.
6. The local climate and soil conditions.
7. The safe management of contaminated plant material.
8. The possibility of pollutants entering food chains.

The correct plant must be selected according to the pollutant and the purpose of treatment.

**Important:**

Phytoremediation does not mean that plants can remove every type of pollution. Its effectiveness depends on the pollutant, plant species and environmental conditions.

# CHAPTER 4 — IMPORTANT COMPARISONS

## 1. Xylem vs Phloem

Xylem:

- Transports water and mineral salts.
- Mainly transports substances upwards.
- Contains dead conducting cells at maturity.
- Has lignified walls.
- Provides mechanical support.

Phloem:

- Transports organic substances.
- Moves substances from sources to sinks.
- Contains living sieve tube elements.
- Has sieve plates.
- Depends on companion cells for metabolic support.

## 2. Apoplast vs Symplast

Apoplast:

- Movement through cell walls and intercellular spaces.
- Does not cross the plasma membrane at every cell boundary.
- Blocked by the Casparian strip at the endodermis.

Symplast:

- Movement through cytoplasm.
- Cells are connected by plasmodesmata.
- Water enters the symplast through a plasma membrane.

## 3. Cohesion vs Adhesion

Cohesion:

- Attraction between molecules of the same substance.
- Water molecules attract one another.
- Helps maintain a continuous water column.

Adhesion:

- Attraction between molecules of different substances.
- Water is attracted to xylem walls.
- Helps water remain in contact with vessel walls.

## 4. Transpiration vs Guttation

Transpiration:

- Loss of water vapour.
- Mainly occurs through stomata.
- Associated with transpiration pull.

Guttation:

- Loss of liquid water droplets.
- Occurs through hydathodes.
- Often associated with root pressure.

## 5. Transpiration vs Translocation

Transpiration:

- Involves water loss from aerial plant parts.
- Water moves through xylem towards leaves.
- Helps generate transpiration pull.

Translocation:

- Involves transport of organic substances.
- Occurs through phloem.
- Moves substances from sources to sinks.

## 6. Phytoextraction vs Phytostabilisation

Phytoextraction:

- Plants absorb pollutants.
- Pollutants accumulate in plant tissues.
- Harvesting removes contaminated plant material from the site.

Phytostabilisation:

- Plants reduce pollutant movement.
- Roots help stabilise soil.
- Pollutants may remain at the site.

# COMMON EXAM MISTAKES

1. Do not confuse xylem with phloem.
2. Do not state that all phloem transport is downward.
3. Do not state that cohesion is the attraction between water and xylem walls. That is adhesion.
4. Do not confuse transpiration with translocation.
5. Do not say that a potometer directly measures transpiration. It estimates water uptake.
6. Do not confuse guttation with transpiration.
7. Do not say that root pressure is the main force responsible for water transport in tall plants.
8. Do not forget that mineral ions can be absorbed by active transport.
9. Do not confuse the apoplast pathway with the symplast pathway.
10. Do not forget the role of the Casparian strip.
11. Do not state that phytoextraction and phytostabilisation are the same process.
12. Do not claim that phytoremediation can remove every pollutant.

# SPM-STYLE PRACTICE QUESTIONS

## QUESTION 1

Differentiate between xylem and phloem.

**Suggested answer:**

1. Xylem transports water and mineral salts, whereas phloem transports organic substances such as sucrose.
2. Xylem transport is mainly upwards, whereas phloem transports substances from sources to sinks.
3. Xylem conducting cells are dead at maturity, whereas sieve tube elements are living but lack a nucleus at maturity.

## QUESTION 2

Explain how root hair cells are adapted for water absorption.

**Suggested answer:**

1. Root hair cells have long projections that provide a large surface area for absorption.
2. Their thin cell walls provide a short distance for water movement.
3. Their large vacuoles help maintain a lower water potential in the cell sap.
4. Water enters the root hair cells from the soil solution by osmosis.

## QUESTION 3

Explain why mineral ions are absorbed by active transport.

**Suggested answer:**

1. The concentration of some mineral ions in the soil solution may be lower than their concentration inside root cells.
2. The ions must move against their concentration gradient.
3. Active transport uses energy from ATP.
4. Transport proteins help move the ions across the plasma membrane.

## QUESTION 4

Describe the apoplast and symplast pathways.

**Suggested answer:**

1. The apoplast pathway involves water moving through cell walls and intercellular spaces.
2. The symplast pathway involves water moving through the cytoplasm of living cells.
3. Neighbouring cells in the symplast are connected by plasmodesmata.
4. The Casparian strip blocks the apoplast pathway at the endodermis.

## QUESTION 5

Explain the role of the Casparian strip in water movement through roots.

**Suggested answer:**

1. The Casparian strip contains the water-resistant substance suberin.
2. It blocks the uncontrolled movement of water through cell walls.
3. Water and dissolved substances must cross a plasma membrane before entering the vascular cylinder.
4. This allows selective control over the substances entering the xylem.

## QUESTION 6

Explain the cohesion-tension mechanism in the transport of water through xylem.

**Suggested answer:**

1. Water evaporates from mesophyll cell surfaces into the leaf air spaces.
2. Water vapour diffuses out through the stomata during transpiration.
3. Water loss reduces the water potential of leaf tissues.
4. Water is drawn from the xylem into the leaf tissues.
5. Tension develops in the xylem and pulls the continuous water column upwards.
6. Cohesion between water molecules helps maintain the continuous water column.

## QUESTION 7

Differentiate between cohesion and adhesion.

**Suggested answer:**

1. Cohesion is the attraction between molecules of the same substance.
2. Adhesion is the attraction between molecules of different substances.
3. Cohesion helps maintain a continuous water column, whereas adhesion helps water remain attached to xylem walls.

## QUESTION 8

Explain why an increase in humidity generally reduces the rate of transpiration.

**Suggested answer:**

1. High humidity means that the surrounding air contains more water vapour.
2. The water vapour concentration gradient between the leaf and atmosphere becomes smaller.
3. Diffusion of water vapour out of the leaf becomes slower.
4. Therefore, the rate of transpiration decreases.

## QUESTION 9

A student uses a potometer to investigate the effect of wind speed on water uptake.

State two variables that should be kept constant.

**Suggested answer:**

Any two:

1. Light intensity.
2. Temperature.
3. Humidity.
4. Leaf area.
5. Type and size of plant shoot.

## QUESTION 10

Explain the pressure flow hypothesis of translocation.

**Suggested answer:**

1. Sucrose is produced at the source and loaded into the phloem.
2. The sucrose concentration in the sieve tube increases.
3. Water enters the sieve tube from the xylem by osmosis.
4. Hydrostatic pressure increases at the source.
5. Phloem sap moves towards the sink due to the pressure difference.
6. Sucrose is unloaded at the sink and used or stored.

## QUESTION 11

Explain why phloem transport can occur in different directions in a plant.

**Suggested answer:**

1. Phloem transport occurs from sources to sinks.
2. Sources and sinks can be located in different parts of the plant.
3. Different sieve tubes can transport substances towards different sinks.
4. Therefore, phloem transport is not restricted to one fixed direction.

## QUESTION 12

Differentiate between phytoextraction and phytostabilisation.

**Suggested answer:**

1. Phytoextraction involves plants absorbing pollutants and accumulating them in their tissues.
2. Contaminated plant material is harvested and removed during phytoextraction.
3. Phytostabilisation uses plants to reduce the movement of pollutants in soil.
4. Pollutants may remain at the site during phytostabilisation.

## QUESTION 13

Explain two advantages and two limitations of phytoremediation.

**Suggested answer:**

Advantages:

1. It can be environmentally friendly because it uses plants and biological processes.
2. It may be less costly than some conventional treatment methods.

Limitations:

1. It can be slow and may take months or years.
2. It is limited by root depth and the ability of plants to tolerate pollutants.

## QUESTION 14

A plant is grown in soil contaminated with a heavy metal. The metal accumulates in its leaves and stems. The plant is then harvested.

Identify the phytoremediation method and explain how it works.

**Suggested answer:**

1. The method is phytoextraction.
2. The plant absorbs the heavy metal through its roots.
3. The metal is transported and accumulates in above-ground tissues.
4. The contaminated plant material is harvested and managed safely.

## QUESTION 15

A plant is used to prevent contaminated soil from being washed into a nearby river.

Identify the phytoremediation method and explain its function.

**Suggested answer:**

1. The method is phytostabilisation.
2. Plant roots hold soil particles together.
3. This reduces soil erosion and the movement of contaminated particles.
4. Therefore, the spread of pollutants into nearby water can be reduced.

# CHAPTER 4 — FINAL REVISION SUMMARY

## 4.1 VASCULAR TISSUES

- Vascular tissues transport substances throughout plants.
- Xylem transports water and mineral salts.
- Phloem transports organic substances.
- Xylem contains vessel elements, tracheids, fibres and parenchyma.
- Phloem contains sieve tube elements, companion cells, fibres and parenchyma.
- Xylem walls are strengthened by lignin.
- Sieve plates connect neighbouring sieve tube elements.

## 4.2 TRANSPORT OF WATER AND MINERAL SALTS

- Root hair cells absorb water and mineral ions.
- Water enters mainly by osmosis.
- Mineral ions may enter by diffusion or active transport.
- Water moves through apoplast and symplast pathways.
- The Casparian strip controls entry into the vascular cylinder.
- Water enters the xylem and moves towards the leaves.
- Transpiration pull is the main driving force for water transport in tall plants.
- Cohesion maintains a continuous water column.
- Adhesion attracts water to xylem walls.
- Humidity, temperature, light intensity and wind speed affect transpiration.
- A potometer estimates water uptake.

## 4.3 TRANSLOCATION

- Translocation is the movement of organic substances through phloem.
- Sucrose is the main transported sugar.
- A source supplies organic substances.
- A sink uses or stores organic substances.
- The pressure flow hypothesis explains mass flow through phloem.
- Sucrose loading lowers water potential in the phloem.
- Water enters by osmosis and increases hydrostatic pressure.
- Phloem sap moves towards a sink.
- Different sieve tubes can transport substances in different directions.

## 4.4 PHYTOREMEDIATION

- Phytoremediation uses plants and associated microorganisms to manage pollutants.
- Phytoextraction removes certain pollutants through plant uptake and harvesting.
- Phytostabilisation reduces pollutant movement.
- Rhizofiltration uses roots to absorb or adsorb contaminants from water.
- Phytodegradation uses plant processes to transform pollutants.
- Rhizodegradation involves microorganisms around roots breaking down pollutants.
- Phytoremediation can be environmentally friendly but may be slow.
- Contaminated plant material must be managed safely.
