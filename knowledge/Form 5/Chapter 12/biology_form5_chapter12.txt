# 12.1 TYPES AND FACTORS OF VARIATION

## 12.1.1 INTRODUCTION TO VARIATION

Variation refers to the differences in characteristics among individuals of the same species.

Although individuals belong to the same species, they may differ in physical characteristics, physiological characteristics and genetic information.

Examples of variation in humans include:
- Blood group.
- Ability to roll the tongue.
- Fingerprint patterns.
- Height.
- Skin pigmentation.
- Body mass.
- Natural hair texture.

Variation occurs because individuals have different genetic combinations and may experience different environmental influences.

**Importance of variation:**
1. Produces differences among individuals in a population.
2. Provides the raw material for natural selection.
3. Helps populations adapt to changing environmental conditions over generations.
4. Contributes to biodiversity.

## 12.1.2 TYPES OF VARIATION

Variation can be divided into two main types:

1. Continuous variation.
2. Discontinuous variation.

### A. CONTINUOUS VARIATION

Continuous variation refers to characteristics that show a continuous range of values between two extremes.

There are no clearly separated categories.

Individuals can have many possible values for a particular characteristic.

**Examples:**
- Height.
- Body mass.
- Hand span.
- Length of leaves.
- Skin pigmentation.

**Characteristics:**
1. Shows a continuous range of values.
2. Usually influenced by several genes (polygenic inheritance).
3. Often influenced by environmental factors.
4. Can be measured quantitatively.
5. Usually produces a bell-shaped distribution in a sufficiently large population under many common conditions.

**Example: Human height**

People do not normally belong to only two height categories. Instead, height varies across a range of measurements.

**Factors affecting human height:**
- Genes inherited from parents.
- Nutrition during growth.
- General health.
- Hormonal conditions.
- Environmental influences.

### B. DISCONTINUOUS VARIATION

Discontinuous variation refers to characteristics that can be divided into distinct categories.

There are no intermediate categories between the recognised forms of the characteristic.

**Examples:**
- ABO blood groups: A, B, AB and O.
- Biological sex as commonly classified in basic human genetics.
- Certain inherited conditions, such as the presence or absence of a particular inherited trait.

**Characteristics:**
1. Individuals belong to distinct categories.
2. Often controlled by one gene or a small number of genes.
3. Usually less influenced by environmental factors than continuous traits.
4. Can be classified by observation or testing rather than measured across a continuous scale.

**Example: ABO blood groups**

A person's ABO blood group is classified as A, B, AB or O. There are four distinct categories.

IMPORTANT:
Not every discontinuous characteristic is controlled by a single gene. Some characteristics have more complex genetic causes.

## 12.1.3 COMPARISON BETWEEN CONTINUOUS AND DISCONTINUOUS VARIATION

| Continuous variation | Discontinuous variation |
|---|---|
| Shows a continuous range | Shows distinct categories |
| Many possible values | Limited recognised categories |
| Usually polygenic | Often controlled by one or a few genes |
| Frequently influenced by environment | Often less influenced by environment |
| Can be measured | Can be classified |
| Example: height | Example: ABO blood group |

## 12.1.4 FACTORS CAUSING VARIATION

Variation is caused mainly by genetic factors and environmental factors.

### A. GENETIC FACTORS

Genetic variation occurs because individuals inherit different combinations of alleles.

Important processes include:

**1. Crossing over**

Crossing over occurs during prophase I of meiosis.

Homologous chromosomes pair up, and corresponding sections of non-sister chromatids may be exchanged.

This produces chromosomes with new combinations of alleles.

**Result:** Gametes may contain new combinations of genetic information.

**2. Independent assortment**

During meiosis I, homologous chromosome pairs arrange independently of one another at the equator.

The maternal and paternal chromosomes are distributed into daughter cells in different combinations.

**Result:** Different gametes receive different combinations of chromosomes.

**3. Random fertilisation**

Fertilisation occurs when one sperm fuses with one ovum.

Because many genetically different gametes may be produced, the combination of sperm and ovum is largely random.

**Result:** Each offspring may inherit a different combination of alleles.

**4. Mutation**

Mutation is a change in the genetic material.

It can produce new alleles and contribute to genetic variation.

Mutation is explained in greater detail in Topic 12.3.

### B. ENVIRONMENTAL FACTORS

Environmental factors can influence the characteristics shown by an organism.

Examples:

**1. Nutrition**

Adequate nutrition supports normal growth and development.

Poor nutrition during childhood may affect growth and development.

**2. Physical activity**

Physical activity can influence muscle development and physical fitness.

**3. Sunlight**

Exposure to ultraviolet radiation can influence melanin production and skin pigmentation.

**4. Climate and temperature**

Environmental temperature can affect the development or appearance of certain organisms.

**5. Disease and health**

Illness and health conditions may affect growth, development and body function.

## 12.1.5 GENETIC AND ENVIRONMENTAL INTERACTIONS

Many characteristics are influenced by both genes and the environment.

For example, human height is influenced by inherited genes as well as nutrition, health and other environmental conditions.

A useful way to understand this is:

**Observed phenotype = genetic influences + environmental influences + interactions between them**

The phenotype is the observable characteristic of an organism.

The genotype refers to its genetic constitution.

IMPORTANT:
The environment does not normally change the inherited DNA sequence simply because a characteristic changes. Environmental influences may alter how genes are expressed or affect development.

## 12.1.6 IMPORTANCE OF VARIATION

Variation is important because:

1. It produces differences among individuals in a population.
2. It provides differences that natural selection can act upon.
3. Individuals with advantageous inherited characteristics may have greater reproductive success in particular environments.
4. Over many generations, populations may become better adapted to their environments.
5. Variation contributes to the survival and evolution of species.

**EXAM TIP**

Question: Explain why variation is important for the survival of a species.

Suggested answer:
1. Variation produces different characteristics among individuals in a population.
2. Some individuals may possess inherited characteristics that are advantageous in a particular environment.
3. These individuals may have greater chances of surviving and reproducing.
4. Advantageous alleles may become more common over generations.

# 12.2 VARIATION IN HUMANS

## 12.2.1 INTRODUCTION

Humans show both continuous and discontinuous variation.

These differences arise from inherited genetic information, environmental influences and interactions between the two.

Examples include:
- Height.
- Body mass.
- Blood groups.
- Skin pigmentation.
- Fingerprint patterns.
- Some inherited conditions.

## 12.2.2 CONTINUOUS VARIATION IN HUMANS

Continuous variation shows a range of values.

Examples include:

### A. HEIGHT

Human height differs among individuals.

It is influenced by:
- Multiple genes.
- Nutrition.
- Hormones.
- General health.
- Environmental conditions during development.

Height is an example of a polygenic characteristic because many genes contribute to its variation.

### B. BODY MASS

Body mass varies among individuals.

Factors include:
- Genetic influences.
- Food intake.
- Physical activity.
- Metabolism.
- Health conditions.
- Age and development.

Body mass is not determined by genes alone.

### C. SKIN PIGMENTATION

Skin pigmentation varies across a range.

Melanin is the pigment that contributes to skin colour.

The amount and type of melanin are influenced by genetic factors. Ultraviolet exposure can also influence melanin production.

## 12.2.3 DISCONTINUOUS VARIATION IN HUMANS

Discontinuous variation involves distinct categories.

Examples include:
- ABO blood groups.
- Rh blood group classification.
- Certain inherited genetic characteristics.

### A. ABO BLOOD GROUP SYSTEM

The ABO blood group system is determined by three main alleles:

- Iᴬ
- Iᴮ
- i

Iᴬ and Iᴮ are codominant.

Both are dominant over i.

This means:
- IᴬIᴬ or Iᴬi produces blood group A.
- IᴮIᴮ or Iᴮi produces blood group B.
- IᴬIᴮ produces blood group AB.
- ii produces blood group O.

**ABO blood group table**

| Genotype | Phenotype |
|---|---|
| IᴬIᴬ | A |
| Iᴬi | A |
| IᴮIᴮ | B |
| Iᴮi | B |
| IᴬIᴮ | AB |
| ii | O |

### B. CODOMINANCE

Codominance occurs when both alleles are fully expressed in a heterozygous individual.

In blood group AB:
- The Iᴬ allele produces A antigen.
- The Iᴮ allele produces B antigen.
- Both antigens are present on red blood cells.

Therefore, neither allele masks the other.

### C. RH BLOOD GROUP SYSTEM

The Rh blood group system is another important blood group system.

In basic human genetics, individuals are commonly classified as:
- Rh positive (Rh⁺).
- Rh negative (Rh⁻).

Rh status depends mainly on whether the D antigen is present on red blood cells.

Rh compatibility is important in blood transfusions and pregnancy.

IMPORTANT:
The ABO and Rh systems are separate blood group systems. A person may be A positive, O negative, AB positive and so on.

## 12.2.4 HUMAN BLOOD GROUP INHERITANCE

Blood group inheritance can be studied using genetic crosses.

**Example 1: A heterozygous parent with blood group A and a heterozygous parent with blood group B**

Parents:

Iᴬi × Iᴮi

Possible gametes:
- First parent: Iᴬ or i.
- Second parent: Iᴮ or i.

Punnett square:

| | Iᴮ | i |
|---|---|---|
| Iᴬ | IᴬIᴮ (AB) | Iᴬi (A) |
| i | Iᴮi (B) | ii (O) |

Possible offspring:
- 25% blood group AB.
- 25% blood group A.
- 25% blood group B.
- 25% blood group O.

Each outcome has an equal probability in this theoretical cross.

IMPORTANT:
Probability does not guarantee that a small family will have exactly one child of each blood group.

## 12.2.5 FINGERPRINT PATTERNS

Fingerprints are examples of human variation.

Common fingerprint patterns include:

1. Arch.
2. Loop.
3. Whorl.

**Arch**
- Ridges enter from one side and leave from the other.
- Usually forms a raised central pattern.

**Loop**
- Ridges enter from one side, curve and leave from the same side.
- Has a loop-shaped appearance.

**Whorl**
- Ridges form circular or spiral-like patterns.

Fingerprint patterns are influenced by genetic and developmental factors.

The exact ridge details are highly individual.

## 12.2.6 IMPORTANCE OF STUDYING HUMAN VARIATION

1. Helps us understand genetic inheritance.
2. Helps explain why individuals differ.
3. Supports the study of inherited disorders.
4. Helps in genetic counselling.
5. Supports medical investigations and blood compatibility testing.
6. Provides information about human genetic diversity.

## 12.2.7 EXAM TIPS

Question: Differentiate between continuous and discontinuous variation in humans.

Suggested answer:
1. Continuous variation shows a continuous range of values, whereas discontinuous variation shows distinct categories.
2. Height is an example of continuous variation, whereas ABO blood group is an example of discontinuous variation.

Question: Explain why height is an example of continuous variation.

Suggested answer:
1. Height shows a wide range of measurements.
2. It is influenced by many genes.
3. Environmental factors such as nutrition also influence growth.
4. Therefore, individuals show many possible height values.

Question: Explain why blood group AB is an example of codominance.

Suggested answer:
1. Blood group AB has genotype IᴬIᴮ.
2. Both alleles are expressed.
3. Both A and B antigens are present on red blood cells.

# 12.3 MUTATION

## 12.3.1 INTRODUCTION TO MUTATION

Mutation is a sudden change in the genetic material of an organism.

The genetic material may be DNA or, in some viruses, RNA.

In organisms with DNA-based chromosomes, mutations can affect:
- A single gene.
- A section of a chromosome.
- The structure of a chromosome.
- The number of chromosomes.

Mutation is an important source of new genetic variation.

Some mutations have no noticeable effect, some are harmful, and some may be beneficial in a particular environment.

IMPORTANT:
A mutation is not automatically harmful. Its effect depends on the genetic change, the gene involved, the cell affected and the environment.

## 12.3.2 TYPES OF MUTATION

Mutations can be broadly classified into:

1. Gene mutation.
2. Chromosomal mutation.

### A. GENE MUTATION

Gene mutation refers to a change in the nucleotide sequence of a gene.

A nucleotide sequence is the order of DNA bases:
- Adenine (A).
- Thymine (T).
- Cytosine (C).
- Guanine (G).

A change in the sequence may alter the information used to produce a protein.

Important types of gene mutation include:

**1. Substitution**

Substitution occurs when one nucleotide or base pair is replaced by another.

Example:

Original DNA sequence:

A T G C C A

Mutated DNA sequence:

A T A C C A

The third base has changed from G to A.

Possible effects:
- The codon may specify a different amino acid.
- The codon may become a stop codon.
- The new codon may still specify the same amino acid.

Therefore, not every substitution changes the protein sequence.

**2. Insertion**

Insertion occurs when one or more nucleotides are added into a DNA sequence.

Example:

Original sequence:

A T G C C A

After insertion:

A T G A C C A

If the number of inserted nucleotides is not a multiple of three in a protein-coding sequence, it may cause a frameshift.

**3. Deletion**

Deletion occurs when one or more nucleotides are removed from a DNA sequence.

Example:

Original sequence:

A T G C C A

After deletion:

A T C C A

If the number of deleted nucleotides is not a multiple of three in a protein-coding sequence, it may cause a frameshift.

### B. FRAMESHIFT MUTATION

A frameshift mutation occurs when insertion or deletion changes the grouping of nucleotides into codons.

During translation, mRNA is read in groups of three bases called codons.

A change in the reading frame may alter many amino acids after the mutation.

This can produce a significantly altered protein.

IMPORTANT:
Insertion or deletion of three nucleotides, or a multiple of three, does not shift the reading frame, although it may still add or remove amino acids and affect protein function.

## 12.3.3 CHROMOSOMAL MUTATION

Chromosomal mutations involve changes in chromosome structure or chromosome number.

They may affect large sections of genetic material or entire chromosomes.

### A. STRUCTURAL CHROMOSOMAL MUTATIONS

Important structural changes include:

**1. Deletion**

A section of a chromosome is lost.

Possible effect:
- Genes in the deleted region may be lost.

**2. Duplication**

A section of a chromosome is repeated.

Possible effect:
- Extra copies of genes may be present.

**3. Inversion**

A chromosome segment breaks off, reverses its orientation and rejoins.

Possible effect:
- Gene arrangement changes.
- Normal gene function or reproductive processes may be affected in some cases.

**4. Translocation**

A chromosome segment moves to a different chromosome or joins a non-homologous chromosome.

Possible effect:
- Genes may be disrupted or their regulation may change.

### B. NUMERICAL CHROMOSOMAL MUTATIONS

Numerical chromosome changes involve an abnormal number of chromosomes.

One important cause is nondisjunction.

**Nondisjunction**

Nondisjunction occurs when chromosomes or sister chromatids fail to separate properly during cell division.

It may occur during:
- Meiosis I, when homologous chromosomes fail to separate.
- Meiosis II, when sister chromatids fail to separate.

This can produce gametes with an abnormal number of chromosomes.

If such a gamete is involved in fertilisation, the resulting zygote may have an abnormal chromosome number.

## 12.3.4 ANEUPLOIDY

Aneuploidy is a condition in which a cell has an abnormal number of individual chromosomes.

It commonly results from nondisjunction.

Examples:

**1. Trisomy**

Trisomy occurs when an individual has one extra copy of a particular chromosome.

The chromosome number is written as 2n + 1.

Example: Down syndrome.

Down syndrome is usually caused by trisomy 21, where there are three copies of chromosome 21.

A person with the usual form of trisomy 21 has 47 chromosomes.

**2. Monosomy**

Monosomy occurs when an individual has only one copy of a particular chromosome instead of the usual pair.

The chromosome number is written as 2n − 1.

Example: Turner syndrome.

Turner syndrome commonly involves one X chromosome and is represented as 45,X.

IMPORTANT:
Not all cases of Down syndrome result from ordinary nondisjunction. Some cases involve translocation or mosaicism.

## 12.3.5 CAUSES OF MUTATION

Mutations may occur spontaneously or be caused by mutagens.

### A. SPONTANEOUS MUTATION

Spontaneous mutations occur naturally without deliberate exposure to an external mutagen.

Possible causes include:
- Errors during DNA replication.
- Natural chemical changes in DNA.
- Errors during DNA repair.

Cells have DNA repair mechanisms that correct many errors. However, some changes may remain and become permanent mutations.

### B. INDUCED MUTATION

Induced mutations occur following exposure to mutagens.

A mutagen is an agent that increases the likelihood of mutation.

Mutagens include:

**1. Physical mutagens**

Examples:
- Ultraviolet (UV) radiation.
- X-rays.
- Gamma radiation.

These forms of radiation can damage DNA.

**2. Chemical mutagens**

Certain chemicals can alter DNA bases or interfere with DNA replication.

Examples include:
- Some chemicals found in tobacco smoke.
- Certain industrial chemicals.
- Some laboratory mutagenic chemicals.

**3. Biological agents**

Some biological agents, including certain viruses, can contribute to genetic changes or disrupt normal genetic regulation.

IMPORTANT:
Exposure to a mutagen does not mean that a mutation or disease will definitely occur. Risk depends on the agent, exposure level, duration and biological factors.

## 12.3.6 EFFECTS OF MUTATION

Mutations can have different effects.

### A. NEUTRAL MUTATION

A neutral mutation has no significant effect on the organism's observable characteristics or biological function under the conditions considered.

Possible reasons:
- The genetic code is degenerate, so a changed codon may still code for the same amino acid.
- The change occurs in a region that does not significantly affect gene function.
- The change does not substantially alter the resulting protein.

### B. HARMFUL MUTATION

A harmful mutation reduces normal biological function or may increase the risk of disease.

Examples include mutations associated with:
- Sickle-cell disease.
- Some forms of inherited cancer susceptibility.
- Certain inherited metabolic disorders.

### C. BENEFICIAL MUTATION

A beneficial mutation provides an advantage in a particular environment.

It may increase an organism's chance of survival or reproduction.

For example, a genetic change may provide resistance to a particular disease or improve survival under a specific environmental condition.

IMPORTANT:
A mutation that is beneficial in one environment may not provide the same advantage in another environment.

## 12.3.7 EXAMPLES OF HUMAN GENETIC CONDITIONS ASSOCIATED WITH MUTATION

### A. SICKLE-CELL DISEASE

Sickle-cell disease is an inherited condition involving mutations in the HBB gene, which provides instructions for producing part of haemoglobin.

In the common form:
- A substitution mutation changes one DNA base.
- The beta-globin protein contains an altered amino acid.
- Haemoglobin S is produced.

Under low-oxygen conditions, haemoglobin S molecules can stick together and form long fibres.

This causes red blood cells to become rigid and sickle-shaped.

Possible effects include:
- Reduced flexibility of red blood cells.
- Blockage of small blood vessels.
- Reduced oxygen delivery to tissues.
- Episodes of pain and other complications.
- Increased breakdown of red blood cells, causing anaemia.

IMPORTANT:
Sickle-cell disease is not the same as sickle-cell trait. People with sickle-cell trait usually inherit one typical and one sickle-cell allele and generally do not have the same disease pattern.

### B. THALASSAEMIA

Thalassaemia is a group of inherited blood disorders involving reduced or absent production of one or more globin chains of haemoglobin.

It can result from different mutations affecting globin genes.

Possible effects:
- Reduced haemoglobin production.
- Anaemia.
- Tiredness.
- In more severe forms, complications requiring regular medical care.

### C. COLOUR VISION DEFICIENCY

Some forms of red-green colour vision deficiency are inherited and associated with changes in genes involved in producing light-sensitive pigments in cone cells.

Possible effects:
- Difficulty distinguishing certain shades of red and green.
- Difficulty identifying some colour combinations.

### D. DOWN SYNDROME

Down syndrome is usually caused by trisomy 21.

It is a chromosomal number abnormality rather than a simple single-gene mutation.

It can affect:
- Physical development.
- Learning and intellectual development.
- Certain aspects of health.

The characteristics and support needs of individuals vary.

IMPORTANT:
Down syndrome is included here to show how chromosomal abnormalities can affect human variation. It should not be confused with a single-gene substitution mutation.

## 12.3.8 MUTATION AND CANCER

Cancer develops when cells acquire changes that disrupt normal control of cell division and other cellular processes.

Mutations in certain genes can contribute to cancer.

Examples of important gene groups:

**1. Proto-oncogenes**

These genes normally help regulate cell growth and division.

Certain mutations can convert a proto-oncogene into an oncogene, which may promote uncontrolled cell growth.

**2. Tumour suppressor genes**

These genes normally help control cell division, repair DNA damage or trigger cell death when necessary.

Mutations that reduce their function may allow abnormal cells to survive and multiply.

**3. DNA repair genes**

These genes help detect and repair DNA damage.

When their function is reduced, mutations may accumulate more easily.

IMPORTANT:
Cancer usually develops through the accumulation of several genetic and cellular changes. One mutation does not automatically cause cancer.

## 12.3.9 SOMATIC AND GERMLINE MUTATIONS

Mutations can be classified according to the cells in which they occur.

### A. SOMATIC MUTATION

A somatic mutation occurs in a body cell that is not involved in producing gametes.

Characteristics:
- Occurs in cells such as skin or liver cells.
- May affect the individual.
- Can contribute to cancer.
- Is generally not passed to offspring through sexual reproduction.

### B. GERMLINE MUTATION

A germline mutation occurs in cells that give rise to gametes or in the gametes themselves.

Characteristics:
- Can be passed from parents to offspring.
- May become present in the offspring's cells.
- Can contribute to inherited genetic variation.

**Comparison**

| Somatic mutation | Germline mutation |
|---|---|
| Occurs in body cells | Occurs in germline cells or gametes |
| Usually affects the individual | Can be inherited by offspring |
| Usually not passed through sexual reproduction | May be transmitted to offspring |
| Can contribute to cancer | Can cause inherited genetic conditions |

## 12.3.10 MUTATION AND EVOLUTION

Mutation is an important source of new alleles.

When mutations occur in germline cells, they may be passed to offspring.

Natural selection acts on inherited variation.

If a mutation gives an organism a reproductive advantage in a particular environment, the associated allele may become more common over generations.

However:
- Mutations do not occur because organisms need them.
- Mutations are not always beneficial.
- Natural selection does not deliberately create mutations.
- A mutation may be neutral, harmful or beneficial depending on the circumstances.

## 12.3.11 DIFFERENCES BETWEEN GENE AND CHROMOSOMAL MUTATIONS

| Gene mutation | Chromosomal mutation |
|---|---|
| Changes the nucleotide sequence of a gene | Changes chromosome structure or number |
| May affect one or a few bases | May affect large chromosome regions or whole chromosomes |
| Includes substitution, insertion and deletion | Includes structural changes and numerical changes |
| May alter the amino acid sequence of a protein | May change the number or arrangement of many genes |

## 12.3.12 DIFFERENCES BETWEEN MUTATION AND VARIATION

| Mutation | Variation |
|---|---|
| A change in genetic material | Differences among individuals |
| Can produce new alleles | Can result from genetic and environmental factors |
| May be neutral, harmful or beneficial | Describes differences in characteristics |
| Is one source of genetic variation | Is a broader biological concept |

## 12.3.13 IMPORTANT EXAM MISCONCEPTIONS

1. Mutation is not always harmful.
2. Mutation is a source of new alleles.
3. Crossing over and independent assortment create new combinations of existing alleles; they do not normally create new alleles themselves.
4. Environmental variation does not necessarily involve a change in DNA sequence.
5. Insertion or deletion may cause a frameshift if the number of bases added or removed is not a multiple of three.
6. Nondisjunction causes abnormal chromosome separation.
7. Down syndrome is usually caused by trisomy 21.
8. Not all chromosomal abnormalities are inherited from a parent; some occur during gamete formation or early development.
9. Somatic mutations are generally not passed to offspring through sexual reproduction.
10. Germline mutations can be inherited.
11. A mutation does not automatically cause cancer.
12. A beneficial mutation provides an advantage only in a particular context or environment.

## 12.3.14 EXAM TIPS

**Question: Define mutation.**

Suggested answer:

Mutation is a sudden change in the genetic material of an organism.

**Question: Explain how crossing over produces genetic variation.**

Suggested answer:
1. Crossing over occurs during prophase I of meiosis.
2. Homologous chromosomes pair up.
3. Non-sister chromatids exchange corresponding DNA segments.
4. New combinations of alleles are produced in the chromosomes.

**Question: Explain how nondisjunction can cause Down syndrome.**

Suggested answer:
1. Nondisjunction occurs when chromosomes fail to separate properly during meiosis.
2. A gamete may receive an extra copy of chromosome 21.
3. The gamete fuses with a normal gamete during fertilisation.
4. The resulting zygote has three copies of chromosome 21.

**Question: Differentiate between substitution and insertion mutations.**

Suggested answer:
1. Substitution occurs when a nucleotide is replaced by another nucleotide.
2. Insertion occurs when one or more nucleotides are added to the DNA sequence.

**Question: Explain how a frameshift mutation affects protein synthesis.**

Suggested answer:
1. Insertion or deletion changes the nucleotide sequence.
2. The grouping of bases into codons may shift.
3. Different codons may be read during translation.
4. The amino acid sequence may change, producing an altered protein.

**Question: Explain how mutation contributes to evolution.**

Suggested answer:
1. Mutation can produce new alleles.
2. Some mutations may be inherited by offspring.
3. Natural selection acts on inherited variations.
4. Advantageous alleles may become more common over generations.

## QUESTION 14

Describe how mutations can contribute to cancer.

**Suggested answer:**
1. Mutations may affect genes that regulate cell division.
2. Mutations may activate genes that promote cell growth.
3. Mutations may reduce the function of tumour suppressor genes.
4. Abnormal cells may divide uncontrollably and form a tumour.

## QUESTION 15

Compare gene mutation and chromosomal mutation.

**Suggested answer:**
1. Gene mutation changes the nucleotide sequence of a gene.
2. Chromosomal mutation changes chromosome structure or number.
3. Gene mutation may affect a small DNA sequence, whereas chromosomal mutation may affect a large region or an entire chromosome.

# 12.5 KEY TERMS

**Variation**
- Differences among individuals of the same species.

**Continuous variation**
- Variation showing a continuous range of values.

**Discontinuous variation**
- Variation showing distinct categories.

**Polygenic inheritance**
- Inheritance in which several genes contribute to a characteristic.

**Crossing over**
- Exchange of DNA segments between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes.

**Independent assortment**
- Random orientation and distribution of homologous chromosome pairs during meiosis.

**Mutation**
- A change in genetic material.

**Gene mutation**
- A change in the nucleotide sequence of a gene.

**Substitution**
- Replacement of a nucleotide with another nucleotide.

**Insertion**
- Addition of one or more nucleotides.

**Deletion**
- Removal of one or more nucleotides.

**Frameshift mutation**
- A mutation that changes the reading frame of codons.

**Chromosomal mutation**
- A change in chromosome structure or number.

**Nondisjunction**
- Failure of chromosomes or sister chromatids to separate properly during cell division.

**Trisomy**
- Presence of three copies of a particular chromosome.

**Monosomy**
- Presence of only one copy of a particular chromosome.

**Mutagen**
- An agent that increases the likelihood of mutation.

**Somatic mutation**
- Mutation occurring in a body cell.

**Germline mutation**
- Mutation occurring in cells that give rise to gametes or in gametes.

**Codominance**
- A pattern of inheritance in which both alleles are fully expressed in a heterozygote.

# 12.6 FINAL REVISION SUMMARY

## 12.1 TYPES AND FACTORS OF VARIATION

- Variation means differences among individuals of the same species.
- Continuous variation shows a range of values.
- Discontinuous variation shows distinct categories.
- Genetic factors include crossing over, independent assortment, random fertilisation and mutation.
- Environmental factors include nutrition, sunlight, physical activity, climate and health.
- Variation provides the raw material for natural selection.

## 12.2 VARIATION IN HUMANS

- Humans show continuous and discontinuous variation.
- Height and body mass are examples of continuous variation.
- ABO blood group is an example of discontinuous variation.
- ABO blood groups involve Iᴬ, Iᴮ and i alleles.
- Iᴬ and Iᴮ are codominant.
- Fingerprint patterns include arch, loop and whorl.
- Human characteristics may be influenced by genes and environmental factors.

## 12.3 MUTATION

- Mutation is a change in genetic material.
- Gene mutations include substitution, insertion and deletion.
- Frameshift mutations may occur when insertion or deletion changes the reading frame.
- Chromosomal mutations may affect chromosome structure or number.
- Nondisjunction can cause an abnormal chromosome number.
- Down syndrome is usually associated with trisomy 21.
- Mutagens include radiation and certain chemicals.
- Mutations may be neutral, harmful or beneficial.
- Somatic mutations generally are not passed to offspring through sexual reproduction.
- Germline mutations may be inherited.
- Mutation introduces new alleles and contributes to genetic variation and evolution.
