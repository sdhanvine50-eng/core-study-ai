# 9.1 COMMUNITY AND ECOSYSTEM

## 9.1.1 Introduction to Ecology

Ecology is the branch of biology that studies the interactions between organisms and their environment.

It examines how organisms interact with:

- Other organisms.
- Physical environmental factors.
- Chemical environmental factors.

Examples of ecological studies include:

- How plants compete for sunlight.
- How predators affect prey populations.
- How rainfall affects plant growth.
- How decomposers recycle nutrients.
- How human activities change ecosystems.

## 9.1.2 Levels of Ecological Organisation

Ecological organisation can be arranged from the smallest level to the largest level.

**Organism → Population → Community → Ecosystem → Biome → Biosphere**

### 1. Organism

An organism is an individual living thing.

Examples:

- One grass plant.
- One frog.
- One tiger.
- One bacterium.

### 2. Population

A population is a group of organisms of the same species living in the same geographical area at a particular time.

Example:

All frogs of the same species living in a particular pond.

### 3. Community

A community consists of populations of different species living and interacting in the same area.

Example:

A pond community may contain:

- Fish.
- Frogs.
- Algae.
- Aquatic insects.
- Snails.
- Bacteria.

### 4. Ecosystem

An ecosystem consists of a community of organisms interacting with one another and with the non-living components of their environment.

Example:

A pond ecosystem contains living organisms, water, sunlight, dissolved minerals, temperature and other environmental factors.

### 5. Biome

A biome is a large geographical region characterised by its climate and typical vegetation and animal communities.

Examples:

- Tropical rainforest.
- Desert.
- Grassland.
- Tundra.

### 6. Biosphere

The biosphere is the part of Earth where life exists.

It includes all ecosystems and living organisms.

### Important Comparison

| Level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Organism | One individual living thing |
| Population | Individuals of the same species in an area |
| Community | Different populations interacting |
| Ecosystem | Community interacting with the physical environment |
| Biome | Large region with characteristic climate and organisms |
| Biosphere | All regions of Earth where life exists |

**Exam tip:**

A population contains organisms of the same species, whereas a community contains populations of different species.

An ecosystem includes both biotic and abiotic components.

## 9.1.3 Components of an Ecosystem

An ecosystem consists of two main components:

1. Biotic components.
2. Abiotic components.

### A. Biotic Components

Biotic components are the living components of an ecosystem.

They include organisms such as:

- Plants.
- Animals.
- Fungi.
- Bacteria.
- Algae.
- Other microorganisms.

Biotic components can be grouped according to their ecological roles.

#### 1. Producers

Producers are organisms that manufacture organic food from inorganic substances.

Most producers carry out photosynthesis.

Examples:

- Green plants.
- Algae.
- Photosynthetic bacteria.

During photosynthesis, light energy is converted into chemical energy stored in organic molecules.

Producers form the base of most food chains.

#### 2. Consumers

Consumers obtain energy by feeding on other organisms or organic matter.

They cannot manufacture all their organic food directly from inorganic substances.

Types of consumers:

**Primary Consumers**

- Feed directly on producers.
- Usually herbivores.

Examples:

- Grasshoppers.
- Rabbits.
- Deer.
- Zooplankton feeding on phytoplankton.

**Secondary Consumers**

- Feed on primary consumers.

Examples:

- Frogs feeding on grasshoppers.
- Small fish feeding on zooplankton.

**Tertiary Consumers**

- Feed on secondary consumers.

Examples:

- Snakes feeding on frogs.
- Large predatory fish feeding on smaller predatory fish.

**Quaternary Consumers**

- Feed on tertiary consumers in food chains containing this level.

The number of consumer levels varies between ecosystems.

#### 3. Decomposers

Decomposers break down dead organisms and organic waste into simpler substances.

Examples:

- Bacteria.
- Fungi.

Functions:

1. Break down dead plants and animals.
2. Break down organic waste.
3. Release nutrients into the environment.
4. Return nutrients to soil and water.
5. Support nutrient cycling.

**Important:**

Decomposers do not simply make dead organisms disappear. They break down organic matter and release substances that can be reused by other organisms.

### B. Abiotic Components

Abiotic components are the non-living physical and chemical factors in an ecosystem.

Examples include:

**1. Light Intensity**

- Provides energy for photosynthesis.
- Influences plant growth.
- Affects the activity of some animals.

**2. Temperature**

- Affects enzyme activity.
- Influences metabolic rates.
- Affects organism distribution.

**3. Water Availability**

- Essential for metabolic reactions.
- Influences plant growth and survival.
- Determines the distribution of many organisms.

**4. pH**

- Affects enzyme activity.
- Influences the availability of mineral nutrients.
- Determines whether organisms can survive in a particular environment.

**5. Humidity**

- Influences the rate of water loss from organisms.
- Affects the distribution of terrestrial organisms.

**6. Soil Minerals**

- Provide essential mineral nutrients for plant growth.
- Influence soil fertility.

**7. Dissolved Oxygen**

- Required for aerobic respiration by many aquatic organisms.
- Influences the survival and distribution of aquatic animals.

**8. Salinity**

- Refers to the concentration of dissolved salts.
- Influences the water balance of aquatic organisms.

### Interaction Between Biotic and Abiotic Components

Organisms depend on abiotic factors for survival.

Examples:

- Plants require sunlight, water and carbon dioxide for photosynthesis.
- Fish require dissolved oxygen for aerobic respiration.
- Earthworms require suitable soil moisture.
- Aquatic organisms require suitable water temperature and pH.

Abiotic conditions also influence which organisms can survive in an ecosystem.

## 9.1.4 Habitat, Niche and Microhabitat

### Habitat

A habitat is the natural place where an organism lives.

Examples:

- A pond is a habitat for frogs.
- A tree trunk can be a habitat for insects.
- A mangrove forest is a habitat for certain crabs and fish.
- Soil is a habitat for earthworms.

### Microhabitat

A microhabitat is a small, specific part of a larger habitat with particular environmental conditions.

Examples:

- The underside of a fallen log.
- A small area beneath leaf litter.
- The surface of a rock in a stream.
- The space between the roots of a plant.

Microhabitats may differ in moisture, temperature, light and shelter.

### Ecological Niche

An ecological niche refers to the role of an organism in its ecosystem and how it uses resources and interacts with other organisms and the environment.

A niche may include:

- Food sources.
- Feeding behaviour.
- Habitat requirements.
- Activity patterns.
- Reproductive behaviour.
- Interactions with other species.

**Example:**

Two bird species may live in the same forest but occupy different niches if they feed on different foods or use different parts of trees.

### Comparison

| Habitat | Niche |
|---|---|
| Where an organism lives | The organism's ecological role |
| Describes its living place | Describes how it lives and interacts |
| Example: pond | Example: predator feeding on aquatic insects |

## 9.1.5 Interactions Between Organisms

Organisms interact with one another to obtain food, space, shelter and other resources.

Important ecological interactions include:

1. Competition.
2. Predation.
3. Symbiosis.

### A. Competition

Competition occurs when organisms require the same limited resource.

Resources may include:

- Food.
- Water.
- Light.
- Space.
- Minerals.
- Nesting sites.
- Mates.

Competition may occur between organisms of the same species or different species.

#### Intraspecific Competition

Intraspecific competition occurs between individuals of the same species.

Examples:

- Two male deer competing for mates.
- Plants of the same species competing for sunlight.
- Two birds competing for nesting sites.

Effects:

1. Limits population growth.
2. May reduce access to resources.
3. May affect survival and reproductive success.

#### Interspecific Competition

Interspecific competition occurs between individuals of different species.

Examples:

- Different plant species competing for sunlight and soil nutrients.
- Different predators competing for similar prey.
- Different bird species competing for nesting sites.

Effects:

1. One species may become less abundant.
2. Species may occupy different niches.
3. Competition may influence the distribution of organisms.

### B. Predation

Predation occurs when one organism, called the predator, captures and feeds on another organism, called the prey.

Examples:

- A snake eating a rat.
- A frog eating an insect.
- A lion eating a zebra.
- A spider catching a fly.

Ecological importance:

1. Transfers energy between trophic levels.
2. Helps regulate prey populations.
3. Influences community structure.
4. Creates selection pressure that may favour adaptations in predators and prey.

#### Predator Adaptations

Predators may have:

- Sharp teeth.
- Claws.
- Strong jaws.
- Good eyesight.
- Speed.
- Camouflage.
- Venom in certain species.

#### Prey Adaptations

Prey may have:

- Camouflage.
- Warning colours.
- Protective shells.
- Spines.
- Speed.
- Defensive chemicals.
- Group behaviour.

**Example:**

A stick insect resembles a twig, making it harder for predators to detect.

### C. Symbiosis

Symbiosis refers to a close and persistent biological association between organisms of different species.

Three commonly studied forms are:

1. Mutualism.
2. Commensalism.
3. Parasitism.

#### 1. Mutualism

Mutualism is an interaction in which both organisms benefit.

Examples:

**Lichen**

- Consists of a fungus and a photosynthetic partner, usually an alga or cyanobacterium.
- The photosynthetic partner provides organic nutrients.
- The fungus provides protection and helps retain water and minerals.

**Rhizobium and Leguminous Plants**

- Rhizobium bacteria live in root nodules of leguminous plants.
- Bacteria fix atmospheric nitrogen into forms that can enter nitrogen compounds.
- The plant supplies organic nutrients and a suitable environment.

**Bees and Flowering Plants**

- Bees obtain nectar or pollen.
- Flowers receive pollen during pollination.

#### 2. Commensalism

Commensalism is an interaction in which one organism benefits while the other is neither significantly helped nor harmed.

Examples:

**Epiphytic Plants on Trees**

- Epiphytic plants use trees as support.
- They obtain light and access to air.
- The supporting tree is generally not significantly affected.

**Remora Fish and Sharks**

- Remora fish may obtain transport and food scraps.
- The shark is generally not significantly affected.

#### 3. Parasitism

Parasitism is an interaction in which one organism, the parasite, benefits while the host is harmed.

Examples:

**Tapeworm and Human**

- The tapeworm obtains nutrients from the host's digestive system.
- The host may experience nutrient loss and other health effects.

**Mistletoe and Host Plant**

- Mistletoe obtains water and mineral nutrients from its host.
- The host plant may be weakened.

**Plasmodium and Human**

- Plasmodium obtains resources while living in a human host.
- It causes malaria.

### Comparison of Symbiotic Relationships

| Interaction | Organism 1 | Organism 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Mutualism | Benefits | Benefits |
| Commensalism | Benefits | Neither significantly benefits nor is harmed |
| Parasitism | Benefits | Harmed |

### Important Exam Distinction

Predation and parasitism are different.

- Predation usually involves a predator capturing and consuming prey.
- Parasitism involves a parasite living on or in a host and obtaining resources from it, usually without immediately killing the host.

## 9.1.6 Food Chains and Food Webs

### Food Chain

A food chain is a sequence showing the transfer of food energy from one organism to another.

An arrow points from the organism being eaten towards the organism that eats it.

Example:

**Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake → Eagle**

Explanation:

1. Grass is the producer.
2. Grasshopper is the primary consumer.
3. Frog is the secondary consumer.
4. Snake is the tertiary consumer.
5. Eagle is the quaternary consumer.

The arrows represent the direction of energy transfer.

### Trophic Level

A trophic level is the feeding position occupied by an organism in a food chain.

| Trophic level | Example |
|---|---|
| First | Producer |
| Second | Primary consumer |
| Third | Secondary consumer |
| Fourth | Tertiary consumer |
| Fifth | Quaternary consumer |

### Food Web

A food web consists of interconnected food chains in an ecosystem.

It shows that organisms may have several food sources and several predators.

Example:

- Grass is eaten by grasshoppers and rabbits.
- Grasshoppers are eaten by frogs and birds.
- Rabbits are eaten by snakes and eagles.
- Frogs are eaten by snakes.
- Snakes may be eaten by eagles.

### Importance of Food Webs

1. Show feeding relationships in an ecosystem.
2. Demonstrate the flow of energy.
3. Show the dependence of organisms on one another.
4. Help explain how population changes may affect other organisms.
5. Show why ecosystems can be affected by the loss of a species.

**Exam tip:**

Always check the direction of the arrows. An arrow points from the food organism to the organism that consumes it.

## 9.1.7 Energy Flow in an Ecosystem

Energy enters most ecosystems through sunlight.

Photosynthetic producers capture light energy and convert it into chemical energy.

Energy then passes through food chains and food webs.

### Main Stages of Energy Flow

1. Sunlight provides energy.
2. Producers absorb light energy during photosynthesis.
3. Chemical energy is stored in organic compounds.
4. Primary consumers obtain energy by eating producers.
5. Higher-level consumers obtain energy by eating other consumers.
6. Energy is transferred between trophic levels.

### Why Energy Decreases at Higher Trophic Levels

Not all energy in one trophic level is transferred to the next level.

Energy is:

- Used in cellular respiration.
- Released as heat.
- Used for movement.
- Used for growth and reproduction.
- Lost in waste products.
- Present in parts that are not eaten or digested.

Therefore, less energy is generally available at higher trophic levels.

### The 10% Rule

A simplified ecological model sometimes uses the 10% rule.

It suggests that approximately 10% of the energy at one trophic level is transferred to the next trophic level.

This is an approximation, not a fixed value for every ecosystem or food chain.

**Example:**

If producers contain 10,000 kJ of energy:

- Primary consumers receive approximately 1,000 kJ.
- Secondary consumers receive approximately 100 kJ.
- Tertiary consumers receive approximately 10 kJ.

The remaining energy is not transferred to the next trophic level.

**Important:** Energy flows in one direction through an ecosystem and is not recycled. Nutrients, unlike energy, can be recycled.

## 9.1.8 Ecological Pyramids

Ecological pyramids represent the trophic structure of an ecosystem.

There are three main types:

1. Pyramid of numbers.
2. Pyramid of biomass.
3. Pyramid of energy.

### A. Pyramid of Numbers

Shows the number of individual organisms at each trophic level.

In many grassland ecosystems, the pyramid is upright because:

- Many producers support fewer primary consumers.
- Fewer primary consumers support even fewer predators.

However, a pyramid of numbers can be inverted or irregular.

Example:

One large tree may support many insects.

### B. Pyramid of Biomass

Shows the total dry mass of organisms at each trophic level per unit area at a particular time.

Biomass is commonly expressed as dry mass to reduce the effect of water content.

Pyramids of biomass are often upright in terrestrial ecosystems.

Some aquatic ecosystems may have an inverted biomass pyramid at a particular moment because phytoplankton have a low standing biomass but reproduce rapidly.

### C. Pyramid of Energy

Shows the amount of energy available at each trophic level per unit area per unit time.

The pyramid of energy is always upright because energy is lost at each transfer between trophic levels.

### Comparison of Ecological Pyramids

| Pyramid | What it measures | Can be inverted? |
|---|---|---|
| Numbers | Number of organisms | Yes |
| Biomass | Total biomass | Yes |
| Energy | Energy available or transferred | No |

## 9.1.9 Nutrient Cycling

Nutrients move between organisms and the physical environment.

Unlike energy, nutrients can be recycled.

Important processes include:

- Feeding.
- Excretion.
- Death.
- Decomposition.
- Absorption by plants.

### Role of Decomposers in Nutrient Cycling

1. Dead organisms and organic waste contain nutrients.
2. Decomposers break down organic matter.
3. Nutrients are released into the environment.
4. Plants absorb available mineral nutrients.
5. Nutrients pass to consumers through feeding.
6. Nutrients return to the environment through waste and death.

**Important distinction:**

Energy flows through ecosystems and is eventually dissipated as heat. Nutrients are recycled through biogeochemical cycles.

# 9.2 POPULATION ECOLOGY

## 9.2.1 Introduction to Population Ecology

Population ecology is the study of population size, structure, distribution, density and changes over time.

A population consists of organisms of the same species living in the same geographical area at a particular time.

Population ecology helps scientists understand:

- Why populations increase or decrease.
- How organisms are distributed.
- How resources affect population size.
- How environmental conditions affect survival.
- How populations respond to competition, predation and disease.

## 9.2.2 Population Characteristics

Important population characteristics include:

1. Population size.
2. Population density.
3. Population distribution.
4. Population dispersion.
5. Age structure.
6. Birth rate and death rate.

### A. Population Size

Population size is the total number of individuals in a population.

Example:

A pond contains 150 frogs of the same species.

Population size = 150 frogs.

Population size may change because of:

- Births.
- Deaths.
- Immigration.
- Emigration.

### B. Population Density

Population density is the number of individuals per unit area or volume.

Formula:

**Population density = Number of individuals ÷ Area or volume**

For terrestrial organisms, density may be expressed as individuals per square metre or square kilometre.

For aquatic organisms, density may be expressed as individuals per cubic metre.

**Example:**

A grassland contains 200 rabbits in an area of 10 hectares.

Population density:

200 ÷ 10 = 20 rabbits per hectare.

### C. Population Distribution

Population distribution describes where individuals are found within an area.

Three common dispersion patterns are:

1. Clumped.
2. Uniform.
3. Random.

#### 1. Clumped Distribution

Individuals occur in groups or clusters.

This is a common distribution pattern in nature.

Causes:

- Resources occur in patches.
- Organisms form social groups.
- Individuals gather for protection.
- Reproduction occurs in particular areas.

Examples:

- Schools of fish.
- Herds of elephants.
- Groups of trees around water sources.

Advantages may include:

- Increased protection.
- Easier access to concentrated resources.
- Improved cooperation.

#### 2. Uniform Distribution

Individuals are spaced relatively evenly.

Causes:

- Territorial behaviour.
- Competition for resources.
- Chemical inhibition in some plants.

Examples:

- Nesting birds that defend territories.
- Certain plants that release chemicals inhibiting nearby plant growth.

#### 3. Random Distribution

Individuals are distributed without a predictable pattern.

This may occur when:

- Resources are widely available.
- Individuals do not strongly attract or repel one another.
- Environmental conditions are relatively uniform.

Random distribution is less common than clumped distribution in many natural populations.

### Comparison of Dispersion Patterns

| Pattern | Arrangement | Possible cause |
|---|---|---|
| Clumped | Individuals form groups | Patchy resources or social behaviour |
| Uniform | Individuals are evenly spaced | Territorial behaviour or competition |
| Random | No predictable arrangement | Weak attraction or repulsion |

**Important distinction:**

Population density measures the number of individuals per unit area or volume. Dispersion describes how individuals are arranged within the area.

## 9.2.3 Factors Affecting Population Size

Population size changes through four main processes:

1. Natality.
2. Mortality.
3. Immigration.
4. Emigration.

### A. Natality

Natality refers to the production of new individuals through reproduction.

An increase in natality generally increases population size.

Factors affecting natality:

- Availability of mates.
- Food supply.
- Environmental conditions.
- Reproductive age.
- Disease and stress.

### B. Mortality

Mortality refers to the death of individuals in a population.

An increase in mortality generally decreases population size.

Causes include:

- Predation.
- Disease.
- Food shortage.
- Competition.
- Extreme environmental conditions.
- Ageing.

### C. Immigration

Immigration occurs when individuals enter a population from another area.

Immigration increases population size.

### D. Emigration

Emigration occurs when individuals leave a population and move to another area.

Emigration decreases population size.

### Population Change Formula

**Change in population size = (Births + Immigration) − (Deaths + Emigration)**

Or:

**N₂ = N₁ + B + I − D − E**

Where:

- N₁ = Initial population size.
- N₂ = Final population size.
- B = Number of births.
- I = Number of immigrants.
- D = Number of deaths.
- E = Number of emigrants.

**Example:**

A population initially contains 500 individuals.

During one year:

- Births = 80.
- Immigration = 20.
- Deaths = 50.
- Emigration = 10.

Final population:

500 + 80 + 20 − 50 − 10 = 540 individuals.

The population increases by 40 individuals.

## 9.2.4 Population Growth

Population growth refers to the change in population size over time.

Population growth depends on:

- Birth rate.
- Death rate.
- Immigration.
- Emigration.
- Resource availability.
- Environmental conditions.

Two important population growth patterns are:

1. Exponential growth.
2. Logistic growth.

### A. Exponential Growth

Exponential growth occurs when a population increases rapidly under favourable conditions with abundant resources and limited environmental resistance.

Characteristics:

- Birth rate is high relative to death rate.
- Resources are abundant.
- Population growth accelerates as population size increases.
- Produces a J-shaped growth curve.

Examples may include:

- Bacteria growing under favourable laboratory conditions.
- A population introduced into a new environment with abundant resources and few limiting factors.

### J-Shaped Growth Curve

The J-shaped curve shows slow initial growth followed by rapid population increase.

Main phases:

**1. Lag Phase**

- Population increases slowly.
- Individuals adapt to the environment.
- Reproduction may initially be limited.

**2. Exponential Phase**

- Population increases rapidly.
- Resources are abundant.
- Birth rate exceeds death rate.

**3. Rapid Growth**

- The population becomes very large over a relatively short period.
- Environmental limitations may eventually become important.

If resources become insufficient, the population may decline sharply.

**Important:** A J-shaped curve does not mean a population can grow indefinitely in nature.

### B. Logistic Growth

Logistic growth occurs when population growth slows as environmental resistance increases.

It produces an S-shaped growth curve.

The population initially increases, but growth gradually slows as resources become limited.

### S-Shaped Growth Curve

**1. Lag Phase**

- Population increases slowly.
- Individuals adapt to environmental conditions.

**2. Exponential Phase**

- Population increases rapidly.
- Resources are initially abundant.

**3. Deceleration Phase**

- Growth begins to slow.
- Competition increases.
- Resources become more limited.
- Environmental resistance increases.

**4. Stationary Phase**

- Population size fluctuates around the carrying capacity.
- Birth rate is approximately balanced by death rate, assuming little net migration.

### Comparison of Exponential and Logistic Growth

| Exponential growth | Logistic growth |
|---|---|
| J-shaped curve | S-shaped curve |
| Resources are assumed to be abundant | Resources become limiting |
| Rapid increase continues in the model | Growth slows as population approaches carrying capacity |
| Environmental resistance is initially limited | Environmental resistance increases |

## 9.2.5 Carrying Capacity

Carrying capacity is the maximum population size that an environment can support sustainably over time under particular environmental conditions.

It is commonly represented by the letter K.

Carrying capacity depends on:

- Food availability.
- Water availability.
- Space.
- Shelter.
- Nutrients.
- Predation.
- Disease.
- Environmental conditions.

### What Happens When a Population Reaches Carrying Capacity?

When population size approaches carrying capacity:

1. Competition for resources increases.
2. Birth rate may decrease.
3. Death rate may increase.
4. Population growth slows.
5. Population size may fluctuate around carrying capacity.

Carrying capacity is not necessarily constant.

It may change when environmental conditions change.

**Example:**

A drought reduces water and food availability. The environment may then support fewer herbivores, causing carrying capacity to decrease.

## 9.2.6 Limiting Factors

Limiting factors are environmental factors that restrict population growth, size or distribution.

They may be density-dependent or density-independent.

### A. Density-Dependent Factors

Density-dependent factors have effects that often become stronger as population density increases.

Examples:

**1. Competition**

- Individuals compete for limited food, water, space or mates.
- Increased competition may reduce growth and reproduction.

**2. Predation**

- A higher prey population may provide more food for predators.
- Predation may limit prey population growth.

**3. Disease**

- Diseases may spread more easily when individuals live close together.
- High population density can increase contact between infected and healthy individuals.

**4. Parasitism**

- Parasites may spread more easily in crowded populations.
- Parasitism can reduce survival and reproductive success.

**5. Food Shortage**

- A large population may consume resources faster than they can be replaced.
- This can reduce survival and reproduction.

### B. Density-Independent Factors

Density-independent factors affect populations regardless of their density.

Examples:

- Floods.
- Droughts.
- Forest fires.
- Extreme temperatures.
- Storms.
- Certain pollution events.

Effects:

1. May cause sudden deaths.
2. May destroy habitats.
3. May reduce food and water availability.
4. May affect both small and large populations.

### Comparison

| Density-dependent | Density-independent |
|---|---|
| Often becomes stronger as density increases | Can affect populations regardless of density |
| Competition | Flood |
| Disease transmission in crowded populations | Drought |
| Predation | Extreme temperature |
| Parasitism | Forest fire |

## 9.2.7 Population Sampling Methods

Scientists often estimate population size instead of counting every organism.

Sampling is useful when:

- The habitat is large.
- Organisms are numerous.
- Organisms are difficult to observe.
- Counting every individual would take too much time.

Two important sampling approaches are:

1. Quadrat sampling.
2. Capture-mark-release-recapture.

### A. Quadrat Sampling

A quadrat is a square frame used to sample organisms within a defined area.

It is commonly used for plants and slow-moving or stationary organisms.

Examples:

- Grass.
- Small plants.
- Mosses.
- Certain slow-moving invertebrates.

### How Quadrat Sampling Works

1. Select a study area.
2. Place quadrats at selected sampling positions.
3. Count the organisms inside each quadrat.
4. Repeat the sampling at several locations.
5. Calculate the average number of organisms per quadrat.
6. Estimate population size for the whole area if appropriate.

### Random Sampling

Quadrats are placed at randomly selected positions.

Advantages:

- Reduces selection bias.
- Provides a more representative estimate when sampling is properly designed.

### Systematic Sampling

Quadrats are placed at regular intervals along a line or transect.

It is useful for studying changes in organism distribution across environmental gradients.

### Estimating Population Size

A simplified formula is:

**Estimated population size = Mean number of individuals per quadrat × Total area ÷ Quadrat area**

**Example:**

A grassland has a total area of 100 m².

Each quadrat covers 1 m².

The mean number of grass plants per quadrat is 12.

Estimated population size:

12 × 100 ÷ 1 = 1,200 grass plants.

### Limitations of Quadrat Sampling

1. Not suitable for highly mobile organisms.
2. Organisms may be distributed unevenly.
3. Too few quadrats may produce an unreliable estimate.
4. Incorrect identification may affect results.
5. Sampling bias may occur if positions are not selected properly.

### B. Capture-Mark-Release-Recapture

This method is used to estimate the population size of mobile animals.

Examples:

- Fish.
- Beetles.
- Small mammals.
- Certain insects.

### Basic Procedure

1. Capture a first sample of animals.
2. Count the animals.
3. Mark them using a suitable method that does not harm them.
4. Release them back into the population.
5. Allow time for the marked animals to mix with the population.
6. Capture a second sample.
7. Count how many animals are marked and unmarked.
8. Estimate the total population size.

### Lincoln-Petersen Formula

**Estimated population size = (First sample × Second sample) ÷ Number of marked animals recaptured**

Or:

**N = (M × C) ÷ R**

Where:

- N = Estimated population size.
- M = Number of animals captured and marked initially.
- C = Total number captured in the second sample.
- R = Number of marked animals recaptured in the second sample.

### Worked Example

A researcher captures and marks 40 beetles.

Later, the researcher captures 50 beetles.

Of the second sample, 10 are marked.

Calculate the estimated population size.

Given:

M = 40  
C = 50  
R = 10

N = (40 × 50) ÷ 10

N = 2,000 ÷ 10

**Estimated population size = 200 beetles.**

### Assumptions of Capture-Mark-Release-Recapture

For the estimate to be reliable:

1. The population should remain relatively closed during the study.
2. Marked individuals should mix randomly with unmarked individuals.
3. Marks should remain visible.
4. Marks should not significantly affect survival or behaviour.
5. Every individual should have a reasonable chance of being captured.
6. Marked animals should not be unusually easy or difficult to recapture.

### Limitations

- Animals may lose their marks.
- Marking may affect behaviour.
- Animals may enter or leave the study area.
- Marked animals may not mix evenly.
- Capture probability may differ between individuals.

## 9.2.8 Population Growth Rate

Population growth rate describes how quickly a population changes in size over a particular period.

A simple numerical change is:

**Population change = Final population − Initial population**

Percentage population change:

**Percentage change = (Population change ÷ Initial population) × 100%**

### Example

A population increases from 200 to 260 individuals.

Population change:

260 − 200 = 60.

Percentage change:

(60 ÷ 200) × 100% = 30%.

The population increased by 30%.

**Important:** When calculating percentage change, use the initial population as the denominator.

## 9.2.9 Human Population Growth

Human population size is influenced by:

- Birth rates.
- Death rates.
- Immigration.
- Emigration.
- Healthcare.
- Food availability.
- Sanitation.
- Living conditions.

Improvements in healthcare, sanitation and food production can reduce mortality and increase life expectancy.

Rapid human population growth may increase pressure on:

- Housing.
- Food supplies.
- Water resources.
- Energy resources.
- Land.
- Ecosystems.

Sustainable resource management is important to reduce environmental impacts.

## 9.2.10 Age Structure of a Population

Age structure refers to the distribution of individuals among different age groups.

Common categories are:

1. Pre-reproductive.
2. Reproductive.
3. Post-reproductive.

Age structure helps scientists predict possible future population trends.

### General Population Patterns

**Growing Population**

- Often has a relatively large proportion of young individuals.
- Many individuals may enter reproductive age in the future.

**Stable Population**

- Age groups are more balanced.
- Birth rates and death rates may be relatively balanced.

**Declining Population**

- May have a relatively small proportion of young individuals.
- Birth rates may be insufficient to replace older generations.

Age structure diagrams can help show these patterns.

# IMPORTANT FORMULAS AND CALCULATIONS

## 1. Population Density

Population density = Number of individuals ÷ Area or volume.

## 2. Population Change

Change in population = (Births + Immigration) − (Deaths + Emigration).

## 3. Final Population Size

N₂ = N₁ + B + I − D − E.

## 4. Quadrat Sampling

Estimated population = Mean number per quadrat × Total area ÷ Quadrat area.

## 5. Capture-Mark-Release-Recapture

N = (M × C) ÷ R.

## 6. Percentage Population Change

Percentage change = (Final population − Initial population) ÷ Initial population × 100%.

# COMMON EXAM MISTAKES

1. A population contains individuals of the same species, while a community contains populations of different species.

2. An ecosystem includes both living organisms and abiotic components.

3. Habitat describes where an organism lives, while niche describes its ecological role.

4. Competition occurs when organisms require the same limited resource.

5. Mutualism benefits both organisms.

6. Commensalism benefits one organism without significantly affecting the other.

7. Parasitism benefits the parasite while harming the host.

8. Food-chain arrows point from the food organism towards the organism consuming it.

9. Energy flows through an ecosystem, whereas nutrients are recycled.

10. The pyramid of energy is always upright.

11. A pyramid of numbers and a pyramid of biomass may be inverted.

12. Population density is not the same as population distribution.

13. Immigration increases population size, while emigration decreases it.

14. Carrying capacity is not necessarily fixed. It changes with environmental conditions.

15. Density-dependent factors often become stronger when population density increases.

16. Floods and droughts are examples of density-independent factors.

17. Quadrat sampling is most suitable for stationary or slow-moving organisms.

18. Capture-mark-release-recapture is used to estimate populations of mobile animals.

19. The number of marked animals recaptured must be used as R in the Lincoln-Petersen formula.

20. Use the initial population when calculating percentage population change.

# FINAL REVISION SUMMARY

## 9.1 COMMUNITY AND ECOSYSTEM

- Ecology studies interactions between organisms and their environment.
- Organism → Population → Community → Ecosystem → Biome → Biosphere.
- Ecosystems contain biotic and abiotic components.
- Producers manufacture organic food.
- Consumers obtain food by feeding on other organisms.
- Decomposers break down dead organic matter and recycle nutrients.
- Habitat is where an organism lives.
- Niche is its ecological role.
- Competition occurs when resources are limited.
- Predation involves predators feeding on prey.
- Symbiosis includes mutualism, commensalism and parasitism.
- Food chains show energy transfer.
- Food webs consist of interconnected food chains.
- Energy generally decreases at higher trophic levels.
- Ecological pyramids include numbers, biomass and energy.
- The pyramid of energy is always upright.
- Energy flows, while nutrients are recycled.

## 9.2 POPULATION ECOLOGY

- Population ecology studies population characteristics and changes.
- Population size is the total number of individuals.
- Population density is the number of individuals per unit area or volume.
- Dispersion may be clumped, uniform or random.
- Natality and immigration increase population size.
- Mortality and emigration decrease population size.
- Exponential growth produces a J-shaped curve.
- Logistic growth produces an S-shaped curve.
- Carrying capacity is the maximum population size an environment can sustainably support.
- Limiting factors may be density-dependent or density-independent.
- Quadrat sampling is useful for stationary organisms.
- Capture-mark-release-recapture estimates mobile animal populations.
- Age structure helps predict future population trends.

# KEY BIOLOGY TERMS

**Ecology:** Study of interactions between organisms and their environment.

**Organism:** An individual living thing.

**Population:** Individuals of the same species living in the same area at a particular time.

**Community:** Populations of different species interacting in an area.

**Ecosystem:** A community interacting with its abiotic environment.

**Biome:** A large region characterised by climate and typical organisms.

**Biosphere:** The part of Earth where life exists.

**Biotic component:** Living component of an ecosystem.

**Abiotic component:** Non-living environmental factor.

**Producer:** Organism that manufactures organic food.

**Consumer:** Organism that obtains energy by feeding on other organisms or organic matter.

**Decomposer:** Organism that breaks down dead organic matter and waste.

**Habitat:** Natural place where an organism lives.

**Microhabitat:** Small, specific part of a habitat.

**Ecological niche:** Role of an organism in its ecosystem.

**Competition:** Interaction in which organisms require the same limited resource.

**Predation:** Interaction in which a predator captures and feeds on prey.

**Mutualism:** Interaction in which both organisms benefit.

**Commensalism:** Interaction in which one organism benefits and the other is not significantly affected.

**Parasitism:** Interaction in which a parasite benefits while its host is harmed.

**Food chain:** Sequence showing the transfer of food energy between organisms.

**Food web:** Network of interconnected food chains.

**Trophic level:** Feeding position in a food chain.

**Biomass:** Total mass of biological material in a given area or volume.

**Population size:** Total number of individuals in a population.

**Population density:** Number of individuals per unit area or volume.

**Natality:** Production of new individuals through reproduction.

**Mortality:** Death of individuals in a population.

**Immigration:** Movement of individuals into a population.

**Emigration:** Movement of individuals out of a population.

**Exponential growth:** Rapid population growth under conditions with abundant resources and limited environmental resistance.

**Logistic growth:** Population growth that slows as environmental resistance increases.

**Carrying capacity:** Maximum population size an environment can sustainably support under particular conditions.

**Limiting factor:** Environmental factor that restricts population growth or distribution.

**Quadrat:** Frame used to sample organisms within a defined area.

**Capture-mark-release-recapture:** Sampling method used to estimate the population size of mobile animals.
