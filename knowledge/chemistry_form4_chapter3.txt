TOPIC 3.1: RELATIVE ATOMIC MASS AND RELATIVE MOLECULAR MASS
------------------------------------------------------------

3.1.1 UNDERSTANDING RELATIVE ATOMIC MASS (Ar)

Atoms are extremely small. Their masses cannot conveniently
be measured using ordinary laboratory balances.

Scientists therefore use a relative scale to compare the
masses of atoms.

The relative atomic mass of an element is the average mass
of one atom of the element compared with one-twelfth of the
mass of one carbon-12 atom.

Definition:

Relative atomic mass, Ar, is the average mass of one atom
of an element compared with one-twelfth of the mass of
one carbon-12 atom.

The reference standard is carbon-12.

Why is carbon-12 used?

1. Carbon-12 is a stable isotope.
2. It provides an agreed reference for comparing atomic
   masses.
3. Its mass is divided into twelve equal parts.
4. The relative masses of other atoms are compared with
   one-twelfth of the mass of carbon-12.

Important:
Ar is a relative value. It has no unit.

Examples of commonly used relative atomic masses:

H = 1
C = 12
N = 14
O = 16
Na = 23
Mg = 24
Al = 27
S = 32
Cl = 35.5
K = 39
Ca = 40
Fe = 56
Cu = 63.5
Zn = 65

These are commonly used examination values. Always follow
the relative atomic masses supplied in the question if
different values are given.

Understanding the meaning:

If the relative atomic mass of magnesium is 24, this means
that a magnesium atom has an average mass 24 times as large
as one-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.

It does NOT mean that one magnesium atom has a mass of
24 grams.

Common mistake:
Do not write g after Ar.

Correct: Ar of Mg = 24
Incorrect: Ar of Mg = 24 g

3.1.2 RELATIVE MOLECULAR MASS (Mr)

A molecule consists of two or more atoms chemically bonded
together.

Relative molecular mass is the mass of a molecule compared
with one-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.

Definition:

Relative molecular mass, Mr, is the average mass of one
molecule compared with one-twelfth of the mass of one
carbon-12 atom.

It is calculated by adding the relative atomic masses of
all atoms present in the molecular formula.

Formula:

Mr = sum of the Ar values of all atoms in a molecule.

EXAMPLE 1: Calculate the Mr of water, H2O.

Given:
Ar(H) = 1
Ar(O) = 16

Step 1: Identify the atoms.
Water contains:
2 hydrogen atoms
1 oxygen atom

Step 2: Multiply each Ar by the number of atoms.

Hydrogen contribution:
2 x 1 = 2

Oxygen contribution:
1 x 16 = 16

Step 3: Add the contributions.

Mr(H2O) = 2 + 16
Mr(H2O) = 18

Answer: Mr of water = 18.

EXAMPLE 2: Calculate the Mr of carbon dioxide, CO2.

Given:
Ar(C) = 12
Ar(O) = 16

Mr(CO2) = 12 + (2 x 16)
Mr(CO2) = 12 + 32
Mr(CO2) = 44

Answer: Mr of carbon dioxide = 44.

EXAMPLE 3: Calculate the Mr of sulphuric acid, H2SO4.

Given:
Ar(H) = 1
Ar(S) = 32
Ar(O) = 16

Mr(H2SO4) = (2 x 1) + 32 + (4 x 16)
Mr(H2SO4) = 2 + 32 + 64
Mr(H2SO4) = 98

Answer: Mr of sulphuric acid = 98.

EXAMPLE 4: Calculate the Mr of calcium hydroxide,
Ca(OH)2.

Given:
Ar(Ca) = 40
Ar(O) = 16
Ar(H) = 1

The bracket means that the entire OH group occurs twice.

Mr(Ca(OH)2) = 40 + 2(16 + 1)
= 40 + 34
= 74

Answer: Mr of calcium hydroxide = 74.

IMPORTANT RULES FOR BRACKETS:

For Ca(OH)2:
- There is 1 calcium atom.
- There are 2 oxygen atoms.
- There are 2 hydrogen atoms.

For Al2(SO4)3:
- There are 2 aluminium atoms.
- There are 3 sulphur atoms.
- There are 12 oxygen atoms.

The number outside a bracket multiplies every atom
inside that bracket.

3.1.3 RELATIVE FORMULA MASS

Ionic compounds do not normally exist as separate
molecules. They form giant ionic lattices.

Therefore, the term relative formula mass is used for
ionic compounds.

Relative formula mass is calculated by adding the
relative atomic masses of all atoms represented in
the chemical formula.

Example: Calculate the relative formula mass of NaCl.

Ar(Na) = 23
Ar(Cl) = 35.5

Relative formula mass = 23 + 35.5
= 58.5

Example: Calculate the relative formula mass of MgCl2.

Ar(Mg) = 24
Ar(Cl) = 35.5

Relative formula mass = 24 + (2 x 35.5)
= 24 + 71
= 95

Difference between Mr and relative formula mass:

Relative molecular mass:
- Used for molecular substances.
- Refers to the mass of a molecule relative to the
  carbon-12 standard.

Relative formula mass:
- Used for ionic compounds.
- Calculated using the formula unit of the compound.

IMPORTANT:
Both values are dimensionless relative quantities.
Do not attach the unit g/mol to Mr or relative formula mass.

TOPIC 3.2: MOLE CONCEPT
------------------------------------------------------------

3.2.1 WHAT IS A MOLE?

Atoms, ions and molecules are extremely small.
A normal sample of matter contains an enormous number
of particles.

Counting individual particles one by one is impractical.

Chemists therefore use a counting unit called the mole.

Definition:

One mole is the amount of substance that contains exactly
6.02214076 x 10^23 elementary entities.

The mole is represented by the symbol mol.

The elementary entities may be:
- Atoms
- Molecules
- Ions
- Electrons
- Other specified particles

For Form 4 calculations, the particle type must be
identified clearly.

Examples:

1 mol of carbon atoms contains
6.02214076 x 10^23 carbon atoms.

1 mol of oxygen molecules contains
6.02214076 x 10^23 oxygen molecules.

1 mol of sodium ions contains
6.02214076 x 10^23 sodium ions.

IMPORTANT DISTINCTION:

Oxygen atom = O

Oxygen molecule = O2

One mole of oxygen atoms is not the same particle
description as one mole of oxygen molecules.

One mole of O atoms contains NA oxygen atoms.

One mole of O2 molecules contains NA oxygen molecules,
which contain 2NA oxygen atoms in total.

3.2.2 AVOGADRO CONSTANT (NA)

The Avogadro constant is the number of specified
elementary entities in one mole of a substance.

Symbol:

NA

Value:

NA = 6.02214076 x 10^23 mol^-1

In many school calculations, it may be rounded to:

NA = 6.02 x 10^23 mol^-1

Use the value provided by the question or examination.

The unit mol^-1 means per mole.

RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN NUMBER OF PARTICLES AND MOLES

Formula:

N = n x NA

Where:
N = number of particles
n = number of moles, in mol
NA = Avogadro constant, in mol^-1

To calculate number of moles:

n = N / NA

To calculate number of particles:

N = n x NA

EXAMPLE 1: Calculate the number of atoms in 2 mol of
magnesium.

Given:
n = 2 mol
NA = 6.02 x 10^23 mol^-1

Formula:
N = n x NA

Substitute:
N = 2 x 6.02 x 10^23

N = 1.204 x 10^24 atoms

Answer:
1.204 x 10^24 magnesium atoms.

EXAMPLE 2: Calculate the number of molecules in
0.5 mol of carbon dioxide.

Given:
n = 0.5 mol
NA = 6.02 x 10^23 mol^-1

N = n x NA

N = 0.5 x 6.02 x 10^23

N = 3.01 x 10^23 molecules

Answer:
3.01 x 10^23 carbon dioxide molecules.

EXAMPLE 3: A sample contains 1.204 x 10^24
oxygen molecules. Calculate the number of moles.

Given:
N = 1.204 x 10^24 molecules
NA = 6.02 x 10^23 mol^-1

Formula:
n = N / NA

Substitute:
n = (1.204 x 10^24) / (6.02 x 10^23)

n = 2 mol

Answer: 2 mol of oxygen molecules.

IMPORTANT:
Always identify whether the question asks for atoms,
molecules or ions. Do not change the particle type
without explaining the relationship.

3.2.3 MOLAR MASS

Molar mass is the mass of one mole of a substance.

Symbol:
M

Common unit:
g mol^-1

Molar mass has the same numerical value as the
relative atomic mass, relative molecular mass or
relative formula mass, but it has a unit.

Examples:

Ar(Mg) = 24
Molar mass of Mg = 24 g mol^-1

Mr(H2O) = 18
Molar mass of H2O = 18 g mol^-1

Relative formula mass of NaCl = 58.5
Molar mass of NaCl = 58.5 g mol^-1

Difference:

Mr(H2O) = 18 (no unit)

Molar mass of H2O = 18 g mol^-1

3.2.4 RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN MASS, MOLES AND
MOLAR MASS

Formula:

n = m / M

Where:
n = number of moles, mol
m = mass of substance, g
M = molar mass, g mol^-1

Rearranged formulae:

m = n x M

M = m / n

Use the formula that matches the unknown quantity.

EXAMPLE 1: Calculate the number of moles in 36 g
of water.

Given:
Mass = 36 g
Mr(H2O) = 18
Molar mass = 18 g mol^-1

Formula:
n = m / M

Substitute:
n = 36 / 18

n = 2 mol

Answer: 2 mol of water.

EXAMPLE 2: Calculate the mass of 0.5 mol of carbon
dioxide.

Given:
n = 0.5 mol
Mr(CO2) = 44
M = 44 g mol^-1

Formula:
m = n x M

Substitute:
m = 0.5 x 44

m = 22 g

Answer: 22 g of carbon dioxide.

EXAMPLE 3: A sample of magnesium has a mass of 12 g.
Calculate the number of moles.

Given:
m = 12 g
Ar(Mg) = 24
M = 24 g mol^-1

n = m / M

n = 12 / 24

n = 0.5 mol

Answer: 0.5 mol of magnesium atoms.

3.2.5 MOLAR VOLUME OF A GAS

Molar volume is the volume occupied by one mole of
a gas at a specified temperature and pressure.

The molar volume depends on the temperature and pressure.

For the KSSM Form 4 calculations, distinguish between
the conditions stated in the question.

Common school values:

At standard temperature and pressure (STP):
Molar volume = 22.4 dm^3 mol^-1

At room conditions:
Molar volume = 24 dm^3 mol^-1

IMPORTANT:
Use the value specified by the question or the
conditions required by the prescribed syllabus.
Do not use 22.4 and 24 interchangeably.

Formula at STP:

V = n x 22.4

Formula at room conditions:

V = n x 24

Where:
V = volume of gas, dm^3
n = number of moles, mol

To calculate number of moles:

At STP:
n = V / 22.4

At room conditions:
n = V / 24

If volume is given in cm^3, convert it into dm^3
before using these formulae.

1 dm^3 = 1000 cm^3

1 cm^3 = 0.001 dm^3

EXAMPLE 1: Calculate the volume occupied by 2 mol
of a gas at room conditions.

Given:
n = 2 mol
Molar volume = 24 dm^3 mol^-1

Formula:
V = n x 24

Substitute:
V = 2 x 24

V = 48 dm^3

Answer: 48 dm^3.

EXAMPLE 2: Calculate the number of moles in 600 cm^3
of gas at room conditions.

Step 1: Convert cm^3 into dm^3.

600 / 1000 = 0.600 dm^3

Step 2: Use the molar volume formula.

n = V / 24

n = 0.600 / 24

n = 0.025 mol

Answer: 0.025 mol.

EXAMPLE 3: Calculate the volume occupied by 0.25 mol
of gas at STP.

Given:
n = 0.25 mol
Molar volume = 22.4 dm^3 mol^-1

V = n x 22.4

V = 0.25 x 22.4

V = 5.6 dm^3

Answer: 5.6 dm^3.

IMPORTANT GAS CALCULATION RULES:

1. Identify whether the question states STP or room
   conditions.
2. Identify the volume unit.
3. Convert cm^3 into dm^3 when necessary.
4. Select the correct molar volume.
5. Substitute the values correctly.
6. Include the correct unit.

3.2.6 SUMMARY OF RELATIONSHIPS

Number of particles and moles:

N = n x NA

n = N / NA

Mass and moles:

n = m / M

m = n x M

Gas volume at STP:

V = n x 22.4

n = V / 22.4

Gas volume at room conditions:

V = n x 24

n = V / 24

The student must understand which formula applies
to the information provided.

TOPIC 3.3: CHEMICAL FORMULA

3.3.1 MEANING OF CHEMICAL FORMULA

A chemical formula represents the types and relative
numbers of atoms or ions present in a substance.

Examples:

H2O = water
CO2 = carbon dioxide
NaCl = sodium chloride
MgO = magnesium oxide
H2SO4 = sulphuric acid

A chemical formula provides information about the
composition of a substance.

SUBSCRIPTS:

A subscript is a small number written at the lower
right of a chemical symbol.

Example:

H2O

The subscript 2 means that one water molecule contains
two hydrogen atoms.

O has no subscript, which means one oxygen atom.

If there is no subscript, the number of atoms is one.

Examples:

CO2:
1 carbon atom
2 oxygen atoms

NH3:
1 nitrogen atom
3 hydrogen atoms

Al2O3:
2 aluminium atoms
3 oxygen atoms

3.3.2 EMPIRICAL FORMULA

Definition:

The empirical formula is the simplest whole-number
ratio of atoms of each element in a compound.

It does not necessarily show the actual number of atoms
in one molecule.

Examples:

Molecular formula C6H12O6
Empirical formula CH2O

Molecular formula H2O2
Empirical formula HO

Molecular formula C2H4
Empirical formula CH2

The empirical formula shows the simplest ratio.

3.3.3 DETERMINING EMPIRICAL FORMULA FROM MASS

General method:

STEP 1:
Write down the mass of each element.

STEP 2:
Convert the mass of each element into moles.

Formula:
Number of moles = mass / Ar

STEP 3:
Divide all mole values by the smallest mole value.

STEP 4:
Obtain the simplest whole-number ratio.

STEP 5:
Write the empirical formula using the ratio as
the subscripts.

EXAMPLE 1: A compound contains 24 g of magnesium
and 16 g of oxygen. Determine its empirical formula.

Given:
Ar(Mg) = 24
Ar(O) = 16

STEP 1: Write the masses.

Magnesium = 24 g
Oxygen = 16 g

STEP 2: Calculate the number of moles.

Moles of Mg = 24 / 24 = 1

Moles of O = 16 / 16 = 1

STEP 3: Divide by the smallest value.

Mg = 1 / 1 = 1

O = 1 / 1 = 1

STEP 4: Simplest ratio.

Mg : O = 1 : 1

STEP 5: Write the empirical formula.

MgO

Answer: MgO.

EXAMPLE 2: A compound contains 12 g of carbon
and 4 g of hydrogen. Determine its empirical formula.

Given:
Ar(C) = 12
Ar(H) = 1

STEP 1: Masses.

Carbon = 12 g
Hydrogen = 4 g

STEP 2: Convert to moles.

Moles of C = 12 / 12 = 1 mol

Moles of H = 4 / 1 = 4 mol

STEP 3: Divide by the smallest value.

C = 1 / 1 = 1

H = 4 / 1 = 4

STEP 4: Ratio.

C : H = 1 : 4

STEP 5: Empirical formula.

CH4

Answer: CH4.

3.3.4 HANDLING NON-WHOLE-NUMBER RATIOS

Sometimes division by the smallest mole value gives
a ratio containing fractions or decimals.

Convert the ratio into the simplest whole-number ratio.

Common conversions:

0.5 -> multiply all ratios by 2
0.33 -> multiply all ratios by 3
0.25 -> multiply all ratios by 4
0.2 -> multiply all ratios by 5
0.67 -> multiply all ratios by 3
0.75 -> multiply all ratios by 4

EXAMPLE:

Suppose the mole ratio is:

A : B = 1 : 1.5

Multiply both values by 2:

A : B = 2 : 3

The empirical formula ratio is 2 : 3.

IMPORTANT:
Do not round a ratio carelessly.
A value such as 1.5 must not be rounded to 2.
Use the correct multiplier to obtain whole numbers.

3.3.5 DETERMINING EMPIRICAL FORMULA FROM
PERCENTAGE COMPOSITION

If percentage composition is given, assume the total
mass of the sample is 100 g.

This allows each percentage to be treated as a mass
in grams.

EXAMPLE:

A compound contains:
40% carbon
6.67% hydrogen
53.33% oxygen

Determine its empirical formula.

Given:
Ar(C) = 12
Ar(H) = 1
Ar(O) = 16

STEP 1: Assume 100 g of compound.

Carbon = 40 g
Hydrogen = 6.67 g
Oxygen = 53.33 g

STEP 2: Convert each mass into moles.

C = 40 / 12 = 3.333 mol

H = 6.67 / 1 = 6.67 mol

O = 53.33 / 16 = 3.333 mol

STEP 3: Divide by the smallest value.

C = 3.333 / 3.333 = 1

H = 6.67 / 3.333 = 2

O = 3.333 / 3.333 = 1

STEP 4: Ratio.

C : H : O = 1 : 2 : 1

STEP 5: Write empirical formula.

CH2O

Answer: CH2O.

3.3.6 DETERMINING EMPIRICAL FORMULA FROM
EXPERIMENTAL DATA

Magnesium oxide is a common example used to understand
empirical formula determination.

When magnesium reacts with oxygen, magnesium oxide
is formed.

Word equation:

Magnesium + Oxygen -> Magnesium oxide

Balanced chemical equation:

2Mg(s) + O2(g) -> 2MgO(s)

The empirical formula of magnesium oxide is MgO.

The ratio of magnesium atoms to oxygen atoms is 1 : 1.

In an experimental calculation, the mass of oxygen
combined with magnesium can be obtained from the
difference between the final and initial relevant masses,
provided the measurements are correctly taken.

Mass of oxygen = mass of magnesium oxide - mass of
magnesium

Then:

Moles of magnesium = mass of magnesium / Ar(Mg)

Moles of oxygen = mass of oxygen / Ar(O)

Divide both mole values by the smaller value to
obtain the simplest ratio.

Copper(II) oxide can be studied in a similar way.

Copper reacts with oxygen to form copper(II) oxide.

2Cu(s) + O2(g) -> 2CuO(s)

The formula CuO represents a 1 : 1 ratio of copper
to oxygen atoms.

IMPORTANT EXPERIMENTAL ACCURACY:
Do not invent experimental masses or claim a particular
experimental result unless the question provides the data.
The calculated empirical formula must come from the
actual measured or supplied values.

3.3.7 MOLECULAR FORMULA

Definition:

The molecular formula shows the actual number of atoms
of each element present in one molecule of a compound.

The molecular formula may be the same as or a whole-number
multiple of the empirical formula.

Examples:

Empirical formula: CH2
Molecular formula: C2H4

Empirical formula: CH2O
Molecular formula: C6H12O6

3.3.8 DETERMINING MOLECULAR FORMULA

Formula:

Molecular formula = (Empirical formula)n

Where:

n = Mr / empirical formula mass

The value of n must be a positive whole number for
a valid molecular formula.

METHOD:

STEP 1:
Determine the empirical formula.

STEP 2:
Calculate the empirical formula mass.

STEP 3:
Divide the relative molecular mass by the empirical
formula mass.

STEP 4:
Multiply every subscript in the empirical formula
by the resulting whole number.

EXAMPLE:

A compound has empirical formula CH2O and relative
molecular mass 180. Determine its molecular formula.

Given:
Ar(C) = 12
Ar(H) = 1
Ar(O) = 16

STEP 1: Empirical formula.

CH2O

STEP 2: Calculate empirical formula mass.

= 12 + (2 x 1) + 16
= 30

STEP 3: Calculate n.

n = Mr / empirical formula mass

n = 180 / 30

n = 6

STEP 4: Multiply every subscript by 6.

CH2O becomes C6H12O6.

Answer: C6H12O6.

IMPORTANT:
Multiply every subscript, including an implied subscript
of 1. Do not multiply only one element.

3.3.9 CONSTRUCTING CHEMICAL FORMULAE OF COMPOUNDS

Chemical formulae must represent the correct ratio
of ions or atoms in a compound.

For ionic compounds, the total positive charge must
balance the total negative charge.

Examples:

Sodium ion = Na+
Chloride ion = Cl-

NaCl

Magnesium ion = Mg2+
Chloride ion = Cl-

MgCl2

Aluminium ion = Al3+
Oxide ion = O2-

Al2O3

Calcium ion = Ca2+
Hydroxide ion = OH-

Ca(OH)2

IMPORTANT RULES:

1. The final compound must be electrically neutral.
2. Use the correct ionic charges.
3. Use the simplest whole-number ratio.
4. Use brackets when a polyatomic ion occurs more
   than once.
5. Do not write the charges as part of the final
   neutral compound formula.

Common polyatomic ions:

Hydroxide: OH-
Nitrate: NO3-
Sulphate: SO4^2-
Carbonate: CO3^2-
Ammonium: NH4+
Phosphate: PO4^3-

Examples:

NaNO3
Mg(NO3)2
Al2(SO4)3
(NH4)2SO4
CaCO3

Do not confuse a chemical formula with a chemical
equation. A formula represents a substance; an equation
represents a chemical reaction.

TOPIC 3.4: CHEMICAL EQUATION
------------------------------------------------------------

3.4.1 MEANING OF A CHEMICAL EQUATION

A chemical equation is a symbolic representation of
a chemical reaction using chemical formulae and symbols.

A chemical equation shows:

- Reactants
- Products
- Relative amounts of substances
- Physical states when included
- Conditions of reaction when indicated

Reactants are the substances that undergo a chemical
reaction.

Products are the new substances formed.

Example:

2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO

Reactants:
Magnesium and oxygen.

Product:
Magnesium oxide.

3.4.2 WORD EQUATIONS

A word equation represents a chemical reaction using
the names of substances.

Example:

Magnesium + Oxygen -> Magnesium oxide

A word equation can be converted into a chemical equation
by writing the correct chemical formulae and balancing
the equation.

3.4.3 WRITING CHEMICAL FORMULAE IN EQUATIONS

The correct chemical formula must be used before
balancing an equation.

Examples:

Hydrogen = H2
Oxygen = O2
Nitrogen = N2
Chlorine = Cl2
Water = H2O
Carbon dioxide = CO2
Magnesium oxide = MgO
Sodium chloride = NaCl

Some elements exist naturally as diatomic molecules.

Common diatomic elements:

H2
N2
O2
F2
Cl2
Br2
I2

Do not automatically write oxygen as O in an equation
when elemental oxygen gas is intended. Its correct
molecular formula is O2.

3.4.4 BALANCING CHEMICAL EQUATIONS

The law of conservation of mass states that matter
is not created or destroyed during an ordinary
chemical reaction.

Therefore, the number of atoms of each element must
be the same on both sides of a balanced equation.

Coefficients are numbers placed in front of chemical
formulae.

They are used to balance the equation.

IMPORTANT:
Never change subscripts to balance a chemical equation.
Changing a subscript changes the identity of the substance.

EXAMPLE 1:

Unbalanced:

Mg + O2 -> MgO

Count atoms:

Left:
Mg = 1
O = 2

Right:
Mg = 1
O = 1

Put coefficient 2 in front of MgO:

Mg + O2 -> 2MgO

Now right:
Mg = 2
O = 2

Put coefficient 2 in front of Mg:

2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO

Final balanced equation:

2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO

Check:
Left Mg = 2, O = 2
Right Mg = 2, O = 2

EXAMPLE 2: Formation of water.

Unbalanced:

H2 + O2 -> H2O

Balance oxygen by putting 2 in front of water:

H2 + O2 -> 2H2O

Right:
H = 4
O = 2

Balance hydrogen by putting 2 in front of H2:

2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O

Check:
Left H = 4, O = 2
Right H = 4, O = 2

EXAMPLE 3: Formation of ammonia.

Unbalanced:

N2 + H2 -> NH3

Balance nitrogen:

N2 + H2 -> 2NH3

Right hydrogen = 6.

Balance hydrogen:

N2 + 3H2 -> 2NH3

Check:
Left N = 2, H = 6
Right N = 2, H = 6

EXAMPLE 4: Decomposition of copper(II) carbonate.

Word equation:

Copper(II) carbonate -> Copper(II) oxide + Carbon dioxide

Formula equation:

CuCO3 -> CuO + CO2

The equation is already balanced.

EXAMPLE 5: Formation of ammonium chloride.

Word equation:

Ammonia + Hydrogen chloride -> Ammonium chloride

Formula equation:

NH3 + HCl -> NH4Cl

The equation is already balanced.

EXAMPLE 6: Formation of lead(II) iodide.

Word equation:

Lead(II) nitrate + Potassium iodide ->
Lead(II) iodide + Potassium nitrate

Formula equation:

Pb(NO3)2 + KI -> PbI2 + KNO3

Balanced equation:

Pb(NO3)2 + 2KI -> PbI2 + 2KNO3

Check:
Pb = 1 on both sides.
K = 2 on both sides.
I = 2 on both sides.
N = 2 on both sides.
O = 6 on both sides.

3.4.5 PHYSICAL STATE SYMBOLS

Physical state symbols indicate the physical state
of each substance in a chemical equation.

(s) = solid
(l) = liquid
(g) = gas
(aq) = aqueous solution

Examples:

Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) ->
MgCl2(aq) + H2(g)

The symbol (aq) means that the substance is dissolved
in water to form an aqueous solution.

The symbol (g) indicates a gas.

The symbol (s) indicates a solid.

The symbol (l) indicates a liquid.

3.4.6 QUALITATIVE INTERPRETATION OF EQUATIONS

Qualitative interpretation explains the substances
involved in a chemical reaction.

Example:

2Mg(s) + O2(g) -> 2MgO(s)

Qualitative interpretation:

Solid magnesium reacts with oxygen gas to form
solid magnesium oxide.

3.4.7 QUANTITATIVE INTERPRETATION OF EQUATIONS

Quantitative interpretation explains the numerical
relationship between the substances in a balanced
chemical equation.

Example:

2Mg(s) + O2(g) -> 2MgO(s)

The coefficient ratio is:

Mg : O2 : MgO = 2 : 1 : 2

This means:

2 mol of magnesium reacts with 1 mol of oxygen
to produce 2 mol of magnesium oxide.

It also represents the ratio of particles:

2 magnesium atoms react with 1 oxygen molecule
to form 2 formula units of magnesium oxide.

IMPORTANT:
The coefficients show mole ratios, not mass ratios.

For example:

2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO

The mole ratio is 2 : 1 : 2.

It does not mean that the masses are 2 g : 1 g : 2 g.

The mass of each substance depends on its molar mass.

SECTION 3: STOICHIOMETRY AND THE FOUR-STAGE
CALCULATION METHOD
============================================================

3.4.8 MEANING OF STOICHIOMETRY

Stoichiometry is the calculation of the quantitative
relationships between reactants and products in a
balanced chemical equation.

It allows chemists to calculate:

- Mass of reactants required.
- Mass of products formed.
- Number of moles of reactants.
- Number of moles of products.
- Volume of gases involved.
- Amount of substances consumed or produced.

THE MOST IMPORTANT CALCULATION RULE FOR THIS AI:

Whenever solving a reaction stoichiometry question,
the AI MUST use the following four stages in this
exact order.

STAGE 1: WRITE THE BALANCED CHEMICAL EQUATION.

STAGE 2: FIND THE NUMBER OF MOLES OF THE KNOWN
SUBSTANCE.

STAGE 3: APPLY THE MOLE RATIO FROM THE BALANCED
CHEMICAL EQUATION.

STAGE 4: CALCULATE THE FINAL ANSWER IN THE
REQUIRED UNIT.

Never skip a stage.
Never reverse the order.
Never use an unbalanced equation to obtain a mole ratio.

------------------------------------------------------------
THE FOUR-STAGE METHOD IN DETAIL
------------------------------------------------------------

STAGE 1: WRITE THE BALANCED CHEMICAL EQUATION

Identify the reactants and products.

Write their correct chemical formulae.

Balance the equation.

Check that the number of atoms of every element
is equal on both sides.

Only after balancing can the coefficients be used
as a mole ratio.

STAGE 2: FIND THE NUMBER OF MOLES

Identify the substance whose mass, volume or particle
quantity is supplied.

Calculate its number of moles using the appropriate
formula.

If mass is given:

n = m / M

If number of particles is given:

n = N / NA

If gas volume at STP is given:

n = V / 22.4

If gas volume at room conditions is given:

n = V / 24

The AI must show the formula, substitution and answer.

STAGE 3: APPLY THE MOLE RATIO

Take the coefficients from the balanced equation.

General relationship:

Moles of required substance =
Moles of known substance x
(coefficient of required substance /
coefficient of known substance)

The coefficient ratio must be taken from the balanced
equation, not from the chemical formula subscripts.

STAGE 4: CALCULATE THE FINAL ANSWER

Convert the moles of the required substance into the
quantity requested.

If mass is required:

m = n x M

If gas volume is required at STP:

V = n x 22.4

If gas volume is required at room conditions:

V = n x 24

If number of particles is required:

N = n x NA

Write the final answer with the correct unit.

QUESTION:

Magnesium reacts with oxygen to form magnesium oxide.

Calculate the mass of magnesium oxide produced when
12 g of magnesium reacts completely with oxygen.

Given:
Ar(Mg) = 24
Ar(O) = 16

STAGE 1: WRITE THE BALANCED CHEMICAL EQUATION

Chemical equation:

STAGE 3: APPLY THE MOLE RATIO

From the balanced equation:

2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO

Mg : MgO = 2 : 2

Therefore:

n(MgO) = 0.5 x (2 / 2)

n(MgO) = 0.5 mol

STAGE 4: CALCULATE THE FINAL ANSWER

Find the molar mass of magnesium oxide.

M(MgO) = 24 + 16

M(MgO) = 40 g mol^-1

Formula:

m = n x M

Substitute:

m(MgO) = 0.5 x 40

m(MgO) = 20 g

FINAL ANSWER:

20 g of magnesium oxide is produced.

SECTION 4: IMPORTANT COMPARISONS AND RELATIONSHIPS
============================================================

4.1 Ar VERSUS Mr

Ar:
- Relative atomic mass.
- Refers to an atom.
- Based on the carbon-12 scale.
- Has no unit.

Mr:
- Relative molecular mass.
- Refers to a molecule.
- Calculated by adding Ar values.
- Has no unit.

4.2 Mr VERSUS MOLAR MASS

Mr:
- Relative mass of a molecule.
- No unit.
- Example: Mr(H2O) = 18.

Molar mass:
- Mass of one mole of a substance.
- Unit g mol^-1.
- Example: M(H2O) = 18 g mol^-1.

4.3 EMPIRICAL FORMULA VERSUS MOLECULAR FORMULA

Empirical formula:
- Simplest whole-number ratio.
- Does not necessarily show actual atom numbers.

Molecular formula:
- Actual number of each type of atom in one molecule.
- May be a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula.

4.4 MOLE VERSUS NUMBER OF PARTICLES

Mole:
- Amount of substance.
- Unit mol.

Number of particles:
- Total number of specified entities.
- No unit as a count.

4.5 MOLECULAR FORMULA VERSUS CHEMICAL EQUATION

Chemical formula:
- Represents a substance.
- Example: H2O.

Chemical equation:
- Represents a reaction.
- Example: 2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O.

4.6 COEFFICIENT VERSUS SUBSCRIPT

Coefficient:
- Number in front of a chemical formula.
- Changes the number of particles or moles represented.

Subscript:
- Small number within a chemical formula.
- Shows the number of atoms in the formula.

Example:

2H2O

Coefficient = 2
Subscript for H = 2

This represents two water molecules, containing
four hydrogen atoms and two oxygen atoms in total.

4.7 MOLE RATIO VERSUS MASS RATIO

Mole ratio:
- Taken from coefficients in a balanced equation.

Mass ratio:
- Depends on the mole ratio and molar masses.

Never treat the coefficients as masses in grams.

SECTION 6: EXAM TIPS AND COMMON MISTAKES
============================================================

6.1 COMMON MISTAKES IN RELATIVE MASS

Mistake:
Adding the atomic masses without multiplying by
the number of atoms.

Correct approach:
Multiply each Ar by its subscript first.

Mistake:
Ignoring brackets.

Correct approach:
Multiply all atoms inside the bracket by the number
outside the bracket.

Mistake:
Adding units to Mr.

Correct approach:
Mr has no unit.

6.2 COMMON MISTAKES IN MOLE CALCULATIONS

Mistake:
Using Mr instead of molar mass with units.

Correct approach:
Calculate Mr first, then express molar mass in
g mol^-1.

Mistake:
Using N = n / NA.

Correct approach:
N = n x NA.

Mistake:
Confusing atoms and molecules.

Correct approach:
Identify the specified particle.

Mistake:
Using the wrong molar volume.

Correct approach:
Identify STP or room conditions.

6.3 COMMON MISTAKES IN EMPIRICAL FORMULA

Mistake:
Using mass ratios directly as subscripts.

Correct approach:
Convert masses into moles first.

Mistake:
Rounding 1.5 to 2.

Correct approach:
Multiply all ratios by 2 to obtain whole numbers.

Mistake:
Forgetting to divide all mole values by the smallest.

Correct approach:
Divide every value by the smallest mole value.

6.4 COMMON MISTAKES IN MOLECULAR FORMULA

Mistake:
Using Mr directly as a multiplier.

Correct approach:
First calculate empirical formula mass, then calculate:

n = Mr / empirical formula mass.

Mistake:
Multiplying only one subscript.

Correct approach:
Multiply every subscript by n.

6.5 COMMON MISTAKES IN BALANCING EQUATIONS

Mistake:
Changing a subscript to balance the equation.

Correct approach:
Change coefficients only.

Mistake:
Forgetting diatomic elements.

Correct approach:
Use the correct elemental formula such as H2, O2,
N2 and Cl2 where applicable.

Mistake:
Using an unbalanced equation to calculate mole ratios.

Correct approach:
Balance and check the equation first.

6.6 COMMON MISTAKES IN STOICHIOMETRY

MOST IMPORTANT:

The AI MUST use the four stages in this exact order.

1. Write the balanced chemical equation.
2. Find the number of moles.
3. Apply the mole ratio.
4. Calculate the final answer.

Mistake:
Calculating the mass of the product directly from
the mass of the reactant without using the mole ratio.

Correct approach:
Convert the known mass into moles, use the mole ratio,
then convert the required moles into mass.

Mistake:
Using the wrong coefficients.

Correct approach:
Take the coefficients from the balanced equation.

Mistake:
Reversing the mole ratio.

Correct approach:
Place the coefficient of the required substance
in the numerator and the coefficient of the known
substance in the denominator.

Mistake:
Forgetting the final unit.

Correct approach:
Write g for mass, dm^3 or cm^3 for volume, mol
for amount of substance and particles or molecules
for particle counts.

CALCULATION RULES:

For simple mole calculations:
Identify the given information, select the correct
relationship, substitute values and show the answer.

For empirical formula:
Use mass -> moles -> divide by smallest -> whole-number
ratio -> empirical formula.

For molecular formula:
Calculate empirical formula mass, calculate the
multiplier and multiply every subscript.

FOR ALL REACTION STOICHIOMETRY QUESTIONS:

The four stages are compulsory and must appear in
this exact order.

STAGE 1:
Write the correct balanced chemical equation.

STAGE 2:
Calculate the number of moles of the known substance.

STAGE 3:
Apply the mole ratio using the coefficients of the
balanced chemical equation.

STAGE 4:
Calculate the final required quantity and state the
correct unit.

Never skip these stages.

Never calculate the required product mass directly
from the reactant mass without applying the mole ratio.

Never use coefficients from an unbalanced equation.

Never confuse the coefficient ratio with the subscripts
inside a chemical formula.

SYMBOL ACCURACY RULES:

Ar = relative atomic mass.
Mr = relative molecular mass.
M = molar mass.
m = mass.
n = number of moles.
N = number of particles.
NA = Avogadro constant.
V = volume of gas.

Do not interchange these symbols.

Do not add units to Ar or Mr.

Use g mol^-1 for molar mass.

Use mol for amount of substance.

Use dm^3 or cm^3 for volume.

Use the correct molar volume for the specified
conditions.
